PROPOSAL 1
Name / filename: Agent_06_propose_forearm_fracture_zoom_contrast
Question it tries to answer: Is the lucent line crossing the mid-to-distal forearm shaft a true cortical fracture, and if so, is it non-displaced, angulated, or subtly offset?
Why this method may help: The candidate fracture occupies a small fraction of the total frame; at full-image scale, subtle cortical stepping or a hairline lucency is hard to judge. A tight crop around the shaft (including a margin of normal bone above and below for comparison) combined with contrast stretching (histogram stretch/CLAHE) on that crop specifically should make any cortical discontinuity, sclerotic margins, or displacement much easier to see without the rest of the image's brightness range diluting the local contrast.
What it expects to reveal: A clearer view of whether the cortex is truly broken on one or both sides of the bone, whether there is any angulation/step-off, and whether the line has the crisp, sharp-edged character of a fracture versus the smoother, gently curving character of a nutrient canal or vascular groove.
Possible artifacts / misleading effects: Aggressive local contrast stretching can create false "halos" or exaggerate normal trabecular lines into looking like cracks; over-sharpening could make a normal nutrient canal look like a fracture. Cropping removes surrounding anatomic context, so the result must always be viewed side-by-side with the uncropped original.
Comparison with original: Overlay/adjacent side-by-side of the cropped-enhanced region against the same region cropped (but unenhanced) from the original, at matching scale, to confirm the finding is present in the source data and not an artifact of processing.

PROPOSAL 2
Name / filename: Agent_06_propose_fracture_edge_enhancement_unsharp
Question it tries to answer: How sharp/well-defined are the cortical margins at the suspected fracture site, and is any blurring there due to true anatomy, motion, or exposure rather than a genuine fracture?
Why this method may help: Unsharp masking / high-pass edge enhancement accentuates boundaries between bone cortex and surrounding tissue, which can help distinguish a genuine, crisp fracture line from a soft, blurred discontinuity more consistent with motion artifact or overlap.
What it expects to reveal: Whether the questioned cortical break has sharp, well-defined edges (favoring true fracture) or soft, gradual edges (favoring artifact/blur/summation).
Possible artifacts / misleading effects: Unsharp masking can introduce ringing/halo artifacts along any high-contrast edge (including normal cortical margins elsewhere), potentially creating the appearance of additional "lines" that are not real. Over-enhancement can also make normal soft-tissue folds or skin creases look like linear findings.
Comparison with original: Directly compare the enhanced fracture-zone edges against the unenhanced original at the same crop/zoom; also check whether similar "edge lines" appear elsewhere in clearly normal bone (a sanity check for over-enhancement artifacts).

PROPOSAL 3
Name / filename: Agent_06_propose_hand_wrist_overlap_separation
Question it tries to answer: Does the hand seen at the bottom of the image belong to the same limb (i.e., an extremely flexed wrist), or is it a second/separate hand (e.g., an assisting hand, or the patient's other hand) gripping the injured wrist/forearm for support during the exposure?
Why this method may help: This materially changes interpretation — if it's the imaged limb's own hand, the wrist and hand should be assessed as part of the injury exam; if it's a different hand providing support, its bones are clinically irrelevant to this limb's injury but the overlap still obscures the true wrist. A localized brightness/contrast adjustment and crop of just the wrist-hand junction, possibly combined with mild edge enhancement, may help visually separate the two sets of overlapping bones (radius/ulna/carpals vs. the gripping hand's metacarpals/phalanges) by their different orientations and cortical outlines.
What it expects to reveal: A clearer sense of how many distinct bony structures converge at that junction, their relative orientations, and whether the true distal radius/ulna and carpal region is visible at all or is fully obscured.
Possible artifacts / misleading effects: Dense bone-on-bone overlap in this region is a genuine superimposition problem that contrast/brightness adjustment alone cannot fully resolve — it cannot separate structures that are truly superimposed in a single 2D projection; false clarity/pseudo-structures could be suggested by processing.
Comparison with original: Side-by-side crop comparison, and cross-checking the apparent finger count and joint orientations against the original to see if they are consistent (i.e., processing should not "invent" structures not present in the source).

PROPOSAL 4
Name / filename: Agent_06_propose_softtissue_window_periarticular
Question it tries to answer: Is there soft-tissue swelling or asymmetry around the suspected fracture site or around the elbow that would support (or argue against) an acute injury?
Why this method may help: Standard bone-optimized display can wash out subtle soft-tissue density differences. A separate windowing/leveling pass tuned to the lower (soft-tissue) end of the intensity histogram, focused on the forearm and elbow, may reveal soft-tissue fullness/swelling adjacent to the fracture site or a displaced fat pad sign at the elbow that is not apparent in the current bone-weighted display.
What it expects to reveal: Presence/absence of localized soft-tissue swelling near the suspected fracture, and any elbow joint effusion sign (e.g., posterior fat pad), which would support the fracture being acute/real rather than incidental.
Possible artifacts / misleading effects: Soft-tissue windowing on a radiograph acquired/exported with bone-optimized processing has limited dynamic range to work with and may just reveal compression/quantization banding rather than true anatomic detail; perceived "swelling" could also reflect normal muscle bulk or the angle of flexion rather than pathology.
Comparison with original: Compare soft-tissue contours and any presumed swelling against the contralateral portion of the same forearm (proximal vs. distal) and against the original image's grayscale at the same location to ensure the finding isn't purely a windowing artifact.

PROPOSAL 5
Name / filename: Agent_06_propose_inverted_grayscale_fullimage
Question it tries to answer: Does inverting the grayscale (black-white polarity reversal) make the suspected fracture line, or any other subtle linear finding, more or less conspicuous than in the standard display?
Why this method may help: Radiologists sometimes use inverted display for subtle fracture lines and foreign bodies because the human eye can detect certain thin lucent/dense lines more readily against reversed polarity. This is a low-cost, whole-image sanity check that requires no assumptions about where else to look.
What it expects to reveal: Either increased confidence that the fracture line is a real, continuous linear structure (visible and consistent in both polarities), or a hint that it was a low-contrast artifact more prominent in one polarity than the other.
Possible artifacts / misleading effects: Inversion itself does not add information and can trick perception — a line that seems more "obvious" after inversion is not necessarily more real; this should be treated as a perceptual aid only, not independent evidence.
Comparison with original: View inverted and original side-by-side at the same crop of the fracture zone and the hand-wrist junction; confirm any newly noticed features are traceable back to the same pixel location in the original.
