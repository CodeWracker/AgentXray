PROPOSAL 1: Region-of-interest crop + histogram/intensity profile
-------------------------------------------------------------------
Question it answers: Is the bright proximal (humerus/joint) region
genuinely losing information to sensor/exposure clipping, or is it just
visually compressed and still recoverable?

Why this method: A pixel-intensity histogram of the full image, and
separately of just the proximal ROI, directly measures how much of the
dynamic range is piled up at or near the maximum value. This is a cheap,
objective way to test my qualitative impression of "the top-right looks
overexposed" instead of relying on visual impression alone.

Expected information: Either (a) a histogram with a hard spike at the
max value, confirming true clipping and an upper bound on what
enhancement can recover, or (b) a smoother distribution, indicating the
area only looks bright relative to the rest of the image and that
contrast stretching should reveal real structure.

Possible artifacts/misleading effects: PNG export or prior
compression/processing could itself have introduced artificial clipping
or posterization not present in the original acquisition, so a clipped
histogram here doesn't necessarily mean the original exam was
overexposed — only that this particular file is limited.

Comparison to original: Overlay/report the histogram statistics next to
the original image and note which specific anatomical sub-regions fall
into the clipped vs. non-clipped intensity band, so any downstream
enhancement claims can be traced back to whether they're recovering real
signal or just remapping noise.

PROPOSAL 2: Local (adaptive) contrast enhancement of the elbow joint
------------------------------------------------------------------------
Question it answers: Are the fat-pad soft-tissue planes and the
overlapping bony margins (coronoid process, radial head, olecranon tip)
actually normal, or is their apparent indistinctness in the original
simply due to insufficient local contrast in that specific region?

Why this method: Unlike a single global brightness/contrast adjustment
(which would just shift the clipping problem elsewhere), a
locally-adaptive method (e.g., adaptive histogram equalization applied
only within the joint ROI) redistributes contrast within that small
region independent of the very different exposure levels found
elsewhere in the frame. This directly targets the exact limitation
identified in Section A (Q6) rather than applying a generic global
filter.

Expected information: Better visual separation between soft tissue fat
planes and muscle around the distal humerus, and sharper delineation of
the coronoid/radial head/olecranon overlap, letting me confirm or revise
the "no obvious fat pad sign" impression with more confidence.

Possible artifacts/misleading effects: Adaptive contrast methods can
amplify existing noise/grain into false textures that look like subtle
lucencies or trabecular irregularities, and can create halo artifacts at
sharp bone/soft-tissue boundaries — any "new" finding seen only after
this processing and not corroborated in the original must be treated
with suspicion rather than reported as a real finding.

Comparison to original: Present as a side-by-side (original vs.
enhanced) crop at matched scale/orientation, and explicitly flag any
feature visible only in the enhanced version as "enhancement-dependent,
verify against raw image" rather than as a new independent finding.

PROPOSAL 3: Edge-emphasis pass (e.g., unsharp mask or gradient/Laplacian
overlay) along the full bone shafts
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
Question it answers: Are the cortical margins of the humerus, radius,
and ulna continuous end-to-end, or is there a subtle step-off,
angulation, or lucent line that is present but too low-contrast to
register clearly against the bright background at full-frame viewing?

Why this method: Cortical fracture lines are fundamentally edge/gradient
discontinuities. An edge-emphasizing transform makes the continuous
cortex render as a continuous bright/dark boundary line, so any break in
that line becomes much easier to trace visually across the whole shaft
in one pass, rather than relying on scanning the plain grayscale image
inch by inch.

Expected information: Confirmation (or refutation) that each cortex
forms one unbroken line from the visible proximal end to the visible
distal end, for all three long bones in frame.

Possible artifacts/misleading effects: Edge filters can produce
spurious "breaks" wherever the original image has real anatomic overlap
(e.g., where radius crosses in front of ulna, or where soft-tissue
folds cross the bone silhouette), and can also amplify film-grain/
compression-noise into fake fine lines. Any suspected discontinuity
found this way needs to be checked against the unprocessed image at the
same location before being called a possible fracture.

Comparison to original: Register the edge-map directly over the
original grayscale image (as an overlay or paired panel at identical
crop/scale) so any candidate discontinuity can be pointed to at an exact
pixel location and checked against the raw data, not just described in
the abstract.
