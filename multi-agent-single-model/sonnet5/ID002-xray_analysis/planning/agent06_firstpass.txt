PROPOSAL 1: Localized soft-tissue contrast enhancement around the elbow
joint (fat-pad check)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Question it answers: Are the anterior and/or posterior fat pads at the elbow
joint elevated/displaced, suggesting a joint effusion (and therefore a
possible occult fracture not otherwise visible on this view)?

Why this method may help: The current image compresses a very wide density
range (empty black background through overexposed pale soft tissue to
bright bone cortex) into one contrast scale. The fat pads are a small,
low-contrast structure sitting in exactly the "washed out" mid-tone soft
tissue region I flagged in Section A.4. Restricting contrast stretching (or
a local adaptive method such as CLAHE) to just the soft-tissue intensity
band immediately around the joint, rather than adjusting the whole image,
should increase the visibility of that specific structure without further
saturating the bone.

What I expect it to reveal: Either a normal, flat/inconspicuous fat pad
pattern (supporting the "unremarkable" impression from Section A.3), or a
sail-shaped anterior fat pad / any visible posterior fat pad (which would
be a meaningful finding worth flagging even without a visible fracture
line).

Possible artifacts/misleading effects: Local contrast enhancement can
create the appearance of a soft-tissue "edge" purely from noise or from
skin-fold/muscle-boundary contours that are not actually fat pads; over-
enhancement could also manufacture a false impression of swelling. Any
positive finding here needs to be checked against normal anatomic
expectations for shape/location, not just presence of a visible line.

How to compare with the original: Side-by-side or overlay of the enhanced
joint-region crop against the same crop from the original image, at matching
zoom/scale, to confirm any apparent fat-pad sign is present (even if subtle)
in the original data and not purely an artifact of the enhancement.

PROPOSAL 2: Cortical edge enhancement along the radius/ulna and around the
olecranon/coronoid/radial neck
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Question it answers: Is there a subtle, non-displaced cortical break (hairline
fracture) anywhere along the visible bone margins that is not apparent on
casual viewing, particularly at the classic elbow fracture sites (radial
neck, olecranon, coronoid tip) and along the forearm shafts?

Why this method may help: A lateral elbow view has substantial anatomic
overlap (radial head over coronoid, etc.), and thin fracture lines can be
low-contrast relative to normal cortex, especially if partly obscured by
superimposition. Edge-detection / unsharp-masking specifically along bone
margins increases local gradient visibility, which is well suited to
distinguishing a true cortical discontinuity from the surrounding smooth
cortex, better than looking at the raw grayscale image alone.

What I expect it to reveal: Most likely, continuous, unbroken cortical
outlines confirming the "no obvious fracture" impression from Section A.3 —
but with more confidence. If not, it should highlight a specific candidate
location for a discontinuity that a human reader should re-examine directly.

Possible artifacts/misleading effects: Edge enhancement will also emphasize
normal anatomic lines that are NOT fractures — the radial tuberosity
margin, nutrient foramina, or the overlap boundary between radius and ulna
in the lateral projection can look like a "break" after aggressive edge
filtering. Any candidate line found this way must be checked for whether it
corresponds to a known normal anatomic landmark before being treated as a
possible fracture.

How to compare with the original: Overlay the edge map (or a blended
original+edge composite) on the unmodified grayscale image at the same
scale, and cross-check any candidate line's location against normal
anatomic landmarks (nutrient foramen locations, radial tuberosity, physis
remnants) before flagging it as suspicious.

PROPOSAL 3: Alignment/geometry check using reference lines (radiocapitellar
line and anterior humeral line)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Question it answers: Is the radial head properly aligned with the
capitellum, and does the anterior humeral cortex pass through the middle
third of the capitellum, as expected in a normal elbow? Malalignment on
either line is a recognized indirect indicator of subtle fracture or subluxation
that can be missed by looking at bone density/edges alone.

Why this method may help: This is specifically a LATERAL elbow view, which
is the correct projection for applying these two classic alignment
constructs. Unlike Proposals 1–2 (which look for direct signs), this checks
alignment/geometry, a different and complementary axis of information that
only this projection supports well.

What I expect it to reveal: Confirmation that the radiocapitellar line and
anterior humeral line both intersect the capitellum normally (supporting a
grossly normal joint), or a deviation that would suggest subluxation or an
occult fracture affecting alignment (e.g., radial head or supracondylar
injury) despite no visible fracture line.

Possible artifacts/misleading effects: These lines are angle- and
positioning-sensitive; if the elbow is not in a true lateral position (some
rotation is plausible here given the oblique framing of the limb in the
image), the lines can appear "abnormal" purely due to projection, not true
pathology. Any apparent malalignment must be interpreted with that
positioning caveat rather than as a definitive finding.

How to compare with the original: Draw both reference lines directly on a
copy of the original image (not a processed/filtered version) so the
anatomic landmarks used to construct them are the true, unaltered pixel
data — the only "processing" here is annotation/measurement, and it should
be presented alongside the plain original for direct visual verification by
a human reviewer.
