--- Proposal 1: Joint-region contrast redistribution (ROI-limited adaptive
    contrast stretch / local histogram equalization) ---
Question it targets: Is there a subtle soft-tissue effusion (abnormal
fat-pad displacement) or low-contrast finding around the elbow joint that
is being visually "flattened" by the image's overall dynamic range?
Why it may help: Roughly half this frame is featureless black background
and another large portion is very bright bone; if global contrast/brightness
is anchored to that full range, the mid-gray soft-tissue detail immediately
around the joint (where fat pads live) may be compressed into a narrow,
hard-to-distinguish band. Redistributing contrast within a crop limited to
just the joint and immediate peri-articular soft tissue should expand that
narrow band and make subtle fat-pad displacement or effusion easier to
judge.
Expected information: Either a clearer, more confident visual read that the
fat pads are normal (supporting my current unremarkable impression), or
revelation of a posterior fat-pad sign / bulging anterior fat pad that
would raise suspicion for an occult fracture not otherwise visible.
Possible artifacts/pitfalls: Local contrast enhancement can create false
"edges" or exaggerate normal soft-tissue planes (e.g., overlying skin
folds, muscle fascia) into something that looks pathological; halo/ringing
artifacts near the very bright cortex are also possible and must not be
mistaken for a true fat-pad line.
Comparison to original: Overlay/toggle the enhanced ROI crop against the
same crop from the original at identical framing/zoom, side by side, and
explicitly ask whether any suspected fat-pad line is present in the
original (even faintly) at the same location — an enhancement finding with
zero counterpart in the original should be treated skeptically.

--- Proposal 2: Cortical-margin edge enhancement (unsharp mask / high-pass
    filter) along the visible bone contours ---
Question it targets: Is there a subtle, non-displaced cortical break
(hairline fracture) anywhere along the humeral condyle, olecranon/coronoid,
radial head/neck, or the two forearm shafts that isn't obvious at normal
viewing contrast?
Why it may help: Hairline fractures often manifest only as a very faint
lucent line crossing an otherwise smooth cortex — a difference of only a
few gray levels. Edge/high-pass enhancement is specifically good at
amplifying exactly this kind of small, local intensity discontinuity
without needing to touch the broader tonal range of the image.
Expected information: Either continued reassurance that all traced cortical
margins remain smooth and unbroken (strengthening the "grossly unremarkable"
read), or localization of a candidate break line worth flagging for closer
review, particularly at the radial neck and coronoid, which are the classic
sites for subtle elbow fractures in a lateral view.
Possible artifacts/pitfalls: Overlapping bone edges (e.g., where the radius
and ulna cross visually, or where the humeral condyle overlaps the radial
head) can produce enhancement lines that look like fracture lines but are
just superimposition of two separate normal structures — this is the main
risk and needs to be checked against expected anatomy at that location.
Sharpening can also amplify PNG compression block edges as false lines.
Comparison to original: For any candidate line found, check whether it is
also faintly present, in the same location and orientation, on the
unprocessed original at high zoom; a "fracture" that only exists after
aggressive sharpening and has zero trace in the source pixels is more
likely an artifact than a real finding.

--- Proposal 3: Inverted-polarity rendering (negative image) as an
    independent visual cross-check ---
Question it targets: Does my subjective visual impression (of intact
cortices, preserved joint space, and no obvious lesion) hold up under a
different, independent visual presentation of the identical pixel data?
Why it may help: Radiographic interpretation is known to be somewhat
polarity-dependent — some subtle lucent lines or density changes are
easier for a human (or automated) reviewer to notice when displayed with
inverted brightness, essentially because it forces a "fresh," pattern-
disrupting look rather than relying on the same learned visual habits used
on the standard view. It's also essentially free (a lossless, reversible
transform), so the cost/benefit is favorable given the residual
uncertainty flagged in Section A.
Expected information: Confirmation of the same impression from a second
independent visual angle, increasing confidence in the "no obvious gross
abnormality" read; or, less likely, a previously unnoticed density change
that stands out only in inverted polarity.
Possible artifacts/pitfalls: Inversion itself introduces no new data and
cannot manufacture true pathology, but it can make certain normal
structures (e.g., nutrient canals, overlapping soft-tissue folds) look
unfamiliar and be misread as new findings simply because they look
different from a standard radiograph.
Comparison to original: Any "new" observation from the inverted view
must be relocated and re-confirmed on the original (non-inverted) image at
the same coordinates before being taken seriously — it should be used only
as a prompt for where to look again, not as standalone evidence.

Question it targets: Is there any focal cortical thinning, asymmetry

Why it may help: Extracting numeric pixel-intensity profiles perpendicular

Expected information: A profile that is broadly consistent from level to

Possible artifacts/pitfalls: Overlying soft tissue, the angle of the bone

Comparison to original: Mark the exact sampling line locations back onto

outlier, to judge whether the numeric anomaly corresponds to something
