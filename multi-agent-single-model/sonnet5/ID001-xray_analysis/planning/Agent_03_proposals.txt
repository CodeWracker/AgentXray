STEP C — INDEPENDENT PROCESSING PROPOSALS

PROPOSAL 1: Localized crop + zoom of the ankle mortise and hindfoot
(tibiotalar joint, talus, calcaneus)
- Question it answers: Is there a true cortical discontinuity or lucent line at the
  distal tibia/fibula/talus overlap, or is the irregular appearance simply due to normal
  superimposition at the current small viewing scale?
- Why it may help: The ankle-mortise region is the most anatomically complex and most
  overlapped part of this lateral view. Simply enlarging that sub-region (nearest-neighbor
  or bicubic upscaling of a crop) lets fine cortical margins and joint-space width be
  judged without the surrounding anatomy competing for attention.
- Expected information: Clearer delineation of whether the tibial and fibular cortices are
  continuous, whether the joint space is uniform, and whether the talar dome contour is
  smooth.
- Possible artifacts/misleading effects: Upscaling can introduce blocky or blurred pixel
  artifacts that could be mistaken for cortical irregularity; interpolation smoothing can
  also hide a genuinely thin fracture line. Any finding here must be cross-checked against
  the non-interpolated original.
- Comparison method: View the crop side-by-side with the same region at native resolution
  in the original image; confirm any suspected line or step-off is present in both.

PROPOSAL 2: Local contrast enhancement (e.g., CLAHE / adaptive histogram equalization)
restricted to the bony anatomy
- Question it answers: Are there subtle trabecular density changes in the calcaneus/talus
  (e.g., localized lucency or sclerosis) that are being masked by the compressed mid-tone
  contrast noted in the original?
- Why it may help: Adaptive local contrast enhancement redistributes gray levels within
  small neighborhoods, which can reveal subtle density variations in trabecular bone that
  are hard to see when contrast is compressed, without over-amplifying contrast in
  unrelated regions the way a global stretch would.
- Expected information: Better visual separation between normal trabecular pattern and any
  focal lucent/sclerotic area, if present.
- Possible artifacts/misleading effects: Local equalization can amplify noise and create
  false "texture" or pseudo-lesions in areas that were actually just noisy background;
  it can also introduce halo/blotchy artifacts near high-contrast edges (e.g., bone-soft
  tissue interface). Any apparent lesion must be treated as a hypothesis to verify, not a
  finding, until checked against the unenhanced image.
- Comparison method: Toggle/overlay between the enhanced and original image at the same
  crop and zoom; a genuine finding should correspond to a real, non-amplified signal in the
  original, not only appear after enhancement.

PROPOSAL 3: Edge enhancement (unsharp masking or a Sobel/Laplacian-based edge map) of the
tibia/fibula/talus cortical margins
- Question it answers: Are the cortical outlines of the distal tibia, fibula, and talus
  continuous and smooth, or is there a discrete break/step suggestive of a fracture line
  that is difficult to trace by eye through the overlapping bones?
- Why it may help: Edge-detection/sharpening specifically highlights boundaries between
  bone and adjacent tissue or between cortex and medullary cavity, which is exactly the
  kind of discontinuity a fracture would produce, making a break easier to trace visually
  through a region of bone-on-bone overlap.
- Expected information: A cleaner line-map of cortical margins that can be visually traced
  for continuity around the mortise and hindfoot.
- Possible artifacts/misleading effects: Edge filters respond strongly to noise and to
  the true anatomic overlap lines between tibia/fibula/talus (which are normal
  superimposition, not pathology); this can create edge segments that look like fracture
  lines but are not. Over-sharpening can also produce ringing artifacts along high-contrast
  boundaries.
- Comparison method: Overlay or place the edge map adjacent to the grayscale original at
  the same crop; only trust an edge segment as clinically relevant if it corresponds to an
  actual density change in the original grayscale image, not merely a projection overlap
  line.

PROPOSAL 4: Noise characterization of the unexposed background vs. the exposed anatomy
(e.g., compare local standard deviation/texture in the black border region to texture
within the bone/soft tissue)
- Question it answers: How much of the mottled/grainy appearance noted in the bone and
  soft tissue is attributable to acquisition noise (quantum mottle) rather than true
  anatomic texture, so that texture-based observations above can be weighted appropriately?
- Why it may help: The black collimated border contains no anatomy, so its noise texture is
  a direct measurement of the acquisition's baseline noise level. Comparing that baseline
  to the texture seen in the calcaneus/talus gives a rough sense of whether the mottled
  trabecular appearance exceeds what noise alone would produce.
- Expected information: A qualitative (or simple quantitative, e.g., local variance) sense
  of baseline image noise, to calibrate confidence in any texture-based observation from
  Proposals 1-2.
- Possible artifacts/misleading effects: Background noise characteristics (in an
  unexposed/collimated region) are not guaranteed to be identical to noise behavior in a
  region that received X-ray exposure, so this comparison is only an approximation, not a
  precise noise model.
- Comparison method: Report the relative noise magnitude alongside the enhanced images from
  Proposals 1-2 so any "texture" finding there is explicitly weighed against this baseline
  rather than interpreted in isolation.

Note: All four proposals are aimed at resolving specific ambiguities described in the
Step B first-pass review (overlapping cortices at the ankle mortise, compressed mid-tone
contrast, mottled trabecular/background texture) rather than being a generic checklist. No
proposal is intended to produce a diagnosis on its own; each is meant to be interpreted
together with, and validated against, the original unprocessed image.
