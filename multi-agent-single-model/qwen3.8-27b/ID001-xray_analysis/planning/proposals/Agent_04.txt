PROPOSAL 1: Adaptive local contrast enhancement (CLAHE)
QUESTION: Does the global contrast fade (especially the upper leg) mask bony or
soft-tissue findings, and would local contrast normalization reveal structure that
is present but under-displayed?
METHOD: Apply contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) with a
moderate clip limit and tile size to the full grayscale image; also produce a
milder variant. Display-level transform only - no deblurring, no interpolation
beyond what the algorithm requires.
WHY IT MAY HELP: The fade I documented is a non-uniform intensity offset across the
image; CLAHE equalizes local histograms, brightening underexposed regions (upper
leg, plantar soft tissue) without clipping the already bright bone. It directly
targets the dominant documented quality limitation.
EXPECTED REVELATION: Sharper cortical margins of the distal tibia/fibula, better
separation of soft-tissue planes (dorsum, plantar, heel fat pad), and increased
conspicuity of low-contrast structures (tibiotalar joint space, faint lucencies).
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: Amplification of background grain and quantization noise into
visible texture; halos/ringing around high-contrast edges; emphasis of the cassette
edge, scratches, or film specks; noise clumps may mimic small lucencies or a
soft-tissue mass.
COMPARISON TO ORIGINAL: Side-by-side comparison at matched geometry. Any "new"
structure must be traceable to a faint counterpart at 1:1 in the original; a finding
that exists only in the enhanced image and has no counterpart in the original is a
noise artifact, not anatomy. Also verify that no anatomy visible in the original
has been erased or inverted.

PROPOSAL 2: Targeted zoom + narrow windowing of the midfoot/metatarsal ROI
QUESTION: Are there subtle non-displaced fracture lines or cortical step-offs in
the navicular, cuneiforms, cuboid, or metatarsal shafts/heads? And exactly what is
cut off at the right border?
METHOD: Extract the ROI spanning the midfoot through the most distal visible
forefoot (roughly 55-85% of image height, 45-100% of width), magnify 2-4x, and
apply a narrow linear contrast window set to the bone intensity range. Separately,
inspect the right-border zone at 1:1 to identify the most distal bone visible
before the crop.
WHY IT MAY HELP: A global stretch optimizes for the whole image (mostly background)
and wastes dynamic range; a narrow window on the bone range maximizes intra-osseous
contrast precisely where the uncertainty is. Magnification makes near-pixel cortical
detail inspectable.
EXPECTED REVELATION: Either confirmation of continuous cortical margins, or a
linear lucency / cortical step-off suggesting a non-displaced fracture; and a
definitive statement of the most distal visible structure at the crop edge
(e.g., metatarsal heads vs proximal phalanges).
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: Interpolation smoothing or pixelation from upscaling;
quantization banding at reduced bit depth can create false step-like contours;
amplified grain may form linear streaks that mimic fracture lines.
COMPARISON TO ORIGINAL: Any candidate line must be verified at 1:1 in the original.
A true fracture persists across multiple independent window settings; a streak that
shifts or disappears with a different window is an artifact. Cross-check against
the CLAHE version (Proposal 1) so the line is not a product of one specific
enhancement.

PROPOSAL 3: Edge enhancement of the hindfoot cortical margins
QUESTION: Are the calcaneal posterior cortex (including the tuberosity) and the
talar dome cortical margin intact, without a subtle break or step?
METHOD: Apply unsharp masking (original minus a Gaussian-blurred copy, added back
with a modest weight) to the hindfoot ROI; as an independent cross-check, compute
a gradient/edge-magnitude map (e.g., Sobel) of the same ROI.
WHY IT MAY HELP: The dominant degradation here is edge blur; unsharp masking
re-emphasizes high-frequency cortical edges, and the gradient map independently
localizes where strong edges lie. The two methods corroborate each other because
they respond to different aspects of the edge signal.
EXPECTED REVELATION: A smooth, continuous cortical contour around the calcaneus
and talus, or a focal edge discontinuity / cortical step suggesting a fracture or
a talar dome osteochondral defect.
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: Ringing/halo (overshoot) on both sides of strong edges -
especially at bone-soft-tissue interfaces - which can mimic a double cortical line
or a fracture; noise amplification within the trabecular region.
COMPARISON TO ORIGINAL: The enhanced edge must sit exactly where the blurred edge
is in the original. Halos appear symmetrically on both sides of a true edge, so a
one-sided "step" lacking an original-image counterpart is suspect. The edge map
should show a single continuous edge path around the calcaneus.

PROPOSAL 4: Soft-tissue contour analysis
QUESTION: Is there soft-tissue swelling (dorsum of foot, plantar, or heel) that is
not clearly delineable on the original?
METHOD: First display the image with a wide window/level that saturates the bone,
so only the soft-tissue envelope is visible. Then fit a smooth reference contour
(low-pass or morphological closing) to the body silhouette and measure local
deviations of the actual contour from the reference.
WHY IT MAY HELP: Swelling is a contour (silhouette) change, not an internal-contrast
change; bone-saturating windowing removes the bright-bone distraction, and
comparing actual contour to a smooth reference turns a vague "looks swollen" into a
measurable deviation.
EXPECTED REVELATION: A focal fullness over the dorsum, midfoot, or heel (e.g.,
plantar fat-pad thickening), or confirmation of a smooth, unremarkable contour.
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: The cassette/film edge or a background brightness gradient can
fake a contour change; skin folds or a superimposed object (e.g., sock remnant)
can mimic a bulge; patient rotation shifts the apparent contour.
COMPARISON TO ORIGINAL: The bulge must correspond to a true silhouette change in
the original, not a brightness change of the background behind an unchanged
contour. Verify by checking whether the brightness difference lies inside the body
silhouette or outside it.

PROPOSAL 5: Quantitative alignment and joint-space assessment
QUESTION: Is tibiotalar congruence preserved, and can the apparent neutral
hindfoot position be confirmed objectively with standard lateral-view angles?
METHOD: On a contrast-enhanced version of the image, trace the tibial plafond and
talar dome and measure the tibiotalar distance; measure the standard hindfoot
angles (calcaneal pitch, tibiocalcaneal, talocalcaneal) using their defined lines
on the lateral view. Report values with an uncertainty band, given blurred edges.
WHY IT MAY HELP: My first pass called alignment "approximately preserved" on gross
impression; quantitative angles provide objective documentation and can separate a
true neutral position from a mild cavus/planus that is hard to see by eye.
EXPECTED REVELATION: Joint-space and angle values within expected ranges (roughly
calcaneal pitch ~20-30 degrees, tibiocalcaneal ~30-45 degrees on a neutral
lateral), supporting the neutral-position impression, or a deviation suggesting
malalignment or subluxation.
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: Subpixel tracing ambiguity from edge blur; overlap of
structures (fibula, talar head) can be misidentified as the plafond or dome edge;
a slightly rotated (non-true) lateral systematically biases the angle values.
COMPARISON TO ORIGINAL: Every traced line must be visibly anchored to a structure
present in the original. Where two candidate edges exist (blurring), report a range
rather than a single value. The quantitative result must be directionally
consistent with the qualitative first-pass impression.

PROPOSAL 6: Noise-floor and detection-limit estimation
QUESTION: Given the grain and blur, what is the smallest visible defect in this
image - i.e., could a faint fracture line be resolved here at all?
METHOD: Measure background noise (standard deviation / local texture) in
anatomy-free regions (dark margins, upper-leg soft tissue), and combine it with an
edge-spread measurement (width of a known sharp cortical edge, e.g., the heel
cortex) to estimate the contrast-size detection limit for a linear defect.
WHY IT MAY HELP: The core uncertainty from my first pass is statistical, not
anatomical: "subtle fracture cannot be excluded" because noise/blur limits
detectability. Quantifying that limit converts "cannot exclude" into "cannot
exclude a defect with contrast below X or length below Y".
EXPECTED REVELATION: A numeric detection limit (e.g., a linear lucency must be at
least N pixels long and M% contrast to be reliably visible), which calibrates how
much weight the midfoot/metatarsal inspection (Proposal 2) can carry.
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: Non-uniform fade means a single background region is not
representative; quantization noise is not Gaussian; bone-region texture differs
from background, so a single global estimate is imperfect.
COMPARISON TO ORIGINAL: The estimate must be consistent with the visual graininess
on the original (sanity check). Perform the measurement on the original image, not
on an enhanced version, because enhancement alters the noise statistics.

PROPOSAL 7: PSF-based deconvolution (exploratory - highest artifact risk)
QUESTION: Is part of the cortical blur recoverable - i.e., is the image degraded
by an estimable blur (PSF), and could deblurring restore fine cortical detail that
simple sharpening cannot?
METHOD: Estimate the point spread function from the sharpest available edges (e.g.,
the heel cortical edge or talar dome), then apply a regularized deconvolution
(e.g., Wiener) to the bony ROIs, testing several regularization strengths.
WHY IT MAY HELP: Unsharp masking (Proposal 3) amplifies existing contrast, noise
included; deconvolution attempts to reverse the blur itself, which is the actual
documented degradation, and can therefore recover detail that is genuinely present
but smeared.
EXPECTED REVELATION: Sharper cortical definition in the midfoot/metatarsal and
calcaneus - or, equally informative, evidence that edges are already at the noise
floor and blur is not the limiting factor.
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: Highest risk of any proposal: ringing, halos, and spurious
linear structures (deconvolution is notorious for creating phantom streaks that
resemble fracture lines); strong dependence on regularization strength.
COMPARISON TO ORIGINAL: Any "new" line must persist across multiple
regularization levels and agree with edge locations in the original. A line that
appears at only one setting, or that crosses a region of homogeneous cortex in the
original, is rejected as an artifact.
