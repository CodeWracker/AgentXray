PROPOSAL 1: Local contrast enhancement (CLAHE) plus edge enhancement of the hindfoot, focused on talus, calcaneus, and distal tibia/fibula
QUESTION: Does a subtle non-displaced fracture line or cortical discontinuity exist in the talus, calcaneus, or distal tibia/lateral malleolus that is below the visibility threshold of the original image?
METHOD: Apply adaptive local histogram equalization (CLAHE) with a moderate clip limit over the whole image (or over a crop covering the hindfoot), then apply a mild unsharp mask / high-pass (e.g., difference-of-Gaussians) to emphasize fine cortical and trabecular detail. Keep a second variant with stronger edge weighting for comparison. No thresholding or segmentation in this pass — keep the image diagnostically natural.
WHY IT MAY HELP: The main limitation identified in the first pass is rescanned-film softness and moderate resolution, which suppresses low-contrast fine detail (hairline lucent lines, subtle cortical step-offs). CLAHE boosts local contrast without globally saturating the bright cortical margins (as global equalization would), and a high-pass filter specifically amplifies the spatial frequencies at which thin fracture lines and trabecular interruptions live.
EXPECTED REVELATION: A previously invisible lucent line traversing the calcaneal tuberosity/body, talar body or neck, or distal fibular metaphysis; subtle cortical step-off; focal trabecular pattern disruption; or confirmation that no such line exists anywhere in the hindfoot cortex (negative result is also valuable).
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: Noise amplification (CLAHE lifts rescanning grain into blocky, patchy background textures); edge "halos" (bright-dark fringes on both sides of strong edges) that can mimic periosteal reaction or a thin fracture line; ghosting/banding at the beam-field boundary; over-enhancement can make normal trabecular anisotropy or cortical laminations look like pathology.
COMPARISON TO ORIGINAL: Display original and enhanced side by side (and overlaid, blended) for each ROI. Every candidate line must satisfy: (a) it is present in both, with the enhanced image only making it more visible (not creating it); (b) it has a consistent width and follows plausible anatomic orientation; (c) its two endpoints meet a cortical margin or another anatomic structure. Any feature appearing only in the enhanced image is treated as an artifact.

PROPOSAL 2: Magnified bone-windowed crops of the ankle mortise and the posterior subtalar joint
QUESTION: Is the tibiotalar joint space and the posterior subtalar joint space maintained, are the articular surfaces congruent, and are there sub-millimeter step-offs, subchondral cysts, or early osteophytes?
METHOD: Extract tight crops (approx. 2-3x the joint region) around (a) the tibiotalar joint/mortise and (b) the posterior talocalcaneal joint + sinus tarsi. Resize to 2-4x using a sharp resampler (Lanczos), then apply a narrow "bone window" (local level/scale) to maximize contrast within the bone and joint-space gray range. Annotate the estimated joint-space width in pixels (and in mm, using a locally calibrated scale).
WHY IT MAY HELP: Joint-space assessment on a full-size image is limited by the 1-3 mm scale of the space versus image resolution and by global windowing that spreads contrast across very different tissue densities. Cropping + local windowing concentrates the display's dynamic range on the bone-joint-bone interface, where a 1 mm step-off or subchondral cyst must be detected; magnification improves the effective sampling of the articular contour.
EXPECTED REVELATION: Quantified joint-space widths for the tibiotalar and posterior subtalar joints; presence/absence of focal articular step-off, joint-space narrowing, subchondral lucency (cyst), or osteophyte at the talar dome, tibial plafond, or posterior talar/calcaneal facets.
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: Resampler ringing/overshoot along sharp cortices (Lanczos ripples) that can be misread as osteophytes; interpolation smoothing (if a soft resampler is used) can erase exactly the 1 mm detail being sought; magnification of rescanning noise; partial-volume blurring of the joint space by overlying soft tissue; moire from the scan; apparent narrowing due to the beam angle (tangential projection of the joint) rather than true loss of space.
COMPARISON TO ORIGINAL: Verify landmarks (plafond, talar dome, posterior facets) map 1:1 between crop and full image; re-detect any suspected lesion on the original at native scale; use the original for the final visual gestalt and the crop only to increase confidence in a specific measurement or finding. Joint widths should be cross-checked against expected anatomic norms (tibiotalar ~1-3 mm on lateral, not directly comparable to AP).

PROPOSAL 3: Skin-line segmentation and heel-pad / posterior soft-tissue contour quantification
QUESTION: Is there heel-pad thickening, plantar displacement of the fat pad (fat-pad sign), or posterior ankle soft-tissue swelling suggestive of occult fracture, hematoma, or effusion?
METHOD: Segment the skin contour (outer soft-tissue boundary) via thresholding/region-growing from the background (background is near-black and the limb is a large bright blob), then: (a) measure heel-pad thickness as the perpendicular distance between the plantar skin line and the inferior calcaneal cortex at the heel; (b) trace the posterior soft-tissue silhouette from the calf to the heel and measure its posterior convexity relative to a best-fit smooth envelope; (c) overlay the extracted contour back onto the image.
WHY IT MAY HELP: Soft-tissue swelling is one of the most sensitive (if non-specific) signs of an acute occult fracture, and the first pass could only judge it qualitatively. A quantitative contour makes the bulge or flatness of the posterior ankle objective, and heel-pad thickness gives a concrete number that can be compared against expected norms (adult heel pad roughly 15-20 mm) and, if ever available, against a contralateral or prior study.
EXPECTED REVELATION: A numeric heel-pad thickness; a measured posterior soft-tissue bulge (or a flat/negative contour excluding gross swelling); detection of the fat-pad sign (loss of the normal fat-tendon contrast at the plantar heel); or a clean negative that lowers the pre-test probability of acute hindfoot injury.
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: Background non-uniformity and the bright beam-field rectangle can break the threshold-based skin-line (erroneous splits or inclusion of the field edge); the leg's overlying soft tissue vs. the foot can be mis-joined at the ankle; motion or film-edge scratches near the boundary; the weight-bearing plantar surface is naturally flat, so plantar contour alone is uninformative — only posterior/heel-pad geometry is diagnostic; over-segmentation noise from grain can jitter the contour.
COMPARISON TO ORIGINAL: Overlaid contour must lie exactly on the visible skin edge at every point; manually correct obvious segmentation slips (scratches, field edges) before measuring; any reported bulge must be re-confirmed by direct visual inspection of the original silhouette; measure at standardized, reproducible reference points (e.g., 10 mm anterior to the most posterior calcaneal point) so the value is comparable across reviewers.

PROPOSAL 4: Sagittal-plane alignment and angle measurements (Bohler, tibiocalcaneal, heel pitch, talonavicular) plus true-lateral projection check
QUESTION: Are the hindfoot and midfoot sagittal alignment angles within normal limits, and is the projection a true (non-rotated) lateral?
METHOD: On a lightly enhanced copy (to sharpen landmark visibility only), place landmarks and compute: Bohler angle (posterosuperior and postero-inferior tuberosity points of the calcaneus, and the superior point of the posterior talar facet) — normal ~20-40 degrees; tibiocalcaneal angle (tibial shaft axis vs. calcaneal plantar axis); heel pitch (plantar calcaneal axis vs. horizontal/longitudinal axis of foot); talonavicular angle (talar and navicular long axes). Also perform the true-lateral check: assess superimposition of the talar head over the talar body and overlap of the malleolar shadows (a rotated foot displaces the talar head medially/laterally off the body).
WHY IT MAY HELP: Angle measurement is the only way to convert a qualitative "alignment looks fine" first-pass impression into quantitative, reproducible numbers; a normal Bohler angle argues against a displaced calcaneal fracture, and a true-lateral check determines whether all subsequent angular and joint-space interpretations on this single view are even valid (rotation systematically alters apparent angles and joint spaces).
EXPECTED REVELATION: Numeric Bohler, tibiocalcaneal, heel-pitch and talonavicular angles with a normal/abnormal call; confirmation (or refutation) that the view is a true lateral; if abnormal, localization of which segment (hindfoot vs. midfoot) deviates.
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: Landmark placement error dominates the error budget (a few pixels shift the Bohler angle by several degrees, especially at this resolution); if the foot is rotated, angles are systematically distorted and may be falsely "abnormal" — hence the projection check must be interpreted together; magnification differences do not affect angles (ratios) but affect the raw pixel geometry if landmarks are taken from a resized image — measure on the native-resolution original; the calcaneal tuberosity points are broad and fuzzy, so inter-point variation should be estimated.
COMPARISON TO ORIGINAL: All landmarks and the drawn angle lines/arcs must be rendered on (or overlaid to) the original image at native scale so each point can be visually verified against the actual anatomy; report the angle both as measured and with a +/- landmark uncertainty estimate; do not report an angle as abnormal unless it lies outside the normal range by more than the estimated measurement error.

PROPOSAL 5: Flat-field background correction plus dedicated edge-region analysis of the distal metatarsals (right crop boundary)
QUESTION: Is there bony pathology (fracture, lucency) in the partially included distal metatarsals, and how exactly is the image truncated at the right edge?
METHOD: Estimate the background illumination field (e.g., by heavy smoothing or fitting a low-order surface to background pixels), divide the image by it (flat-field / gain correction), then extract and enhance a crop of the right-edge region containing the visible distal metatarsals and the plantar soft tissue. Characterize the beam-field (collimation) boundary location relative to anatomy.
WHY IT MAY HELP: The first pass noted cropping at the right edge (toes excluded) and slight background non-uniformity; without the correction, the exposure falloff near the field edge can mask low-contrast detail in the distal metatarsals and the field-edge line itself can be confused with a fracture. A flat-fielded, locally enhanced crop is the only way to responsibly render a (limited) opinion on the visible distal metatarsals and to state precisely what anatomy is cut off.
EXPECTED REVELATION: Fracture line, cortical break, or focal lucency in the visible distal metatarsals (or their absence); a precise description of the cropping (which metatarsals/phalanges are excluded, where the collimation edge lies); confirmation that the field-edge is a straight, uniform, non-anatomic line distinct from any bony structure.
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: Over-correction (divide-by-estimation error) can create curved brightness artifacts that mimic periosteal reaction or a fracture line; the collimation/field edge is a straight high-contrast line that is a classic mimic of a fracture if not recognized; rescanning scratches and dust near the edge; partial-volume loss at the extreme edge (anatomy fades into background); division amplifies noise in the darkest background regions.
COMPARISON TO ORIGINAL: Show the corrected crop next to the uncorrected crop and the full original; any candidate bony line must be verified in the uncorrected original (the correction may reveal or create, so the original is the arbiter); the field edge is identified by its straightness, uniformity, and continuation across soft tissue and background; state explicitly which anatomy remains outside the field so no reader assumes a metatarsal is unfractured simply because it is not visible.
