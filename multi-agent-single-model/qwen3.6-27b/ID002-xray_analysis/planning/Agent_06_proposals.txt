PROPOSAL 2 — Bone-detail window (high-narrow window/level)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Question: Is the trabecular bone pattern in the distal humerus,
  proximal radius, and proximal ulna intact? Are there lucent lines
  consistent with fractures?

  Why this method: The 8-bit conversion has compressed the bone
  intensity range. The current image shows bone as uniformly bright
  (pixel values >180). A narrow window focused on the bone intensity
  range (e.g., window center ~210, width ~80, mapping values 170–250
  to the full 0–255 display range) will spread the subtle density
  variations within bone tissue across the full grayscale, making
  trabecular disruption, microfractures, and cortical irregularities
  more visible.

  What it will reveal:
    - Cortical bone continuity along the radius and ulna shafts
    - Trabecular pattern in the metaphyseal regions (proximal radius/
      ulna, distal humerus)
    - Any lucency lines that could represent non-displaced fractures
    - Cortical thickening or thinning suggestive of stress or pathology

  Potential artifacts:
    - Noise amplification: with a narrow window, small random
      intensity variations will appear as dark spots within bone,
      potentially mimicking cystic changes or fractures
    - Soft tissue detail will be completely lost (saturated to black)
    - This window should NOT be used to assess soft tissue

  Comparison with original:
    - Any suspected fracture line must be verified against the original
      image, as windowing can create false linear artifacts at
      intensity boundaries
    - Measure the pixel intensity profile across suspected lucency
      lines to confirm a genuine density drop vs. a windowing artifact


PROPOSAL 3 — Soft-tissue window around the elbow joint
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Question: Is there soft tissue swelling, joint effusion, or the
  "sail sign" (anterior fat pad elevation) around the elbow?

  Why this method: The current global contrast maps both bone and
  soft tissue, making the subtle soft-tissue details around the
  elbow nearly invisible. A soft-tissue window (e.g., window center
  ~40, width ~120, mapping values 0–120 to full display range) will
  reveal fat pads, muscle outlines, and joint capsule details.
  Anterior fat pad elevation (sail sign) is a classic indirect sign
  of elbow joint effusion, often associated with occult fracture.

  What it will reveal:
    - Anterior and posterior fat pad visibility
    - Soft tissue swelling proximal or distal to the joint
    - Joint space widening (suggesting effusion)
    - Muscle belly outlines and any displacement

  Potential artifacts:
    - Bone structures will be saturated to white and will obscure
      any adjacent soft-tissue detail at their margins
    - The window may introduce a washed-out appearance to regions
      with intermediate density (e.g., overlapping bone edges)
    - Partial volume effects at bone-soft-tissue interfaces may
      create false "halo" artifacts

  Comparison with original:
    - Confirm fat pad identification by correlating with expected
      anatomical positions relative to the radial notch and
      olecranon fossa
    - Any swelling claim must be checked against the original
      mid-range window to rule out windowing illusion


PROPOSAL 4 — Edge detection (Canny/Sobel) for fracture line
  identification
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Question: Are there discontinuities in the cortical bone outline
  of the radius or ulna that would suggest a fracture?

  Why this method: Cortical bone has a sharp intensity transition
  from bone (bright) to soft tissue (dark). Edge detection algorithms
  compute the gradient magnitude and will highlight these transitions.
  A normal cortex produces a smooth, continuous edge. A fracture
  produces an abrupt interruption, step, or angular deviation in
  the edge contour. Canny edge detection, with appropriate threshold
  tuning, will produce a clean map of all intensity boundaries.

  What it will reveal:
    - Complete cortical contour of both the radius and ulna
    - Any breaks, steps, or kinks in the cortical outline
    - Edge of the distal humeral condyles and elbow joint surfaces
    - The interosseous space between radius and ulna

  Potential artifacts:
    - Edge detection will also respond to soft-tissue boundaries,
      creating a cluttered edge map that must be interpreted
      carefully
    - Threshold selection is critical: too low = excessive noise
      edges; too high = missed subtle cortical disruptions
    - The image noise pattern (154 single-pixel bright components
      identified in the first pass) will produce spurious edge
      fragments
    - Edge detection does not distinguish cortical fracture from
      normal anatomical landmarks (e.g., radial styloid, ulnar
      tuberosity)

  Comparison with original:
    - Every edge discontinuity must be traced back to the original
      image to confirm it corresponds to a real structural
      discontinuity and not a soft-tissue edge
    - Use the edge map as a guide, not as evidence; the original
      image is the ground truth


PROPOSAL 5 — Local contrast enhancement (CLAHE) of the
  distal radius/ulna and wrist region
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Question: Are there fractures, subluxations, or alignment
  abnormalities in the distal radius/ulna or the proximal wrist
  joint?

  Why this method: The distal forearm and wrist area (visible at the
  bottom of the content region) is anatomically complex, with
  multiple bone structures in proximity (distal radius, distal
  ulna, proximal carpal row). Global histogram equalization would
  be dominated by the bright humerus and forearm shafts. CLAHE
  operates in small local tiles, adapting contrast independently
  in each region. This will enhance the distal structures without
  being swamped by the brighter proximal anatomy.

  What it will reveal:
    - Distal radius fracture patterns (e.g., Colles' fracture line)
    - Ulnar styloid integrity
    - Radio-ulnar alignment at the distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ)
    - Proximal carpal bone alignment and any subluxation
    - Joint space at the radiocarpal joint

  Potential artifacts:
    - CLAHE can amplify noise within uniform regions, creating
      speckled or grainy appearance that mimics trabecular
      pathology
    - Tile boundary artifacts: visible seams between CLAHE tiles
      if the tile size is too small relative to anatomical features
    - Over-enhancement: with high clip limits, normal anatomical
      borders may appear artificially sharp

  Comparison with original:
    - Verify any suspected distal fracture or subluxation against
      the original image
    - Measure the inter-bone distances in the original to confirm
      alignment assessments


PROPOSAL 6 — Bone alignment and angulation analysis
  (medial longitudinal axis measurement)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Question: Is there abnormal angulation, rotation, or displacement
  of the radius and ulna relative to each other or to the humerus?

  Why this method: The radius and ulna should run approximately
  parallel from the elbow to the wrist. In a lateral projection,
  they may partially overlap, but their central axes should be
  straight and smoothly curved. A quantitative approach:

    1. Threshold the image at a bone-level intensity (e.g., >150)
    2. Extract the left and right cortical edges of each bone
    3. Compute the medial axis (skeleton) of each bone
    4. Fit a smooth curve to each medial axis
    5. Measure deviations from smooth curvature

  This will detect subtle angular deformities that the eye might
  miss, especially if the image was viewed quickly.

  What it will reveal:
    - Angular deformity of the radius or ulna shaft (bayonet
      deformity, greenstick angulation)
    - Rotation of the forearm relative to the humerus
    - Displacement at the elbow joint (proximal radio-ulnar)
    - Abnormal interosseous width along the shaft length

  Potential artifacts:
    - The lateral projection means the radius and ulna are partially
      superimposed. Skeletonization may merge the two bones in
      overlapping regions, producing a single merged medial axis
      instead of two
    - Image resolution at 1600x1280 may be insufficient for
      sub-degree angulation measurements
    - Patient rotation during acquisition will distort the apparent
      alignment, producing false-positive angulation

  Comparison with original:
    - Confirm any measured angulation by visual inspection of
      the cortical outlines in the original image
    - Consider whether the projection angle (pure lateral vs.
      oblique) explains any apparent non-parallelism


PROPOSAL 7 — Elbow joint space assessment
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Question: Is the elbow joint space preserved? Is there joint space
  narrowing, widening, or incongruity suggesting dislocation,
  fracture-dislocation, or degenerative changes?

  Why this method: The elbow joint (humeroulnar and humeroradial
  articulation) is a critical region to assess. In a lateral view,
  the trochlear notch of the ulna should articulate smoothly with
  the trochlea of the humerus, and the radial head should sit in
  the radial notch. The joint space should be uniform (~2-4 mm).
  A targeted analysis:

    1. Crop to the elbow joint region
    2. Apply a medium window to visualize the joint surface
    3. Measure the joint space width at multiple points along the
       articulation
    4. Assess congruity of the articular surfaces

  What it will reveal:
    - Joint space narrowing (osteoarthritis)
    - Joint space widening (effusion, ligamentous injury,
      dislocation)
    - Articular surface incongruity (intra-articular fracture)
    - Radial head position relative to the capitellum (should
      align in a straight line — the "capitellar line")

  Potential artifacts:
    - Lateral projection may not show the full joint space (only
      visible in true lateral or AP view)
    - If the lateral view is not perfectly tangential, the joint
      space may appear asymmetric due to projection geometry
    - Overlapping bone structures may obscure the true joint space

  Comparison with original:
    - Joint space measurements must be contextualized by the
      exact projection angle, which is unknown
    - Capitellar line assessment should be verified visually
    - Any effusion sign (fat pad elevation) must be correlated
      with Proposal 3

PROPOSAL 10 — Comparison with an atlas reference for lateral
  elbow/forearm radiograph
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Question: Does the overall anatomy and alignment of this forearm
  X-ray match the expected appearance of a normal lateral elbow/
  forearm radiograph?

  Why this method: Without DICOM metadata confirming the projection,
  orientation, and patient positioning, it is difficult to assess
  whether apparent abnormalities are real or projectional artifacts.
  A side-by-side comparison with a reference normal lateral elbow/
  forearm radiograph of similar magnification and orientation will
  highlight:

    - Whether the radial head and neck are normally positioned
    - Whether the olecranon process sits normally in the olecranon
      fossa
    - Whether the distal humeral condyles are normally shaped
    - Whether the soft tissue envelope is of normal thickness
    - Whether the relative proportions of the radius and ulna
      are normal

  What it will reveal:
    - Global deviations from normal anatomy
    - Whether the projection is a true lateral or an oblique
    - Whether the elbow is in normal flexion angle for the view
    - Regions where this image differs most from a reference

  Potential artifacts:
    - Comparison is only valid if the reference image has similar
      magnification and projection angle
    - Normal anatomical variation (age, sex, handedness) may
      produce legitimate differences from a reference
    - Without knowing patient age, distinguishing developmental
      variants from pathology is limited

  Comparison with original:
    - Use the reference only for gross pattern matching, not
      for quantitative measurements
    - Any discrepancy identified should be investigated in
      the original image before being flagged as abnormal
