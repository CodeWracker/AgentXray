PROPOSAL 2 — ADAPTIVE CONTRAST ENHANCEMENT (CLAHE-STYLE WINDOWING)
================================================================================

Question being answered:
  Can low-contrast structures (subtle trabecular changes, cortical thinning,
  soft tissue details) be made visible by better distributing the available
  pixel intensity range?

Why this method helps:
  My first-pass showed the histogram is extremely skewed: the three most common
  values (0, 1, 2) account for ~1.4 million pixels (~69%). Only 13% of pixels
  exceed intensity 100. The clinically relevant bone and soft tissue
  intensities (approximately 10-250) are compressed into a narrow range,
  wasting the dynamic range. Contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization
  (CLAHE) or manual window/level adjustment would redistribute the intensity
  values across the full 0-255 range, making subtle density variations visible.

Expected to reveal:
  - Improved visibility of the trabecular bone pattern within the humeral
    metaphysis and distal forearm bones.
  - Better delineation of the soft tissue envelope around the bones,
    potentially revealing fat pad signs (anterior and posterior elbow fat pads).
  - Enhanced visibility of joint space margins at the humeroulnar and
    humeroradial articulations.
  - Potential subtle cortical irregularities that are invisible in the
    original contrast range.

Potential artifacts/misleading effects:
  - Over-enhancement can introduce false "fracture lines" by amplifying
    background noise or compression artifacts.
  - CLAHE can create unnatural brightness transitions at block boundaries if
    the tile size is too small.
  - Soft tissue enhancement may reduce bone contrast; a separate "bone window"
    and "soft tissue window" may be needed.
  - Any new apparent abnormalities must be verified against the original image.

How to compare with original:
  - Always maintain the original image for cross-reference.
  - For each enhanced region, zoom to the same area in the original to confirm
    whether the feature is real or an artifact of contrast manipulation.
  - Apply conservative enhancement first (e.g., clip limit 2.0 in CLAHE),
    then assess whether stronger settings add real information or just noise.

PROPOSAL 3 — EDGE ENHANCEMENT VIA UNSHARP MASKING FOR CORTICAL BONE
  MARGIN ANALYSIS
================================================================================

Question being answered:
  Are there any subtle cortical step-offs, breaks, or irregularities along the
  ulnar and radial shafts, the olecranon, coronoid process, or radial head that
  might indicate nondisplaced fractures?

Why this method helps:
  Unsharp masking subtracts a blurred version of the image from itself,
  amplifying high-frequency content (edges). Cortical bone margins in X-rays
  are sharp transitions from dense bone to less dense soft tissue. Enhancing
  these transitions makes subtle discontinuities more apparent. This is
  particularly valuable for:
  - Radial head fractures (often occult on lateral view)
  - Olecranon fractures (common in falls on outstretched hand)
  - Coronoid process fractures (Mayo classification relevant to elbow
    instability)
  - Supracondylar humerus fractures

Expected to reveal:
  - Enhanced cortical outlines of the ulna (both anterior and posterior
    cortices) along the visible shaft.
  - Enhanced cortical outline of the radius, particularly at the radial head
    and neck.
  - The articular surfaces of the trochlea and capitellum.
  - Any subtle cortical step-off or lucency that corresponds to a fracture
    line would appear as a localized dark break in the enhanced cortical line.

Potential artifacts/misleading effects:
  - Unsharp masking amplifies noise alongside edges. Background noise in the
    dark regions may appear as spurious "fracture lines."
  - The halo effect (dark rings around bright objects) is characteristic of
    unsharp masking and may be mistaken for cortical erosions.
  - If the kernel size is too large, real fine features may be suppressed;
    if too small, noise dominates.
  - Must use mild settings (e.g., sigma 1-2 pixels, amount 0.5-1.0) and
    validate all findings against the original.

How to compare with original:
  - For any apparent cortical disruption, immediately verify on the original
    image at full resolution.
  - Edge enhancement alone is never diagnostic; it is an aid to visual
    inspection. A "positive" finding must be confirmed on the unprocessed
    image.
  - Compare the enhanced cortical line with the expected smooth anatomical
    contour: the ulnar shaft should be a smooth curve; the radial shaft should
    be nearly straight.

PROPOSAL 4 — LINE PROFILE ANALYSIS ALONG THE ULNAR AND RADIAL CORTICAL
  MARGINS
================================================================================

Question being answered:
  Is the cortical margin of the ulna and radius smooth and continuous, or are
  there localized intensity dips or peaks that suggest cortical disruption
  (fracture) or periosteal reaction?

Why this method helps:
  Visual inspection of bone cortices can miss subtle changes. A quantitative
  line profile traces pixel intensity along a path following the expected
  cortical margin. In a normal bone, the cortical margin shows a smooth,
  consistent high-intensity band. A fracture, periosteal reaction, or
  lytic lesion would create a characteristic local deviation:
  - Fracture: a dip in intensity (radiolucent line through the cortex)
  - Periosteal reaction: an additional intensity peak outside the cortex
  - Cortical thinning: a reduction in the width of the high-intensity band

Expected to reveal:
  - Posterior ulnar cortex (olecranon to distal shaft): expected to be smooth
    with a consistent intensity profile. Any dip >20 intensity units from the
    local baseline would warrant closer inspection.
  - Anterior ulnar cortex (coronoid to distal shaft): similarly expected to
    be smooth.
  - Radial head and neck: the radial neck is a common site of subtle fracture
    and may show a localized intensity dip or cortical irregularity.
  - Distal humeral cortex at the supracondylar region: a common site of
    fracture, particularly in children.

Potential artifacts/misleading effects:
  - Partial volume effect: if the line path does not perfectly follow the
    cortical surface, the profile may show artifactual intensity variations.
  - Overlap of the radius and ulna in the lateral projection may create
    confusing intensity patterns where the two bones are superimposed.
  - Noise in the image can create small fluctuations that are not clinically
    significant; the profile should be smoothed (e.g., moving average over
    3-5 pixels) before interpretation.

How to compare with original:
  - Any anomaly detected in the line profile should be mapped back to the
    original image at the corresponding pixel location for visual confirmation.
  - The line profile path should be verified against the original image to
    ensure it follows the actual cortical margin.
  - Compare anterior and posterior cortex profiles of the same bone: they
    should be roughly similar in pattern.

PROPOSAL 5 — REGIONAL DENSITY ANALYSIS OF THE ELBOW JOINT SPACE
================================================================================

Question being answered:
  Is the humeroulnar and humeroradial joint space uniform and within normal
  limits, or is there joint space narrowing, asymmetry, or irregularity that
  suggests degenerative change, effusion, or occult fracture?

Why this method helps:
  The elbow joint space on a lateral view consists of the humeroulnar joint
  (trochlea in the trochlear notch) and the humeroradial joint (capitellum
  articulating with the radial head). These joint spaces should appear as
  uniform radiolucent gaps between the articular surfaces. Quantitative
  measurement of the joint space width along its length would detect
  asymmetries or focal narrowing that might be missed by visual inspection.

Expected to reveal:
  - Humeroulnar joint space width: normally ~1-3 mm. Focal narrowing could
    indicate early degenerative changes or cartilage loss.
  - Humeroradial joint space width: normally ~1-2 mm.
  - The "double arc sign" (normal alignment of radial head arc with
    capitellar arc) can be assessed.
  - Joint space widening on one side could indicate ligamentous injury.

Potential artifacts/misleading effects:
  - Lateral projection with the elbow flexed at 90 degrees means the joint
    space width is viewed obliquely, not in true cross-section. The measured
    width may not correspond to the actual anatomical width.
  - Overlap of the radial head with the capitellum in a true lateral view
    makes separate measurement of the humeroradial joint space challenging.
  - Image resolution limits (estimated ~3-5 pixels/mm) may not resolve joint
    spaces of 1-2 mm accurately.

How to compare with original:
  - Verify the joint space measurement on the original image at full
    resolution.
  - Compare the measured joint space with expected normal values for the
    patient's age (if known).
  - Assess whether the joint space appears uniform along its visible length.

PROPOSAL 6 — SOFT TISSUE SILHOUETTE ANALYSIS: FAT PAD SIGNS
================================================================================

Question being answered:
  Is there an elevated anterior fat pad (sail sign) or visible posterior fat
  pad in the elbow, which would indicate intra-articular effusion and possible
  occult fracture?

Why this method helps:
  The fat pad signs are one of the most important indicators of occult elbow
  fracture. The anterior fat pad (in the coronoid fossa) is normally not
  visible or barely visible. Elevation of the anterior fat pad (sail sign)
  indicates joint effusion, which on a traumatized elbow is highly suggestive
  of an occult fracture (especially radial head or supracondylar). The
  posterior fat pad (in the olecranon fossa) should never be visible; its
  presence is always abnormal.

  My first-pass quantitative analysis noted that the soft tissue region has
  low contrast (mean intensity 39.2 in the dark regions, with values 0-20
  for most non-bone pixels). Enhancing the soft tissue window specifically
  would bring these fat pads into view.

Expected to reveal:
  - Anterior fat pad: if visible as a triangular radiolucency at the anterior
    aspect of the distal humerus (coronoid fossa), this constitutes a positive
  - sail sign and suggests joint effusion.
  - Posterior fat pad: if visible as a radiolucency behind the olecranon,
    this is pathognomonic for intra-articular pathology.
  - Overall soft tissue swelling around the elbow: diffuse thickening of the
    soft tissue envelope compared to normal.

Potential artifacts/misleading effects:
  - Normal soft tissue folds and skin creases may mimic fat pad elevation.
  - The lateral projection with elbow flexion means the anterior and posterior
    compartments overlap, potentially obscuring the fat pads.
  - Contrast enhancement can create false soft tissue boundaries by
    amplifying noise.

How to compare with original:
  - Any fat pad identified in the enhanced image must be confirmed on the
    original image.
  - The expected location of the anterior fat pad is just anterior to the
    coronoid process. The posterior fat pad would be in the olecranon fossa.
  - Soft tissue thickness should be compared to expected normal for the
    patient's body habitus (if known).

PROPOSAL 7 — RADIAL HEAD DEDICATED ASSESSMENT
================================================================================

Question being answered:
  Is the radial head intact, or is there a subtle fracture (Mason type I) that
  is obscured by the lateral projection and low image contrast?

Why this method helps:
  Radial head fractures are among the most common elbow fractures and are
  notoriously difficult to detect on a single lateral view. A nondisplaced
  radial head fracture (Mason type I) may show only a subtle cortical
  impression or hairline lucency. A dedicated assessment of the radial head
  region, with optimized windowing and edge enhancement, can reveal findings
  missed on general review.

  The radial head is located at the elbow joint, articulating with the
  capitellum. On a true lateral view, the radial head should be a smooth,
  rounded structure with a continuous cortical rim. The "capitellar line"
  (drawn down the center of the capitellum) should bisect the radial head
  on an AP view, but on lateral view the key finding is the smoothness of the
  radial head contour.

Expected to reveal:
  - Smooth, continuous cortical rim of the radial head with no step-offs,
    lucencies, or irregularities.
  - Alternatively, a focal cortical depression or hairline lucency would
    suggest a nondisplaced fracture.
  - The radial neck region, just distal to the radial head, is another common
    fracture site.

Potential artifacts/misleading effects:
  - Superimposition of the radial head over other structures in the lateral
    view can create confusing radiopacity patterns.
  - Edge enhancement may create a false "step-off" at the junction between
    the radial head and radial neck (a normal anatomical transition).
  - The limited resolution (~3-5 pixels/mm) may not resolve a hairline
    fracture that is less than 1 pixel wide.

How to compare with original:
  - The radial head region should be examined at maximum zoom on the original
    image.
  - Any apparent fracture line on the enhanced image must be verified on the
    unprocessed original.
  - The normal smooth contour of the radial head should be used as the
    reference standard.

PROPOSAL 8 — FOREARM BONE ALIGNMENT AND BOWING ASSESSMENT
================================================================================

Question being answered:
  Is the alignment of the ulna and radius normal, or is there evidence of
  malalignment, bowing, or angular deformity suggestive of a healing fracture
  or congenital abnormality?

Why this method helps:
  The ulna normally has a slight anterior bow (convex posteriorly) when viewed
  laterally. The radius normally runs straight or with a very slight curve.
  Abnormal bowing could indicate:
  - A healed or healing displaced fracture with malunion
  - Congenital bowing (e.g., congenital pseudarthrosis)
  - Post-traumatic deformity

  Quantitative assessment involves tracing the midline of each bone shaft and
  measuring the deviation from a straight line connecting the proximal and
  distal reference points.

Expected to reveal:
  - Normal ulnar bow: slight posterior convexity (the ulnar shaft bows
    posteriorly, with the apex of the curve at the midshaft).
  - Normal radial alignment: nearly straight or very slight anterior bow.
  - Any focal angulation (sudden change in direction) along either shaft
    would suggest a healed fracture with malunion.
  - The interosseous space between the radius and ulna should be relatively
    uniform.

Potential artifacts/misleading effects:
  - Patient positioning (degree of elbow flexion, forearm rotation) can
    alter the apparent alignment.
  - The image may be partially cropped at the proximal humerus and distal
    forearm, limiting the length of bone available for alignment assessment.
  - Mild rotation of the forearm away from true lateral will alter the
    apparent relationship between the radius and ulna.

How to compare with original:
  - Trace the midline of each bone on the original image at full resolution.
  - Verify that the apparent bowing is consistent along the entire visible
    shaft, not localized to one region.
  - Compare the measured alignment with standard reference images of normal
    lateral elbow/forearm anatomy.

PROPOSAL 9 — GRAYSCALE HISTOGRAM ANALYSIS OF THE ANATOMICAL REGION
================================================================================

Question being answered:
  What is the actual distribution of pixel intensities in the anatomical
  content region, and what does this tell us about exposure quality and the
  optimal windowing parameters?

Why this method helps:
  My first-pass analysis showed the global histogram is dominated by empty
  background pixels (~69% at values 0-2). A regional histogram computed only
  on the anatomical content (after cropping) would reveal the true intensity
  distribution of the radiographic data. This would:
  - Identify the optimal window/level for bone visualization
  - Identify the optimal window/level for soft tissue visualization
  - Assess whether the exposure is appropriate (bone structures should
    occupy a substantial range of the intensity spectrum)
  - Detect whether the 8-bit conversion from the original DICOM has
    discarded clinically relevant information

Expected to reveal:
  - The histogram should show a multimodal distribution: a peak at low
    intensities (soft tissue/air), a broad peak at medium intensities
    (trabecular bone and soft tissue overlap), and a peak at high intensities
    (cortical bone).
  - The width of the high-intensity peak indicates the range of cortical bone
    density. A narrow peak suggests limited dynamic range, meaning the 8-bit
    conversion may have compressed important detail.
  - The low-intensity range reveals soft tissue detail. A very narrow low-
    intensity range suggests soft tissue detail is compressed and could be
    recovered with appropriate windowing.

Potential artifacts/misleading effects:
  - If the anatomical region boundaries are imprecise, background pixels will
    contaminate the histogram and distort the analysis.
  - The histogram reflects the combined intensity of overlapping structures
    (e.g., humerus, soft tissue, and background in the same column of pixels).
  - 8-bit PNG conversion is lossy relative to the original DICOM data; the
    histogram cannot recover information that was lost in the conversion.

How to compare with original:
  - Use the histogram to define specific window/level parameters, then apply
    them to the original image to verify that the resulting visualization is
    diagnostically useful.
  - Compare the regional histogram with published reference histograms for
    normal elbow radiographs (if available).

PROPOSAL 10 — GRAYSCALE TO SINGLE-CHANNEL CONVERSION AND QUANTITATIVE
  PIXEL ANALYSIS
================================================================================

Question being answered:
  Is the image truly single-channel grayscale stored redundantly as RGB, and
  what are the exact pixel statistics of the anatomical region?

Why this method helps:
  My first-pass analysis confirmed that all three RGB channels are
  bit-identical (max inter-channel difference = 0.0), meaning the image is
  effectively 8-bit grayscale stored as 24-bit RGB. Converting to single-
  channel grayscale is the foundation for all subsequent quantitative
  analysis. Additionally, computing precise statistics on specific anatomical
  subregions (humerus, elbow joint, proximal ulna, distal ulna, radial head,
  radial shaft) would establish baseline density values for comparison.

Expected to reveal:
  - Confirmed single-channel data (R = G = B for all pixels).
  - Regional mean, standard deviation, min, max, and percentile values for
    each anatomical subregion.
  - The dynamic range within the anatomical region: the difference between
    the brightest and darkest anatomical pixels (excluding empty background).
  - Identification of any localized areas of abnormally high or low density
    that could represent pathology (e.g., osteolytic or osteoblastic lesions).

Potential artifacts/misleading effects:
  - Defining anatomical subregions imprecisely will mix different tissue types
    and confound the statistics.
  - Small regions (e.g., radial head, which may occupy fewer than 100 pixels)
    will have noisy statistics that are not meaningful.
  - The statistics alone cannot diagnose pathology; they must be interpreted
    in anatomical context.

How to compare with original:
  - Verify that the grayscale conversion is lossless (all information
    preserved, since R=G=B means no color information exists).
  - Use the statistics to identify regions of interest for closer visual
    inspection on the original image.

PROPOSAL 11 — FREQUENCY-DOMAIN ANALYSIS (2D FOURIER TRANSFORM)
================================================================================

Question being answered:
  What are the frequency characteristics of the image, and are there any
  directional patterns or artifacts that would inform the optimal sharpening
  or noise-reduction strategy?

Why this method helps:
  A 2D Fourier transform decomposes the image into its frequency components.
  For an X-ray of the elbow, this would reveal:
  - The dominant spatial frequencies (related to bone width and cortical
    thickness)
  - Whether there are directional frequency components (e.g., periodic
    artifacts from compression, scanning artifacts, or grid lines)
  - The noise floor (high-frequency content that is random noise vs.
    meaningful detail)

  Understanding the frequency content guides the choice of sharpening
  parameters: sharpening should amplify frequencies corresponding to
  anatomical detail while suppressing noise frequencies.

Expected to reveal:
  - Low-frequency components: the overall shape and position of the bones
    (large-scale structure).
  - Mid-frequency components: cortical bone margins, trabecular patterns,
    joint spaces.
  - High-frequency components: noise, fine detail, and any artifacts.
  - The power spectrum would show the distribution of energy across
    frequencies, guiding the design of an optimal band-pass filter for
    enhancing diagnostically relevant features.

Potential artifacts/misleading effects:
  - The Fourier transform of a cropped rectangular region can introduce
    edge artifacts (high-frequency components from the sharp transition
    between anatomical content and black background).
  - Windowing (e.g., Hann or Hamming window) should be applied before the
    FFT to minimize these edge artifacts.
  - The frequency analysis is informative for guiding processing parameters
    but does not directly identify anatomical abnormalities.

How to compare with original:
  - Use the frequency analysis to select optimal parameters for sharpening
    and noise reduction.
  - Apply the derived filters and compare the filtered image with the original
    to verify that anatomically relevant detail is enhanced and noise is
    suppressed.

PROPOSAL 12 — LOCALIZED REGION-OF-INTEREST (ROI) EXTRACTION AND
  INDIVIDUAL ENHANCEMENT FOR THE OLECRANON AND CORONOID PROCESSES
================================================================================

Question being answered:
  Are the olecranon and coronoid processes of the proximal ulna intact, or is
  there a fracture, avulsion, or impaction injury at either of these critical
  anatomical landmarks?

Why this method helps:
  The olecranon and coronoid processes are small, complex bony structures at
  the proximal end of the ulna. The olecranon is the most common site of
  elbow fracture in adults (often from falling on an outstretched hand with
  the elbow flexed). The coronoid process is a key stabilizer of the elbow
  joint, and fractures here are associated with elbow instability
  (terrible triad injury: coronoid fracture + radial head fracture +
  posterolateral rotatory instability).

  Because these structures are small relative to the overall image, they may
  not benefit from global contrast enhancement. Extracting them as individual
  ROIs and applying tailored windowing and sharpening would optimize
  visualization of these specific regions.

Expected to reveal:
  - Olecranon: smooth, continuous cortical margin at the tip and along the
    articular surface. No lucent fracture lines.
  - Coronoid process: smooth, triangular bony prominence at the anterior
    ulna. No cortical disruption.
  - The olecranon fossa of the humerus: should be a smooth concave surface
    that accommodates the olecranon in extension.
  - Any step-off, lucent line, or comminution at either process would
    indicate fracture.

Potential artifacts/misleading effects:
  - The olecranon and coronoid are close together in the lateral projection,
    and the joint space between them (trochlear notch) may be confused with
    a fracture line.
  - Over-sharpening of small ROIs can create edge halos that mimic cortical
    step-offs.
  - The FOV may not include the very tip of the olecranon if it extends
    beyond the image frame.

How to compare with original:
  - Any suspected fracture in the ROI-enhanced view must be verified on the
    original full-resolution image.
  - The normal anatomy of the trochlear notch should be used as a reference
    to distinguish it from a fracture line.
