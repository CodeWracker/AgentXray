PROPOSAL 1 — BONE-CENTRIC WINDOW LEVEL RE-MAPPING
================================================================================

QUESTION:
What is the actual cortical and trabecular bone detail across the visualized
distal humerus, proximal radius, and proximal ulna? Does the cortex show any
subtle step-offs, thinning, or irregularity that might indicate a
nondisplaced fracture or stress injury?

WHY THIS METHOD:
The current 8-bit PNG wastes ~247 of 256 available intensity levels on the
black background (pixel values 0-8). The actual bone content occupies roughly
the range 50-254. Re-mapping this range to 0-255 spreads the bone detail
across the full display range, effectively recovering contrast that was
squeezed by the suboptimal DICOM-to-PNG conversion windowing. A narrow
"bone window" (e.g., mapping pixels 100-254 to 0-255) is even more targeted
and will maximize visibility of cortical thickness variation and trabecular
pattern.

WHAT TO EXPECT:
- Sharper cortical margins on the distal humerus, olecranon, radial head, and
  ulnar shaft
- Visible trabecular architecture in the epiphyseal/metaphyseal regions
- Any cortical thinning, focal lucency, or subtle step-off becomes more
  conspicuous
- The radial head/neck region (a common occult fracture site) may reveal
  subtle fractures invisible at current windowing

ARTIFACTS / MISLEADING EFFECTS:
- The re-mapping amplifies any quantization noise present at the 8-bit level.
  Adjacent intensity bands that were indistinguishable may become artificially
  distinct, potentially suggesting trabecular abnormalities where none exist.
- Pixels at the mapping boundary (e.g., value 100 in a narrow bone window)
  become hard-clipped to 0, losing all information below that threshold.
- Bone trabecular patterns can appear coarser than they truly are because the
  8-bit quantization cannot faithfully represent the fine density gradients
  of normal cancellous bone.

COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL:
Always display the bone-windowed version side-by-side with the original. Any
finding on the enhanced view must be confirmed to exist on the original — if
a "cortical step-off" appears only on the enhanced view and not on the
original, it is likely a contrast-enhancement artifact.

PROPOSAL 2 — SOFT-TISSUE WINDOW FOR FAT PAD AND SWELLING ASSESSMENT
================================================================================

QUESTION:
Are the anterior and posterior fat pads visible at the elbow joint? Is the
anterior fat pad elevated (positive "sail sign")? Is the posterior fat pad
visible at all (which is always abnormal)? Is there periarticular soft tissue
swelling suggesting occult intra-articular injury?

WHY THIS METHOD:
The fat pad signs are among the most sensitive indirect radiographic markers
of occult elbow fracture. The anterior fat pad sits in the coronoid fossa and
is normally barely visible; elevation by hemarthrosis from an occult fracture
makes it triangular and prominent. The posterior fat pad in the olecranon
fossa is normally invisible — its visibility always indicates joint effusion.
The current image maps the soft-tissue intensity range (approximately 5-100)
into only ~95 of 256 levels, making these low-contrast structures hard to
discern. A dedicated soft-tissue window (mapping pixel values 5-120 to 0-255)
will expand this range and make fat-muscle interfaces much clearer.

WHAT TO EXPECT:
- Clearer visualization of the anterior fat pad in the coronoid fossa
  (anterior to the distal humerus)
- Ability to determine whether the posterior fat pad is visible behind the
  olecranon
- Better definition of the soft tissue envelope around the elbow, allowing
  comparison of anterior vs. posterior soft tissue thickness
- Detection of any calcific foci or foreign bodies in the soft tissues

ARTIFACTS / MISLEADING EFFECTS:
- The soft-tissue window will wash out all bone detail (bone pixels 100-254
  will be clipped to 255, appearing as featureless white silhouettes). This
  is expected and acceptable for soft-tissue assessment, but bone detail
  cannot be evaluated on this view.
- Quantization noise at the 8-bit level may create apparent "bands" or
  "steps" in the soft tissue gradient that do not correspond to real
  anatomical interfaces.
- The soft tissue around the upper humerus (thick muscle mass) may have
  different attenuation from the thinner forearm soft tissue, creating an
  apparent density difference that is purely geometric, not pathological.

COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL:
Cross-reference any fat pad visibility or swelling finding with the original
image. The posterior fat pad, when present, is a robust finding that should
be visible at multiple window settings. Anterior fat pad assessment should
also be consistent across views, as the coronoid fossa boundary provides a
clear anatomical landmark.

PROPOSAL 3 — CLAHE (ADAPTIVE HISTOGRAM EQUALIZATION) FOR LOCAL CONTRAST
================================================================================

QUESTION:
What fine anatomical detail is hidden by locally variable contrast,
particularly at the elbow joint articulation, radial head, olecranon, and
coronoid process?

WHY THIS METHOD:
Global contrast stretching (Proposals 1-2) treats the entire image uniformly.
However, the elbow region has vastly different density characteristics in
adjacent areas: dense cortical bone next to the joint space next to soft
tissue. CLAHE operates on small tiles (e.g., 32x32 or 64x64 pixels) and
equalizes the histogram within each tile, then blends the results. This
preserves global brightness relationships while boosting local contrast.
It is particularly effective at revealing fine detail at tissue boundaries
and in regions where the dynamic range is locally compressed.

WHAT TO EXPECT:
- Enhanced visibility of the radial head cortex — critical for detecting
  Mason-type fractures (nondisplaced radial head fractures are frequently
  missed on standard viewing)
- Clearer definition of the olecranon-corporonoid process junction and
  cortical margins
- Better visualization of the articular surface of the trochlea and
  capitellum, helping assess for osteochondral injuries
- Enhanced trabecular pattern in the distal humeral metaphysis

ARTIFACTS / MISLEADING EFFECTS:
- CLAHE can over-amplify noise, especially in the soft-tissue regions where
  signal is low. Use a low clip limit (e.g., 2-4) to prevent noise blowup.
- Tile boundaries can produce visible seams if the tile size is too small
  relative to anatomical features. A tile size of at least 64x64 pixels is
  recommended for this image.
- CLAHE may create apparent "holes" or hypodense regions within bone that
  correspond to noise rather than true lytic lesions.
- The adaptive nature means the same anatomical structure may appear with
  different contrast depending on its neighbors, which can be misleading
  for global density assessment.

COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL:
The CLAHE view should only be used to generate hypotheses about specific
locations. Any potential finding must be verified on the original image and
on the global bone-window view. The CLAHE view is NOT diagnostic on its own
— it is a contrast-enhancement aid.

PROPOSAL 4 — LINE PROFILE ANALYSIS ALONG CORTICAL MARGINS
================================================================================

QUESTION:
Do the cortical margins of the radius, ulna, and distal humerus maintain
smooth continuity, or are there focal irregularities (step-offs, notches,
buccal deformities) suggesting occult fracture, periosteal reaction, or
cortical erosion?

WHY THIS METHOD:
Visual inspection of cortical margins is the primary method for detecting
nondisplaced fractures. However, subtle step-offs (<2mm) can be missed by
the eye, especially on an image with suboptimal contrast and limited
resolution. A line profile traces pixel intensity along a user-defined path
that follows the expected cortical margin. In a normal cortex, the profile
shows a smooth, continuous high-intensity band. A cortical disruption creates
a characteristic pattern: the bright cortical band dips (fracture gap) and
rebounds. Quantitative line profiles make these discontinuities objective
and measurable.

Specific paths to trace:
  (a) Anterior cortex of the distal humerus (supracondylar region, ~1cm
      proximal to the joint) — trace from the medial epicondyle region
      laterally along the anterior surface
  (b) Posterior cortex of the olecranon — trace the outer posterior margin
      of the olecranon process from the tip distally
  (c) Lateral cortex of the radial head and neck — trace the outer margin
      of the radial head down through the neck
  (d) Anterior cortex of the coronoid process — trace the anterior surface
  (e) Diaphyseal cortices of the radius and ulna — trace along the
      interosseous borders

WHAT TO EXPECT:
- Smooth, continuous intensity peaks along normal cortical margins
- Any dip in the intensity profile that exceeds the local noise floor and
  has a width consistent with a fracture gap (typically 1-3 pixels on this
  image) would be suspicious
- Periosteal reaction would appear as a secondary parallel bright line
  offset from the main cortex
- Cortical thickening (sclerosis) would appear as a broader-than-expected
  high-intensity region

ARTIFACTS / MISLEADING EFFECTS:
- The line profile path must be manually or algorithmically placed. If the
  path does not follow the true cortical margin (due to curvature or
  misalignment), the profile will show artificial dips where the line crosses
  the cortex at an oblique angle rather than along it.
- Superimposed anatomy (e.g., the radius overlapping the ulna in a lateral
  view) can create apparent "dips" in the profile that correspond to the
  boundary between two bones, not a cortical disruption.
- Projectional overlap means a fracture line running perpendicular to the
  beam may not produce a visible dip in the profile (it would be seen on an
  orthogonal AP view instead).

COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL:
Plot the line profiles as intensity-vs-distance graphs alongside the original
image with the traced path overlaid in color. Any suspicious dip should be
visually confirmed on the original image at the corresponding location. The
profile is a quantitative aid, not a replacement for visual inspection.

PROPOSAL 5 — GRADIENT MAGNITUDE MAP FOR FRACTURE LINE DETECTION
================================================================================

QUESTION:
Are there linear hypodense structures traversing the cortical or trabecular
bone that could represent fracture lines, and particularly, is there a
fracture line through the radial head, olecranon, or supracondylar humerus?

WHY THIS METHOD:
A fracture line on X-ray appears as a dark (hypodense) linear structure
within bright (hyperdense) bone. The gradient magnitude (computed via Sobel
or Scharr operators) measures the local intensity change. At a fracture line,
there is a sharp transition from bone brightness to the darker fracture gap,
producing a high gradient. Unlike edge detection, which produces a binary
output, a gradient magnitude map preserves the strength of each transition,
allowing discrimination between true anatomical boundaries (cortex-air
interface, very high gradient) and subtle internal lines (fracture, moderate
gradient).

The gradient map should be computed on the bone-windowed version (Proposal 1)
to maximize the contrast at internal bone interfaces.

WHAT TO EXPECT:
- Strong gradient signals at all cortical margins (expected, and serves as a
  quality control — if cortical margins don't show up, the processing is
  wrong)
- Normal trabecular bone produces a moderate, spatially random gradient
  pattern
- A fracture line would appear as a continuous, linear high-gradient feature
  within the bone, typically oriented perpendicular to the long axis of the
  bone (for transverse fractures) or oblique (for oblique/spiral fractures)
- The radial head would show a characteristic circular gradient pattern if
  intact; a disruption in this circle would suggest a fracture

ARTIFACTS / MISLEADING EFFECTS:
- The gradient operator is extremely sensitive to noise. Any single-pixel
  noise spike will produce a spurious high-gradient response. A Gaussian
  pre-smoothing step (sigma=1 pixel) is essential before computing gradients.
- Trabecular bone patterns produce many moderate-gradient features that can
  mimic fracture lines. The key discriminator is continuity and orientation:
  a true fracture line is continuous and follows a mechanically plausible
  path.
- Superimposed anatomy creates gradient features at bone-bone interfaces
  that are not fractures. In a lateral elbow view, the radius and ulna
  overlap, creating a complex internal gradient pattern in the forearm
  region.
- The gradient at the cortex-air boundary is orders of magnitude higher than
  at a fracture line. Displaying the full dynamic range of the gradient map
  will show only cortical margins. The map should be thresholded or windowed
  to suppress the highest gradient values (cortical margins) and reveal the
  moderate-gradient internal features.

COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL:
The gradient map is a processing aid. Any linear feature flagged on the
gradient map must be verified on the original bone-windowed image. A fracture
line visible on the gradient map but invisible on all conventional views is
suspicious for a processing artifact. Conversely, a fracture line visible on
the original but NOT on the gradient map suggests the line is very subtle and
may warrant orthogonal imaging (AP view) for confirmation.

PROPOSAL 6 — CROP, REORIENT, AND SPATIAL DOWNSCALING OF CONTENT REGION
================================================================================

QUESTION:
Can the effective resolution and display size of the anatomical content be
improved by removing the wasted background space?

WHY THIS METHOD:
Approximately 45% of the image width (columns 0-720) is pure black
background with no anatomical content. The elbow and forearm occupy
approximately columns 720-1600 (width ~880 pixels) and rows 85-1280
(height ~1195 pixels). Cropping to this bounding box eliminates the wasted
space. Additionally, the current orientation (humerus extending right,
forearm extending down) is a rotated "L" shape. Rotating the cropped image
by approximately -45 degrees (clockwise) would reorient the limb along a
more natural vertical axis, making it easier to compare with standard
reference images and to trace longitudinal features along the bone axes.

After cropping and rotation, the image can be upscaled (e.g., 2x bilinear or
lanczos interpolation) to make the anatomy larger and easier to examine.
While upscaling does not add true information, it improves the display
experience by reducing the need for manual zooming, which can introduce
aliasing on standard viewers.

WHAT TO EXPECT:
- A compact image containing only the anatomical content, with the limb
  oriented vertically
- Improved effective pixel density on the anatomy (from ~880px wide to the
  full display width)
- Easier comparison with reference lateral elbow X-rays from textbooks or
  databases
- Better manual measurement of anatomical parameters (joint space width,
  bone alignment)

ARTIFACTS / MISLEADING EFFECTS:
- Rotation introduces interpolation artifacts. Nearest-neighbor preserves
  pixel values but creates stair-stepped edges. Bilinear and lanczos produce
  smoother edges but introduce slight blurring and value mixing between
  adjacent pixels.
- Upscaling (interpolation) does not add new information and can create a
  false impression of detail. Lanczos interpolation can introduce ringing
  artifacts near sharp edges.
- The right edge of the original image may truncate part of the proximal
  humerus. This cropping artifact will remain after reorientation.

COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL:
Always retain a link between coordinates on the reoriented image and the
original. Any finding on the reoriented image should be verifiable on the
original by mapping coordinates back through the inverse transformation.

PROPOSAL 7 — LOCAL PIXEL STATISTICS HEATMAP FOR DENSITY ABNORMALITIES
================================================================================

QUESTION:
Are there focal regions of abnormal bone density (sclerosis or lucency) in
the distal humerus, proximal radius, or proximal ulna that could indicate a
bone lesion (tumor, infection, metabolic disease)?

WHY THIS METHOD:
Diffuse changes in bone density (osteopenia, osteosclerosis) and focal bone
lesions (osteolytic or osteoblastic tumors, Brodie's abscess) can be subtle
and easily missed on visual inspection, especially with the limited 8-bit
dynamic range of this image. A local statistics heatmap computes the mean
and standard deviation of pixel intensity within a sliding window (e.g.,
16x16 or 32x32 pixels) across the bone regions. Regions where the mean
deviates significantly from the expected local bone density (based on
surrounding bone) are flagged. This makes both focal lytic lesions (local
darkening) and sclerotic lesions (local brightening) quantitatively visible.

The analysis should be restricted to the bone regions (identified by
thresholding pixels >120) to avoid computing statistics over background or
soft tissue, which would be meaningless.

WHAT TO EXPECT:
- A color-coded overlay on the bone regions showing local mean intensity
  deviations
- Normal cortical bone appears uniformly bright (high mean, low std dev)
- Normal trabecular bone shows moderate mean with variable std dev (the
  trabecular pattern creates natural variance)
- A focal osteolytic lesion would appear as a localized region of reduced
  mean intensity within bone
- A focal sclerotic lesion would appear as a localized region of increased
  mean intensity
- Diffuse osteopenia would show a general reduction in mean intensity across
  the trabecular regions compared to expected values

ARTIFACTS / MISLEADING EFFECTS:
- The sliding window size is critical. Too small, and normal trabecular
  variation is flagged as abnormal. Too large, and small lesions are
  averaged out. A window of 16x16 to 32x32 pixels is recommended for this
  image scale.
- Projectional superimposition of normal anatomy (e.g., the radial head
  overlapping the proximal ulna) creates apparent density changes that are
  purely geometric.
- Beam hardening and scatter — inherent to X-ray imaging — create density
  gradients that are not pathological but may be flagged by the analysis.
- The 8-bit quantization creates "banding" in the density map: instead of a
  smooth gradient, the heatmap will show step changes corresponding to the
  256 available intensity levels.

COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL:
The heatmap is an adjunct to visual inspection. Any flagged region should be
examined on the original image with the bone-windowed view. A finding is only
credible if the density abnormality is visible both on the heatmap and on
direct visual inspection of the original.

PROPOSAL 8 — FOURIER SPECTRUM ANALYSIS FOR ARTIFACT AND ORIENTATION
CHARACTERIZATION
================================================================================

QUESTION:
Is the image free of periodic artifacts (grid lines, moire patterns, scanning
artifacts)? What is the dominant spatial orientation of the anatomical
content, and does this help confirm the image orientation?

WHY THIS METHOD:
The 2D Fourier transform converts the image from spatial domain to frequency
domain. The magnitude spectrum reveals:
  (a) Periodic artifacts: Grid lines from the anti-scatter grid produce
      characteristic cross-shaped patterns in the frequency domain. Moire
      patterns from digitization produce regular frequency spikes.
  (b) Dominant orientation: The elongation of the spectrum perpendicular to
      the dominant spatial structures reveals the main orientation. For a
      lateral elbow with the humerus horizontal and forearm vertical, the
      spectrum should show elongation in both horizontal and vertical
      directions, confirming the expected anatomy and orientation.

The FFT should be computed on the cropped content region (after Proposal 6)
to avoid the background from dominating the spectrum. A 2D Hamming window
should be applied before the FFT to reduce edge discontinuity artifacts.

WHAT TO EXPECT:
- No periodic spike patterns if the image is free of grid/moire artifacts
- Dominant frequency components aligned with the long axes of the humerus
  and forearm, confirming the expected L-shaped anatomy
- The frequency content distribution will show the image is rich in
  low-frequency content (overall shape) with moderate mid-frequency content
  (trabecular pattern, cortical thickness) and low high-frequency content
  (fine detail, noise)
- Any unexpected frequency spikes would indicate artifacts worth
  investigating

ARTIFACTS / MISLEADING EFFECTS:
- The Fourier transform is global: it cannot localize where a periodic
  artifact is coming from, only that it exists somewhere in the image.
- Edge discontinuities from cropping produce strong low-frequency components
  that can obscure the spectrum. Windowing (Hamming or Blackman) is essential.
- The interpretation of the spectrum requires expertise in frequency-domain
  analysis and is not directly diagnostic.

COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL:
The Fourier spectrum is a quality-assurance check, not a diagnostic tool.
If artifacts are detected, they should be visually confirmed on the original
image. The orientation confirmation from the spectrum should agree with the
visual assessment of the anatomical layout.

================================================================================
SUMMARY OF PROPOSED ANALYSES
================================================================================

The following 8 analyses are proposed, ordered by expected diagnostic value:

  1. Bone-window re-mapping          — cortical detail, occult fractures
  2. Soft-tissue window              — fat pad signs, swelling
  3. CLAHE local contrast            — radial head, articular surfaces
  4. Line profiles along cortex      — quantitative cortical continuity
  5. Gradient magnitude map          — fracture line detection
  6. Crop + reorient + upscale       — improved display and measurement
  7. Local density heatmap           — bone lesions, density abnormalities
  8. Fourier spectrum                — artifact screening, orientation check

Proposals 1, 2, 3, and 6 are foundational contrast/display improvements.
Proposals 4, 5, and 7 are targeted diagnostic aids. Proposal 8 is a
quality-assurance check.

All processed views should be displayed alongside the original image, and no
finding on a processed view should be considered credible until confirmed on
the original.
