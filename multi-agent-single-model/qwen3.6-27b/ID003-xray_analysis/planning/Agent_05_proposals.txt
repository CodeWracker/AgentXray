AGENT_05 — IMAGE-PROCESSING PROPOSALS
======================================
(Independent proposals — not influenced by other agents' plans)

Based on my first-pass assessment of a subacute both-bone mid-forearm fracture
with early callus formation in a skeletally immature patient, I propose the
following six targeted image-processing analyses.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROPOSAL 1: HARRIS CORNER / EDGES-ENHANCED PERIosteal REACTION MAP
================================================================================

QUESTION:
  What is the precise longitudinal extent of the periosteal new bone formation
  along both the radius and ulna? Can we measure how far the periosteal reaction
  extends from the fracture line in millimeters, and is it symmetric between the
  two bones?

METHOD:
  1. Segment the fracture zone region of interest (ROI) — approximately the
     middle third of the forearm — using adaptive thresholding to isolate the
     high-density cortical bone.
  2. Apply a Canny edge detector (low threshold ~50, high threshold ~150) or
     Sobel gradient operator to enhance linear structures.
  3. Apply a morphological dilation followed by skeletonization to thin the
     detected edges to 1-pixel-wide lines, making the periosteal new bone
     margins measurable.
  4. Use a directional filter (e.g., Gabor filter bank tuned to vertical
     orientations) to preferentially enhance structures running longitudinally
     along the bone shafts — this captures the periosteal reaction while
     suppressing transverse trabecular patterns.
  5. Measure the continuous length of enhanced signal extending from the fracture
     line proximally and distally on both bones.

WHY:
  The periosteal reaction is relatively faint on the original image, blending
  with the normal cortical margin. Directional filtering exploits the fact that
  periosteal new bone forms parallel to the bone shaft, making it distinct from
  the transverse fracture line. This separation is key to measuring the
  longitudinal extent accurately.

EXPECTED RESULT:
  A processed image in which the periosteal reaction appears as enhanced linear
  signal running parallel to the cortex, clearly separable from the main
  cortical boundary. This will allow measurement of:
  - Proximal and distal extent of periosteal reaction on each bone
  - Asymmetry between radius and ulna
  - Whether the reaction is continuous or interrupted (discontinuity may
    indicate a different process superimposed on the fracture)

ARTIFACT WARNING:
  - Gabor filtering may amplify noise in regions with low signal, creating
    false linear enhancements. Noise should be suppressed with a mild Gaussian
    pre-filter (sigma ~1.0) before applying Gabor.
  - Overlap between the radius and ulna in this projection may cause the edge
    detector to conflate the two bones' periosteal reactions.
  - The Gabor filter bank will also enhance the normal cortical margin itself;
    post-processing must subtract or mask the primary cortex before measuring
    the periosteal overlay.

COMPARISON:
  Side-by-side with the original image, with the processed periosteal map
  overlaid as a semi-transparent colored layer (e.g., warm red) on the
  grayscale original. Regions where the enhanced signal extends beyond the
  visible cortex on the original will confirm true periosteal reaction.

PROPOSAL 2: CALLUS DENSITY GRADIENT ANALYSIS
================================================================================

QUESTION:
  What is the density profile of the callus relative to mature cortical bone
  and normal medullary bone? Does the density gradient suggest early
  fibrocartilaginous callus (lower density) versus more mature woven bone
  (higher density)?

METHOD:
  1. Define three regions of interest along the radius (and separately for the
     ulna):
     a. Reference cortical bone — proximal diaphysis, normal cortex, ~20mm
        segment
     b. Reference medullary bone — same proximal segment, central medullary
        cavity
     c. Callus zone — 15mm proximal to the fracture line, 15mm distal,
        encompassing the periosteal and endosteal reaction
  2. Extract the mean, median, and standard deviation of pixel intensity
     (HU-equivalent if calibrated; otherwise raw grayscale values) for each
     ROI.
  3. Compute the callus-to-cortex ratio (CCR) and callus-to-medulla ratio
     (CMR).
  4. Generate a cross-sectional density profile: for each row of pixels crossing
     the callus zone, plot the intensity gradient from cortex, through callus,
     to contralateral cortex.
  5. Apply a 1D Gaussian kernel (sigma ~2 pixels) along each profile to smooth
     acquisition noise while preserving the density transition zones.

WHY:
  The degree of mineralization in the callus correlates with healing stage.
  Early callus (fibrocartilaginous, 1-2 weeks) is relatively radiolucent,
  while woven bone callus (2-4 weeks) is more radiopaque. By quantifying
  the density ratio, we can narrow the estimated age of the fracture
  beyond subjective visual assessment.

EXPECTED RESULT:
  - A set of numeric ratios (CCR, CMR) that place the callus on a density
    spectrum between soft tissue (very low density) and mature cortex
    (highest density).
  - If CCR < 0.5: early fibrocartilaginous callus (likely 1-2 weeks)
  - If CCR 0.5-0.8: transitioning to woven bone (likely 2-3 weeks)
  - If CCR > 0.8: mature woven or lamellar bone (likely 3-4+ weeks)
  - Cross-sectional profiles showing the density transition at the
    fracture-cortex interface.

ARTIFACT WARNING:
  - Beam hardening from the overlapping radius and ulna will artificially
    elevate densities in the callus zone, potentially overstating the CCR.
    This is a significant confounder that must be noted.
  - The image may not be calibrated to Hounsfield units; raw grayscale
    values are relative only. The ratios are meaningful within this single
    image but cannot be compared across different scanners or exposure
    settings.
  - Partial volume averaging at the cortical edge will blur the cortex
    intensity measurement. Use a central sub-ROI within the cortex to
    mitigate.

COMPARISON:
  Display the three ROIs as colored overlays on the original image, with the
  computed mean densities annotated. Show the cross-sectional density profiles
  as line plots adjacent to the image, with arrows pointing to the
  corresponding anatomical location.

PROPOSAL 3: LOCAL ADAPTIVE CONTRAST ENHANCEMENT (CLAHE) ON FRACTURE ZONE
================================================================================

QUESTION:
  Are there additional fracture fragments, subtle comminution, or butterfly
  fragments that are invisible at the current exposure and contrast level?

METHOD:
  1. Crop a generous ROI around the fracture zone — extending ~40mm proximally
     and distally, and encompassing the full width of both bones plus a
     20mm soft tissue margin on each side.
  2. Apply Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) with:
     - Clip limit: 2.0 (to limit noise amplification)
     - Tile grid size: 8x8 (to capture local rather than global contrast)
  3. Before applying CLAHE, run a mild anisotropic diffusion filter (2-3
     iterations, kappa ~30) to reduce quantum mottle while preserving edges.
  4. Compare the enhanced image with the original crop for new visible
     structures.

WHY:
  The fracture zone contains the most clinically relevant detail, yet the
  global exposure is optimized for the entire image field. CLAHE redistributes
  the local histogram to maximize contrast in the fracture region without
  amplifying contrast in already-well-exposed areas. The pre-filtering reduces
  the quantum mottle that would otherwise be amplified by CLAHE into
  misleading structures.

EXPECTED RESULT:
  - Sharper visualization of the fracture line morphology on both bones
  - Potential revelation of small butterfly fragments or comminution not
    visible on the original
  - Better definition of the endosteal margin reaction
  - Clearer delineation of the fracture gap width, allowing measurement of
    displacement in millimeters

ARTIFACT WARNING:
  - CLAHE can create false edges at the boundary between tiles (tile grid
    artifacts). An 8x8 tile grid on a ~200x400 pixel crop minimizes this,
    but it must be checked visually.
  - Over-enhancement may make normal trabecular pattern variations appear
    as fracture lines. Any newly visible "fracture" must be confirmed
    against the original image.
  - Anisotropic diffusion may smooth very fine fracture lines if
    over-applied. Limit to 2-3 iterations.

COMPARISON:
  Split-view display: original crop on the left, CLAHE-enhanced crop on the
  right. Any new finding must be visible (at least faintly) on the original;
  the enhanced image should only improve conspicuity, not invent structure.

PROPOSAL 4: PHYSIS (GROWTH PLATE) DETAIL OPTIMIZATION VIA SHARPENING
================================================================================

QUESTION:
  Are the distal radial and ulnar physes normal? Is there any physeal
  widening, irregularity, or Salter-Harris injury involving the distal
  growth plates?

METHOD:
  1. Crop two separate ROIs: one around the distal radial physis, one around
     the distal ulnar physis. Each ROI should include the distal metaphysis
     (~20mm), the physis, and the epiphysis (~15mm).
  2. Apply unsharp masking with:
     - Gaussian blur kernel: sigma ~0.5 pixels (very tight, for fine detail)
     - Mask amount: 1.5 (moderate sharpening)
  3. Apply a bandpass filter (Gaussian difference of Gaussians, DoG) tuned
     to the spatial frequency of the physis — estimated at 0.1-0.3 cycles/pixel
     based on the visible width of the growth plate.
     - Large Gaussian: sigma ~8 pixels
     - Small Gaussian: sigma ~2 pixels
     - Subtract small from large, then add the result back to the original
       at reduced weight (0.3-0.5).
  4. Measure the physis width at the center and at the margins. Compare
     the width to the expected range for the estimated skeletal age.

WHY:
  The physes are critical structures in pediatric trauma. Salter-Harris
  injuries can be subtle and missed on unprocessed images. Sharpening and
  bandpass filtering enhance the thin radiolucent line of the growth plate
  and the adjacent ossification front, making irregularity or widening
  more conspicuous.

EXPECTED RESULT:
  - Clearer visualization of the physis width and contour
  - Measurement of physeal width (normal distal radial physis in a child
    is typically 1-3mm, varying with age)
  - Detection of any asymmetric widening, irregular metaphyseal margin
    (corner fracture), or epiphyseal separation
  - Confirmation that the physes are indeed open (skeletally immature)

ARTIFACT WARNING:
  - Unsharp masking amplifies noise, especially at the physis-metaphysis
    transition where there is already a natural density gradient. Ringing
    artifacts may appear as false "fracture lines" at sharp transitions.
  - The DoG filter may suppress legitimate broad-scale findings (e.g.,
    metaphyseal lucency from pathology) by design, since it is tuned to
    narrow spatial frequencies.
  - Hand positioning and superimposition of carpal bones may limit the
    quality of the distal ROI extraction.

COMPARISON:
  Overlay the sharpened physeal margin as a thin colored contour on the
  original image. The measured width should be annotated. Any finding of
  abnormality must be traceable to a visible feature on the original —
  the processing aids detection but does not replace visual confirmation.

PROPOSAL 5: SOFT TISSUE SWELLING MAP VIA LOW-FREQUENCY EXTRACTION
================================================================================

QUESTION:
  What is the extent and severity of soft tissue swelling around the fracture
  zone? Is there evidence of compartment swelling, and is the swelling
  symmetric between volar and dorsal aspects?

METHOD:
  1. Extract the low-frequency component of the image using a Gaussian
     low-pass filter (sigma ~15 pixels). This removes bone detail and
     retains broad soft tissue density variations.
  2. Subtract the low-frequency component from the original to isolate
     high-frequency content (bone detail), then invert the process:
     the low-frequency image alone represents soft tissue bulk and
     broad density changes.
  3. Define a soft tissue boundary mask: threshold the original image to
     segment the limb silhouette (patient vs. background), then erode the
     mask by ~5mm to exclude the cortical bone.
  4. Within the soft tissue mask, compute the cross-sectional width of
     soft tissue at regular intervals along the forearm (every 10mm).
  5. Compare the soft tissue width at the fracture zone to the proximal
     and distal reference segments.

WHY:
  Soft tissue swelling is a critical clinical finding that correlates with
  injury severity, risk of compartment syndrome, and timing. The standard
  bone window used for X-rays compresses soft tissue contrast. Low-frequency
  extraction removes the dominant bone signal, allowing the subtler soft
  tissue density gradients to become visible.

EXPECTED RESULT:
  - A soft tissue width profile along the forearm, showing a peak at the
    fracture zone and baseline values proximally and distally
  - Quantification of swelling as a percentage increase over baseline
    (e.g., "swelling is 45% greater than proximal reference")
  - Assessment of whether swelling is circumferential or asymmetric
    (asymmetric swelling may suggest a more focal injury)
  - Detection of any abnormal lucency within the soft tissue (gas, which
    would appear as very low-density areas)

ARTIFACT WARNING:
  - The Gaussian low-pass filter with sigma=15 will blur the soft tissue
    boundary, making the limb silhouette imprecise. The thresholding for
    the mask should use the original image, not the filtered version.
  - Patient positioning (hand rotation, elbow flexion) creates natural
    variations in apparent soft tissue width that are unrelated to
    swelling. The width measurement must be interpreted in context of
    the projection.
  - If a splint, cast, or bandage is present, it will confound the soft
    tissue measurement. Check the original image for any radiopaque
    external material.
  - The low-frequency image may also contain beam-hardening gradients
    that mimic soft tissue density changes.

COMPARISON:
  Display the low-frequency soft tissue map as a grayscale image alongside
  the original, with the measured width profile overlaid as a colored line
  tracing the soft tissue boundary. Annotate the peak swelling location and
  percentage increase over baseline.

PROPOSAL 6: BONE MINERAL DENSITY COMPARISON — FRACTURE ZONE VS CONTRALATERAL
    NORMAL SEGMENTS
================================================================================

QUESTION:
  Is there regional bone loss (osteopenia) around the fracture zone that
  would suggest a pathological fracture rather than simple trauma? Or is
  the bone density normal, supporting a traumatic etiology?

METHOD:
  1. Define four pairs of matched regions of interest on the radius (repeat
     for ulna):
     a. Zone immediately proximal to fracture (~10mm) vs. same distance
        from a reference point in the distal diaphysis (far from injury)
     b. Zone immediately distal to fracture (~10mm) vs. matched distal
        reference
     c. Mid-cortex intensity at the fracture level vs. proximal reference
     d. Trabecular pattern density in the metaphysis proximal to the
        fracture vs. the metaphysis distal to the fracture
  2. For each ROI pair, compute the mean intensity and coefficient of
     variation (CV = std/mean). A higher CV in the trabecular regions
     suggests coarser trabecular pattern (lower bone density).
  3. Apply a texture analysis using the Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix
     (GLCM) on the trabecular bone regions. Compute contrast, entropy,
     and homogeneity metrics. Compare the GLCM metrics between the
     fracture-adjacent zone and the reference zone.
  4. Generate a bone mineral ratio: (fracture-zone density) /
     (reference density), normalized to account for beam attenuation
     differences along the path.

WHY:
  A pathological fracture (from bone tumor, osteogenesis imperfecta,
  infection, or osteoporosis) would show reduced bone density around the
  fracture compared to normal segments. In a pediatric patient, localized
     osteopenia near the fracture is also a recognized phenomenon after
     immobilization (disuse osteopenia), but this would not be present at
     initial injury. If the bone density is normal throughout, it supports
     a simple traumatic etiology in a normal bone.

EXPECTED RESULT:
  - Bone mineral ratio close to 1.0: normal density, traumatic fracture in
    healthy bone
  - Ratio significantly below 1.0: osteopenia, raising suspicion for
    pathological process or prior immobilization
  - GLCM entropy: higher entropy suggests coarser, less organized trabecular
    pattern (demineralization). Normal bone has lower entropy with more
    homogeneous trabecular architecture.
  - If density is asymmetric (reduced on one side only), this may indicate
    a focal lesion.

ARTIFACT WARNING:
  - Beam attenuation differs between proximal and distal segments due to
    varying tissue thickness and bone overlap. The normalization step
    is critical but imperfect.
  - The fracture zone itself contains callus, which artificially increases
    the local density. ROIs must exclude the callus zone and sample only
    the intact bone adjacent to the fracture.
  - GLCM analysis requires sufficient sampling area; small ROIs may give
    unreliable texture metrics. Use at least 30x30 pixel windows.
  - Patient age affects normal bone density and trabecular pattern;
    reference values should be age-appropriate.

COMPARISON:
  Show the matched ROI pairs on the original image with colored overlays,
  annotated with their mean intensities and computed ratios. Display the
  GLCM metrics as a small bar chart comparing fracture-adjacent vs.
  reference regions. Any density abnormality must be visually confirmed
  on the original image.

SUMMARY OF PROPOSED ANALYSES
================================================================================

  # | Analysis                           | Target Finding
  --+------------------------------------+----------------------------
  1 | Periosteal reaction map            | Longitudinal extent, symmetry
  2 | Callus density gradient            | Healing stage estimation
  3 | CLAHE on fracture zone             | Hidden comminution / fragments
  4 | Physis sharpening                  | Growth plate integrity
  5 | Soft tissue swelling map           | Swelling severity, compartment risk
  6 | Bone density comparison            | Traumatic vs pathological etiology

Priority order: Proposals 2 and 3 (callus density and fracture zone detail)
are highest priority, as they directly inform the clinical question of
healing stage and fracture morphology. Proposals 1 and 4 are secondary
(periosteal extent and growth plate detail). Proposals 5 and 6 are
tertiary (soft tissue and bone etiology assessment).

All proposals include artifact warnings and comparison strategies to ensure
that processing artifacts are not mistaken for clinical findings. Every
processed result must be cross-referenced with the original image before
being considered valid.
