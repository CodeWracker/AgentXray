PREAMBLE
--------
This image is a lateral radiograph of the left ankle/hindfoot saved as a
PNG, not a DICOM. That constraint matters: we cannot apply true
window/level adjustment because the 8-bit raster has already compressed
the original 12- or 16-bit radiographic data. Every processing technique
proposed below works within that limitation. I am not proposing a
comprehensive menu of filters. I am proposing the specific analyses I
believe will answer specific unresolved questions about this study.

I am particularly focused on three concerns:
  1. Whether the calcaneus has a subtle occult fracture.
  2. Whether the talar dome harbors an osteochondral defect.
  3. Whether the posterior soft tissue envelope shows occult swelling

PROPOSAL 1 — Böhler's Angle AND Gissane's Angle Measurement
============================================================

(a) QUESTION:
    Is the calcaneus structurally intact, or is there an occult
    intra-articular fracture causing subtle collapse?

    On visual inspection, the calcaneal silhouette appears normal.
    However, Böhler's angle is the single most sensitive plain-film
    indicator of calcaneal fracture. A normal calcaneus has a Böhler's
    angle of 20–40°. Even subtle depression of the posterior facet from
    a nondisplaced fracture can reduce this angle by 5–10° before any
    cortical disruption becomes visible to the naked eye. Gissane's
    angle (120–145° normal) provides a second independent measure of
    anterior calcaneal integrity.

(b) WHY THIS METHOD HELPS:
    Angles are objective. Where the eye struggles to distinguish
    between a normal variant and early collapse in trabecular bone,
    the geometry of the calcaneus tells the story. Böhler's angle
    is constructed from the highest point of the posterior facet,
    the apex of the anterior process, and the highest point of the
    posterior tuberosity. Any fracture depressing the posterior facet
    reduces this angle. No filter or enhancement can do what a
    simple measurement does here.

(c) WHAT I EXPECT TO FIND:
    Given my first-pass impression that the calcaneus looks normal,
    I expect Böhler's angle to be within 25–35° and Gissane's angle
    to be approximately 130°. If either falls outside range, it
    immediately raises a red flag for occult fracture even if no
    cortical step-off is visible.

(d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS:
    - Lateral positioning must be a true lateral for these angles to
      be valid. If the foot is rotated, the calcaneal outline is
      distorted and the angle will be inaccurate. In this image, the
      tibia/fibula overlap suggests acceptable true lateral
      positioning, but I should verify this before trusting the
      measurement.
    - Landmark identification is subjective: the "highest point" of
      the posterior facet could vary by a few pixels between
      observers, translating to 2–4° of inter-observer variability.
    - The calcaneal cortex has variable thickness; selecting the
      inner vs. outer cortex changes the angle.

(e) HOW TO COMPARE WITH ORIGINAL:
    The measurement should be performed on the original, unprocessed
    image with no enhancement applied, to avoid introducing edge
    artifacts that could shift landmark identification. I recommend
    marking the three landmark points on a copy of the original image
    so the measurement can be audited visually. The angle itself is
    a number that needs no "comparison" — it is compared to the known
    normal range.

    CRUCIAL CHECK: Measure the overlap of the distal tibial and
    fibular malleoli. If they are not superimposed within a few
    millimeters, this is not a true lateral and the angles are not
    reliable.

PROPOSAL 2 — Targeted Crop + Inverse Negative of the Talar Dome
================================================================

(a) QUESTION:
    Is the inferior surface of the talar dome smooth and continuous,
    or is there a subtle osteochondral defect (OCD) or subchondral
    cyst?

    The talar dome is the weight-bearing surface of the ankle joint.
    Osteochondral lesions here are common, often occult on plain
    radiography, and notoriously difficult to detect because the
    dome's curvature blends into the joint space. In my first-pass,
    I flagged this region as warranting closer attention.

(b) WHY THIS METHOD HELPS:
    Two complementary steps:

    1. CROPPING to the talar dome region (approximately a 120×150
       pixel window centered on the talar body and inferior dome)
       allows display at higher magnification than the full image.
       The original image is modest in resolution; displaying the
       full image means we are looking at structures that are only
       5–10 pixels across. Cropping and upsampling (even bilinearly)
       makes each pixel more visible for careful inspection.

    2. INVERTING the grayscale (negative) in the cropped region
       reverses the familiar radiographic polarity: bone becomes
       dark on a light background. This is not cosmetic — the human
       visual system is sensitive to different types of contrast
       reversals. A subtle hypodense area (sclerotic rim around an
       OCD, or a small lucent subchondral cyst) that "blends in" on
       a dark-field radiograph may become more salient on a
       light-field inverted version. Radiologists routinely switch
       polarity in PACS for this reason. I want to replicate that
       experience.

(c) WHAT I EXPECT TO FIND:
    I expect the talar dome surface to be smooth with a uniform
    cortical margin. If there is an OCD, I would look for:
    - A small focal lucency in the subchondral bone (the "cyst"
      appearance of a chronic OCD)
    - A sclerotic rim surrounding that lucency
    - Minor surface irregularity of the dome contour

    If the inverted view reveals nothing the original did not, I will
    note that the talar dome appears truly normal by both polarities.

(d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS:
    - Cropping and upsampling does not create new information; it
      only redistributes existing pixels. Upsampling can create a
      false sense of resolution. I must remember that a smooth-
      appearing dome after upsampling could still have a defect
      smaller than the pixel resolution.
    - Inversion makes the dark background (which was uniform black)
      into uniform white. Any noise, compression artifacts, or
      JPEG artifacts in the original become more visible on white
      background and could be mistaken for pathology. I need to
      distinguish image artifacts from anatomical findings.
    - The joint space, which is naturally lucent (dark) on the
      original, becomes bright white after inversion. This could
      create a "halo" effect that obscures the dome margin.

(e) HOW TO COMPARE WITH ORIGINAL:
    I will display the cropped, inverted region side-by-side with the
    same crop from the original image. Any finding should be present
    in both views. The inverted view is a hypothesis generator; the
    original is the adjudicator. If I see something on the inverted
    image that is not confirmed on the original, I will flag it as
    "possibly artifact" and request further scrutiny, not dismiss it
    outright.

PROPOSAL 3 — High-Pass Filter (Unsharp Mask) on the Distal Tibial
                  Metaphysis
============================================================

(a) QUESTION:
    Is there an occult stress fracture or subtle spiral fracture in
    the distal tibial shaft, approximately 5–10 cm proximal to the
    tibial plafond?

    The distal tibia is subject to both traumatic fractures (supination-
    external rotation injuries) and stress fractures (march fractures
    in runners and military recruits). A nondisplaced stress fracture
    may present as a faint transverse lucency or a subtle area of
    periosteal thickening that is essentially invisible in the normal
    trabecular texture. On my first-pass, I saw no periosteal reaction
    and no obvious fracture line, but the trabecular pattern can
    mask fine linear lucencies.

(b) WHY THIS METHOD HELPS:
    Unsharp masking (USM) is a high-pass filter: it subtracts a
    blurred version of the image from the original, leaving behind
    only the high-frequency content — edges and fine detail. In
    cortical bone, fracture lines are high-frequency features: a
    sharp transition from one density to another. Trabecular bone,
    by contrast, is lower-frequency and somewhat uniform in texture.
    USM suppresses the low-frequency trabecular "noise" and amplifies
    any sharp linear structures, including fracture lines, cortical
    defects, and periosteal reactions.

    This is different from edge detection (which creates a binary or
    pseudo-binary edge map). Unsharp masking preserves the image as a
    radiograph — it just makes the high-frequency features more
    prominent. A reader can still see the bone as bone, not as a
    wireframe.

(c) WHAT I EXPECT TO FIND:
    If there is a stress fracture, I expect to see a fine linear
    lucency in the cortex of the distal tibial shaft, typically
    transverse to the long axis of the bone, about 5–10 cm proximal
    to the plafond. Accompanying findings might include subtle
    periosteal new bone formation (a thin white line parallel to the
    cortex, on the outer surface).

    More likely, given the visual impression of normal trabecular
    pattern, I expect the USM to confirm the absence of such findings.
    That confirmation is itself valuable — it gives me more confidence
    in saying "no fracture in the tibial shaft."

(d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS:
    - Ringing: Unsharp masking can create dark halos (ringing
      artifacts) around sharp edges, especially cortical margins.
      These halos could be mistaken for fracture lines. I will
      look specifically for this pattern: a halo that is perfectly
      parallel to and immediately adjacent to the cortex is likely
      ringing, not a fracture. A fracture line would typically
      traverse the cortex or extend into the medullary cavity.
    - Amplification of compression artifacts: The original PNG may
      have JPEG-like compression blocks. USM will amplify any block
      edges, creating a grid-like pattern that has nothing to do
      with anatomy.
    - Parameter sensitivity: The result depends heavily on the
      Gaussian blur kernel size and the USM strength multiplier.
      Too aggressive = overwhelming noise. Too mild = no benefit.
      I recommend testing at least two settings (e.g., sigma=2 and
      sigma=5) and not trusting either in isolation.

(e) HOW TO COMPARE WITH ORIGINAL:
    The USM image should be viewed in direct comparison with the
    original, unprocessed crop of the distal tibia. A finding is
    valid only if the same linear feature is visible (even if faint)
    on the original. The USM's role is to draw attention to regions
    that then need to be verified on the original. I will annotate
    the original image with arrows at any locations where the USM
    suggests a possible finding, so the council can independently
    evaluate them.

PROPOSAL 4 — Soft Tissue Thickness Measurement at Two Standard
                  Points
============================================================

(a) QUESTION:
    Is there occult soft tissue swelling around the ankle and hindfoot
    that would suggest acute injury, even in the absence of visible
    fracture?

    Soft tissue swelling is often the earliest sign of injury on
    radiography — it can precede any visible fracture by hours to
    days. In ankle fractures, posterior soft tissue swelling over
    the calcaneus and anterior swelling over the distal tibia are
    common early signs. In my first-pass, I assessed the soft tissues
    qualitatively ("appears within normal limits"), but a qualitative
    assessment is vulnerable to the observer's subjective sense of
    "normal." A measurement is not.

(b) WHY THIS METHOD HELPS:
    I propose measuring soft tissue thickness at two specific points
    using a standardized approach:

    Point A — Posterior calcaneal soft tissue (Achilles region):
      Measure the perpendicular distance from the posterior cortex
      of the calcaneal tuberosity to the posterior skin line at the
      level of the Achilles tendon insertion (approximately the
      junction of the middle and distal thirds of the calcaneal
      height).

    Point B — Dorsal distal tibial soft tissue:
      Measure the perpendicular distance from the anterior cortex of
      the distal tibia (at the level of the plafond) to the dorsal
      skin line.

    These measurements are not diagnostic in isolation. A thick heel
    pad is normal in some body habituses. But they establish an
    objective baseline that can be compared to published norms:

    - Posterior calcaneal soft tissue thickness: typically 15–25 mm
      in adults (depends on body habitus, shoe wear, BMI).
    - Anterior distal tibial soft tissue: typically 10–20 mm.

    More importantly, I want to compare Point A to Point B within
    the same image. The ratio of posterior to anterior thickness can
    indicate whether swelling is asymmetric (e.g., posterior swelling
    from Achilles injury or retrocalcaneal bursitis would make Point
    A disproportionately thick relative to Point B).

(c) WHAT I EXPECT TO FIND:
    I expect both measurements to fall within normal ranges, and
    their ratio to be approximately 1:1 to 1.5:1 (posterior slightly
    thicker is normal due to the heel fat pad). If the posterior
    measurement is >30 mm, or if the posterior/anterior ratio exceeds
    2:1, that would be suspicious for localized posterior swelling.

(d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS:
    - Patient positioning: If the foot is plantarflexed or
      dorsiflexed beyond neutral, the soft tissue envelope shifts
      and measured thicknesses change. In this image, the foot
      appears to be in approximately neutral position (the tibia and
      talus form roughly a right angle), which is acceptable.
    - External pressure: Shoe remnants, padding, or a positioning
      device could artificially increase soft tissue thickness. I
      should inspect the skin line for any evidence of external
      compression or added material.
    - Projectional magnification: The heel is closer to the
      detector in a lateral view than the anterior tibia, so the
      posterior measurement is less magnified than the anterior one.
      This actually works in our favor — it makes any posterior
      swelling measurement a conservative estimate.
    - Pixel calibration: These measurements are in pixels, not
      millimeters, unless the image has a calibration marker.
      Without a calibration marker, the measurements are useful for
      relative comparison (A vs. B) but not absolute comparison
      against published norms. I should note this limitation.

(e) HOW TO COMPARE WITH ORIGINAL:
    The measurements are made directly on the original image. I will
    draw the measurement lines on an annotated copy so the council
    can verify the landmarks. The key comparison is the ratio of
    Point A to Point B within this single image. If the image had
    a known calibration marker, I would also convert to millimeters
    and compare to published reference values.

    IMPORTANT: These are soft tissue measurements. They do not
    replace evaluation of the soft tissues visually. They supplement
    it by providing a numerical anchor for an otherwise subjective
    assessment.

PROPOSAL 5 — Crop + Magnification of the Anterior Calcaneal Process
===================================================================

(a) QUESTION:
    Is there a fracture of the anterior process of the calcaneus?

    Anterior process calcaneal fractures are notoriously subtle and
    frequently missed. They can result from direct trauma, avulsion
    by the bifurcate ligament, or as part of a lateral calcaneal
    wall fracture pattern. In a lateral view, the anterior process
    is a small bony projection that overlaps with the cuboid and
    calcaneocuboid joint. A nondisplaced fracture line here can be
    only a few pixels long and can blend into the trabecular pattern
    of the adjacent bone.

    On my first-pass, I flagged this region but could not resolve
    definitively whether the cortex is intact. The region is simply
    too small in the full-image view.

(b) WHY THIS METHOD HELPS:
    Straightforward cropping to the anterior calcaneal process and
    adjacent cuboid, displayed at 3x linear magnification, is the
    simplest and most direct approach. I do not propose any filter or
    enhancement here because:

    1. The limiting factor is resolution (how many pixels cover the
       region), not contrast. The region is adequately exposed; we
       just need more display pixels per anatomical pixel.

    2. Enhancement filters on a small crop risk amplifying
       compression artifacts, which could be confused with fracture
       lines. Simple magnification without filtering is the safest
       approach for a region where artifact risk outweighs contrast
       benefit.

    3. I can then do a careful cortical tracing exercise: mentally
       (or digitally) trace the cortical outline of the anterior
       process and look for any break in continuity, step-off, or
       abrupt change in curvature.

(c) WHAT I EXPECT TO FIND:
    I expect the anterior process to have a smooth cortical outline
    with no fracture line. The calcaneocuboid joint space should be
    visible as a clean lucent line. If there is a fracture, I would
    expect to see a thin lucent line extending from the articular
    surface of the anterior process into the calcaneal body, or a
    subtle cortical step-off at the ligament attachment site.

    Given the limitations of a single lateral view, even a clean
    appearance here does not exclude an anterior process fracture —
    some are only visible on an axial (short-axis) view of the
    calcaneus. But ruling in a fracture is achievable.

(d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS:
    - Bilinear interpolation at 3x magnification will smooth the
      image, potentially masking a very fine fracture line that
      spans only 1–2 pixels. I should also render a nearest-neighbor
      interpolation version, which preserves the original pixel
      values but gives a blocky appearance. A true fracture line
      should be visible in both interpolation modes.
    - Overlap of the cuboid cortex with the anterior calcaneal
      cortex can create the illusion of a fracture line where there
      is only a joint space. I need to follow the cortical line
      carefully and determine whether a "line" I see is a true
      fracture or the edge of a superimposed bone.

(e) HOW TO COMPARE WITH ORIGINAL:
    The magnified crop is simply a zoomed window into the original
    image. No processing is applied beyond cropping and upsampling.
    Any finding on the magnified view should be immediately locatable
    on the full original image. I will mark the corresponding
    location on the full image with a rectangle or arrow.

    Because no filter is applied, there is essentially no risk of
    the processing itself creating a false finding. The only risk is
    misinterpreting a real anatomical feature (joint space, trabecula)
    as a fracture — which is a reader error, not a processing error.

PROPOSALS I AM DELIBERATELY NOT PROPOSING
==========================================

I want to be explicit about what I considered and rejected, to
demonstrate that these proposals are curated, not a checklist.

1. CLAHE (Contrast-Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization):
   I considered this for the trabecular bone of the talus and
   calcaneus. However, CLAHE is a contrast manipulation that can
   create or enhance local structures that do not exist in the
   original data. In a region where we are looking for subtle
   density changes (stress fractures, osteonecrosis), CLAHE's
   tendency to amplify local contrast means it can make normal
   trabecular variation look pathological. The risk of false
   positives is too high for a structure I am already closely
   inspecting by other means. I will only use CLAHE if all other
   methods are inconclusive and a specific region remains in doubt.

2. Sobel/Canny edge detection:
   Edge detection creates a binary or pseudo-binary representation
   of the image. This loses the density information that is
   essential for distinguishing a fracture line from a normal
   trabecular septum or joint space margin. An edge detector would
   light up every cortical margin, every trabecula, and every joint
   space — and I would need to distinguish the fracture from the
   noise. Unsharp masking (Proposal 3) gives me the same high-
   frequency information but preserves the image as a radiograph,
   which is far more interpretable.

3. Deep learning / AI fracture detection:
   I do not have access to a validated AI model in this environment,
   and running an unvalidated model on a single image would produce
   a result I cannot interpret or trust. I would rather invest in
   methods whose behavior I understand and can explain.

4. Phase-contrast or spectral analysis:
   These require the original raw detector data or at minimum a
   DICOM file with full bit depth. A PNG cannot support these
   analyses.
