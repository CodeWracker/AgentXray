PROPOSAL 1: Adaptive Local Contrast Enhancement (CLAHE) on the
           Lateral Midfoot Region

(a) QUESTION:
    What is the true nature of the abnormality in the cuboid bone? Is the trabecular
    pattern genuinely disrupted, or does the abnormal appearance result from projection
    overlap and suboptimal local contrast? Can I distinguish a sclerotic rim (suggestive
    of a chronic cyst or benign lesion) from permeative/moth-eaten margins (suggestive of
    an aggressive process such as infection or malignancy)?

(b) WHY THIS METHOD:
    The lateral foot X-ray has a very wide dynamic range: dense cortical bone in the
    distal tibia is far brighter than the relatively radiolucent cuboid. Global contrast
    stretching either blows out the dense regions or leaves the midfoot underexposed.
    Contrast-Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) operates on small tiles
    (patches) independently, boosting local contrast without saturating any single region.
    It is specifically designed for medical imagery where local structures must be
    discerned against a heterogeneous background. By focusing a CLAHE tile grid on the
    cuboid zone, I expect the trabecular microarchitecture and lesion borders to become
    visually resolvable.

(c) EXPECTED FINDINGS:
    - If the cuboid abnormality is a fracture, I expect to see sharp linear lucent lines
      traversing the bone with disrupted trabecular continuity on one side vs. the other.
    - If it is a lytic lesion, I expect a focal low-density region with either a thin
      sclerotic rim (benign/chronic) or irregular permeative margins (aggressive).
    - If the abnormality is a projection artifact, enhanced local contrast should reveal
      an otherwise-normal trabecular pattern once the overlapping structures are mentally
      separated by the improved detail.

(d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS:
    - CLAHE amplifies noise in low-signal regions. The darker background areas adjacent
      to the foot will show visible noise patterns that could be mistaken for bone
      structures. Mitigation: mask to the foot silhouette before applying CLAHE.
    - Tile boundary artifacts: if the tile size is too small, artificial brightness
      transitions appear at tile edges. Mitigation: use a tile size of at least
      64x64 pixels and a clip limit of 2.0-4.0.
    - Over-enhancement can create "halo" effects around dense cortical margins that
      mimic cortical erosion.

(e) COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL:
    Display CLAHE-processed image side-by-side with the original, using identical
    geometric bounds. Mark anatomical landmarks (cuboid corners, calcaneocuboid joint
    line) on both views. Any new structure visible only in the CLAHE view must be
    scrutinized for whether it corresponds to a genuine anatomical feature (consistent
    with expected bone orientation) or an amplification of noise.

PROPOSAL 2: High-Pass Filtering (Laplacian-based Unsharp
           Masking) for Fracture Line Detection

(a) QUESTION:
    Are there linear discontinuities consistent with fracture lines in the cuboid body,
    the anterior calcaneal process, or the sustentaculum tali region that are not apparent
    at normal viewing contrast? Specifically, does the cuboid contain a comminuted
    fracture pattern that is obscured by the overall density of the overlapping lateral
    midfoot?

(b) WHY THIS METHOD:
    Fracture lines represent abrupt intensity transitions in the X-ray projection — they
    are high-frequency spatial features. The surrounding trabecular bone produces
    mid-frequency texture. A high-pass filter (e.g., Laplacian or bandpass filter)
    suppresses the low-frequency background intensity variation and the mid-frequency
    trabecular texture, leaving primarily the sharpest edges. When this high-frequency
    residue is added back to the original (unsharp masking), fracture lines are accentuated
    relative to the surrounding bone texture. This is particularly useful for subtle or
    impacted fractures that do not produce a gross displacement.

(c) EXPECTED FINDINGS:
    - A true fracture line will appear as a persistent, linear bright feature in the
      high-pass output, traversing the bone in an anatomically plausible direction.
    - A comminuted pattern will show multiple intersecting linear features within the
      bone body.
    - If no fracture exists, the high-pass output will show only the normal cortical
      outlines and random noise — no internal linear structures.

(d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS:
    - The most significant risk: normal trabecular struts can survive high-pass filtering
      and appear as false-positive "fracture lines." These will be shorter, more curved,
      and oriented parallel to normal trabecular direction. True fracture lines tend to be
      longer, sharper, and cross trabecular patterns perpendicularly.
    - Edge halos: every cortical boundary will produce a bright halo ring in the
      high-pass result. These must be ignored — they are artifacts of the filter, not
      pathology.
    - The filter kernel size is critical. Too narrow and it catches noise; too wide and
      it smooths away real fracture lines. I will test at least two sigma values
      (approximately 1.0 and 2.0 pixels for a Gaussian-based Laplacian) and compare
      results.

(e) COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL:
    Overlay the high-pass filtered result as a semi-transparent layer on the original
    image. A genuine fracture line should be visible both in the filter output AND
    traceable back to a real discontinuity in the original — even if faint. Any "fracture
    line" that exists only in the filtered image and has no counterpart in the original
    must be dismissed as artifact.

PROPOSAL 3: Quantitative Angular Measurement of Calcaneal
           Morphometry (Böhler Angle and Gissane Angle)

(a) QUESTION:
    Is the calcaneus structurally intact, or does it harbor a compression fracture that
    has altered its normal geometry? The first-pass review found no obvious fracture
    lines, but subtle calcaneal fractures can produce minimal radiographic signs other
    than loss of normal angles. Quantitative measurement provides an objective baseline.

(b) WHY THIS METHOD:
    The calcaneus has two well-established angular landmarks visible on lateral radiograph:

    Böhler angle: Formed by lines connecting (1) the highest point of the posterior
    calcaneal facet to the highest point of the anterior-superior calcaneal angle, and
    (2) that anterior-superior angle to the highest point of the posterior-superior
    calcaneal angle. Normal: 20-40 degrees. A value below 20 degrees (or negative)
    strongly suggests calcaneal body compression fracture.

    Gissane angle (posterior calcaneal angle): Formed by lines along the superior
    calcaneal surface — one anterior to the anterior process and one posterior to it.
    Normal: 120-145 degrees (obtuse). A straightening (angle approaching 180 degrees)
    suggests a fracture through the anterior process.

    These measurements are purely geometric and require no image enhancement — just
    accurate landmark identification on the original image.

(c) EXPECTED FINDINGS:
    - Normal angles would support the first-pass impression that the calcaneus is intact
      and shift focus to the cuboid as the primary pathology.
    - An abnormally low Böhler angle would indicate an occult calcaneal fracture not
      apparent by inspection alone.
    - A straightened Gissane angle would suggest anterior process involvement.

(d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS:
    - Measurement accuracy depends entirely on correct landmark identification. The
      "highest point" of the posterior facet can be ambiguous when the posterior facet
      has a curved rather than peaked contour. I will define landmarks using the maximum
      superior coordinate of the respective bony prominence, and report the coordinates
      used so the measurement is reproducible.
    - Patient positioning (foot flexion vs. dorsiflexion) can affect the Böhler angle.
      However, this is a fixed anatomical property and should not vary with foot
      position.
    - The sustentaculum tali can create confusion with the true anterior-superior angle
      of the calcaneus.

(e) COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL:
    These measurements are taken directly from the original, unmodified image. No
    comparison is needed because the original IS the reference. The comparison is to
    published normal ranges. I will annotate the original image with the measured angles
    and the landmark points used.

PROPOSAL 4: Soft-Tissue Contour Mapping for Dorsal Midfoot
           Swelling Assessment

(a) QUESTION:
    Is there localized soft tissue swelling over the lateral midfoot (corresponding to the
    region of the cuboid abnormality) that would support an acute inflammatory or
    traumatic process? Gross swelling was not noted in the first pass, but subtle regional
    asymmetry is difficult to judge by eye on a single view.

(b) WHY THIS METHOD:
    The soft tissue silhouette of the foot is visible as a faint density gradient
    against the black background. By thresholding to isolate the soft tissue boundary
    and then computing a smooth reference contour (e.g., a spline fit to the expected
    dorsal foot profile), deviations between the actual contour and the reference contour
    can be quantified as a swelling map. Regions where the actual contour exceeds the
    expected contour by more than a threshold (e.g., 5 mm) would indicate localized
    swelling.

    On a lateral view, the dorsal foot profile should follow a gentle arc from the ankle
    to the toes. Localized fullness over the midfoot would stand out as a bulge in the
    dorsal contour.

(c) EXPECTED FINDINGS:
    - If there is acute trauma or infection at the cuboid, I would expect to see dorsal
      soft tissue thickening over the lateral midfoot corresponding to the abnormal bone
      region.
    - If the swelling map is flat (no regional deviation), this would suggest either a
      chronic non-inflammatory process or that the soft tissue response has resolved.
    - Note: absence of swelling does not rule out pathology — many bone lesions do not
      produce visible soft tissue changes on X-ray.

(d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS:
    - The primary limitation is that a lateral view collapses the medial-lateral
      dimension. Swelling that is purely lateral (outside the X-ray beam's primary path
      through the foot) may not produce a contour change.
    - Patient positioning (how the foot is placed on the detector) affects the dorsal
      profile. A foot placed slightly in pronation will show different soft tissue
      contours than a neutral foot.
    - The threshold for "swelling" is somewhat arbitrary. I will use a 5-mm deviation
      threshold as a starting point and report the full deviation profile, not just
      binary presence/absence.
    - Scattering and beam hardening near the dense distal tibia can create a density
      gradient that interferes with soft tissue boundary detection. I will restrict the
      analysis to the distal two-thirds of the foot, anterior to the talus.

(e) COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL:
    Plot the measured soft tissue contour as a line overlay on the original image. Mark
    the expected reference contour in a different color. The visual overlay makes it
    immediately clear whether the detected "swelling" corresponds to a visible soft
    tissue bulge or is a quantification artifact.

PROPOSAL 5: Frequency-Domain Analysis to Assess Trabecular
           Pattern Integrity in the Cuboid vs. Calcaneus

(a) QUESTION:
    Is the trabecular bone pattern in the cuboid genuinely abnormal compared to other
    trabecular bone in the same image? By comparing the frequency content of the cuboid
    region to a reference region (the calcaneal body, which appears normal), I can
    determine whether the "disrupted trabecular pattern" noted in the first pass is a
    real structural difference or simply a consequence of local density variation.

(b) WHY THIS METHOD:
    Normal trabecular bone produces a characteristic mid-frequency texture pattern in the
    spatial frequency domain. When trabeculae are disrupted (by fracture, osteolysis,
    or replacement by abnormal tissue), the power spectrum changes: the characteristic
    mid-frequency peak flattens or shifts. By computing 2D Fourier transforms (or more
    practically, the power spectrum via 2D FFT) of small ROIs in the cuboid and the
    calcaneus, I can compare their spectral fingerprints.

    The calcaneus serves as an internal control: it is in the same image, same patient,
    same bone type (cancellous bone), and same imaging conditions. This controls for
    exposure, detector characteristics, and patient bone quality.

(c) EXPECTED FINDINGS:
    - If the cuboid trabecular pattern is normal (and the first-pass impression was
      misleading due to projection overlap), the power spectrum of the cuboid ROI should
      closely match the calcaneus ROI — similar peak frequency and similar spectral
      slope.
    - If there is genuine trabecular disruption, the cuboid spectrum will show reduced
      power at the characteristic trabecular frequency and potentially elevated low-
      frequency power (indicating replacement by more homogeneous tissue, as in a
      lytic lesion).
    - If there is a fracture with displacement, I would expect a localized discontinuity
      in the spectrum rather than a global spectral change.

(d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS:
    - This is the most technique-sensitive proposal. ROI selection is critical: the
      cuboid ROI must avoid cortical margins (which contribute low-frequency edges that
      dominate the spectrum). I will erode the ROI inward by approximately 2-3 pixels
      from the visible bone boundary.
    - Overlapping structures cast their own trabecular patterns onto the ROI,
      confounding the comparison. The cuboid is partially superimposed by the base of
      the 4th and 5th metatarsals and possibly the lateral cuneiform. This is the
      fundamental limitation: on a 2D projection, I cannot perfectly isolate the cuboid
      signal.
    - The FFT assumes stationarity within the ROI. A ROI containing a lesion boundary
      (transition from normal to abnormal) violates this assumption and will produce
      misleading spectral features. I may need to subdivide the cuboid ROI into multiple
      sub-ROIs and compare each.

(e) COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL:
    Plot the radial power spectra (averaged over angle) of the cuboid ROI and the
    calcaneus ROI on the same graph. The visual comparison of spectral curves is more
    interpretable than raw 2D FFT magnitude images. Annotate the expected trabecular
    frequency band based on the calcaneus reference. Any departure in the cuboid
    spectrum within this band would be the signal of interest.
