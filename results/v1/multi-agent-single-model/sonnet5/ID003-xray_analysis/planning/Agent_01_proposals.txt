3. Agent_01_propose_full_image_clahe
------------------------------------------------------------
QUESTION IT'S TRYING TO ANSWER:
Is there low-contrast detail (soft tissue swelling, subtle periosteal reaction, faint secondary lucent/sclerotic lines) anywhere in the image that is being compressed out by the current exposure/windowing, particularly given the very large uniformly black background suggests most dynamic range is unused by the anatomy?

WHY THIS METHOD MAY HELP:
Contrast-Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) applied to the whole image (with the black background masked out or capped to avoid amplifying noise there) redistributes local contrast and can reveal subtle bone texture or soft-tissue planes that are present but visually flattened in the native grayscale mapping.

WHAT INFORMATION IT EXPECTS TO REVEAL:
Better visibility of soft-tissue contours (e.g., any focal swelling near the elbow or forearm that might localize an injury), and possibly better delineation of trabecular pattern/cortical margins throughout the humerus, radius, and ulna.

ARTIFACTS / MISLEADING EFFECTS THAT MAY OCCUR:
CLAHE can amplify quantum noise and compression artifacts in the near-black background into visible speckle or false texture, and can locally over-enhance contrast in a way that makes normal trabecular bone look heterogeneous/mottled, potentially mimicking a lesion. Background should be excluded or heavily damped before equalization.

HOW THE RESULT SHOULD BE COMPARED WITH THE ORIGINAL:
Place the CLAHE output next to the original at identical crop/zoom and specifically check whether any newly visible "finding" corresponds to a plausible faint signal in the raw pixel values (e.g., via a quick pixel-intensity profile) rather than being purely an equalization artifact.

4. Agent_01_propose_cortex_edge_enhancement
------------------------------------------------------------
QUESTION IT'S TRYING TO ANSWER:
Are the cortical margins of the humerus, radius, and ulna continuous along their entire visualized length, or is there a focal break/step-off (beyond the mid-shaft line already noted) anywhere else that is difficult to appreciate against the current smooth grayscale rendering?

WHY THIS METHOD MAY HELP:
An edge-detection or gradient-magnitude rendering (e.g., Sobel filter) highlights boundaries between cortical bone and surrounding tissue/medulla as bright contour lines, making it easier to visually trace continuity of each bone's outer margin end-to-end and spot any discontinuity, step, or angulation that might be subtle in the plain grayscale image.

WHAT INFORMATION IT EXPECTS TO REVEAL:
A clean contour map of each long bone that can be visually scanned for breaks, offsets, or unexpected angulation along the shaft, and confirmation of whether the elbow joint contours (distal humerus, radial head, olecranon) look smooth and congruent.

ARTIFACTS / MISLEADING EFFECTS THAT MAY OCCUR:
Edge filters respond to any strong intensity gradient, including those from soft-tissue overlap, skin folds, or superimposed structures (e.g., the digit cluster overlapping the distal forearm), which can create edge-lines that look like cortical breaks but aren't. Edge maps also do not preserve absolute intensity, so they can't by themselves distinguish a fracture line from a superimposition shadow — they should be used only to flag candidate regions for further scrutiny in the grayscale original.

HOW THE RESULT SHOULD BE COMPARED WITH THE ORIGINAL:
Any discontinuity flagged in the edge map must be traced back to the identical location in the original grayscale image and confirmed as a real intensity change (not just an edge-filter response to noise or overlap) before being considered a candidate finding.
