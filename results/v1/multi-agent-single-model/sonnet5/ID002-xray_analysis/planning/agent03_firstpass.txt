PROPOSAL 1: Soft-tissue window / local contrast stretch of the peri-elbow
region (fat-pad assessment)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Question it answers: Is there an elevated/displaced anterior or posterior
fat pad indicating a joint effusion, which would be the main indirect sign
of an occult (non-displaced) elbow fracture on this view?

Why it may help: The current global exposure appears tuned toward showing
bone cortex clearly, which compresses the narrower, lower-contrast intensity
range that separates fat-pad fat density from adjacent joint capsule/muscle.
Re-mapping (stretching) just the intensity band corresponding to soft tissue
around the joint — e.g., via a localized histogram stretch or CLAHE limited
to a bounding box around the olecranon fossa/coronoid fossa area — should
make a subtle fat pad more visible without blowing out the rest of the
image.

Expected findings: Either a clearly visible, normal-appearing anterior fat
pad (sail sign absent) with no posterior fat pad — supporting a normal
study — or an unmasked posterior fat pad / abnormally bulging anterior fat
pad, which would raise suspicion for occult fracture and change the overall
read.

Possible artifacts/pitfalls: Aggressive local contrast stretching can create
a false impression of a fat pad from normal overlying skin folds, triceps
tendon fat planes, or JPEG/PNG compression blocking; CLAHE can also
introduce halo artifacts at high-contrast bone/soft-tissue boundaries that
could be mistaken for a displaced fat pad line. Any positive finding must be
checked against expected anatomic location and shape (crescentic, not a hard
edge) before being trusted.

Comparison with original: Overlay/side-by-side the enhanced ROI against the
original at the same crop and scale; verify any "new" fat-pad line
corresponds to a real, smooth curvilinear density and not an artifact
introduced only in the processed version, by checking whether a fainter
version of the same edge is present in the unprocessed image at a stretched
eye-adapted brightness.

PROPOSAL 2: Edge-enhancement (high-pass / unsharp masking) of the radial
neck, radial head, and coronoid process cortex
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Question it answers: Is there a subtle, non-displaced cortical fracture
line at the radial neck or coronoid — the two structures most often
responsible for missed elbow fractures on a lateral view — that is present
but too low-contrast to register on casual visual inspection?

Why it may help: Non-displaced fractures often present as a thin lucent
line running roughly perpendicular to the cortex, with contrast only
slightly different from surrounding bone. A high-pass/unsharp-mask filter
selectively boosts local intensity gradients (edges) while leaving broad,
slowly varying regions (like uniform cortex or soft tissue) relatively
unchanged, which should make a thin real fracture line more conspicuous
without amplifying the whole image's noise floor as much as a naive
brightness/contrast change would.

Expected findings: Either confirmation that the cortical outline of the
radial neck and coronoid is a single continuous line (supporting no
fracture), or an accentuated secondary line crossing the cortex that
warrants flagging as a possible fracture for further correlation.

Possible artifacts/pitfalls: Edge enhancement amplifies real noise and can
manufacture apparent "lines" from trabecular bone crossing the cortex,
overlapping soft-tissue skin folds, or the radial tuberosity's normal
anatomic contour — all of which can mimic a fracture line. Over-sharpening
can also exaggerate the normal radial neck's slight concavity into
something that looks like angulation. Any candidate line must be checked
for continuity across the full cortical width and for anatomic plausibility
of orientation.

Comparison with original: Directly overlay the candidate line's location on
the unprocessed image at matched zoom; a genuine fracture should show at
least a faint correlate in the original, and its course should be traceable
consistently across multiple enhancement strengths (i.e., not appear only
at one specific filter parameter, which would suggest it's a processing
artifact).

PROPOSAL 3: Quantitative alignment check (radiocapitellar line / anterior
humeral line measurement overlay)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Question it answers: Given that the elbow is flexed more than the typical
90 degrees used for a standard lateral, is the radiocapitellar alignment
actually normal, or does the increased flexion angle make a subtly
malaligned joint (e.g., from an occult radial head subluxation or subtle
supracondylar injury) look deceptively normal to the eye?

Why it may help: Alignment lines are a geometric, reproducible check rather
than a subjective visual impression, and they are specifically designed to
catch malalignment that is easy to miss by eye, especially when normal
positioning assumptions (standard 90-degree flexion) do not strictly hold.
Drawing the line through the radial shaft/neck axis and checking whether it
bisects the capitellum is a purely measurement-based approach, distinct
from just "looking harder" at the same pixels.

Expected findings: Either the radial axis line passes through the
capitellum center (supporting normal alignment despite the atypical flexion
angle), or it does not, which would suggest possible radial head
subluxation/dislocation or an associated injury that visual inspection
alone might have discounted as a positioning artifact.

Possible artifacts/pitfalls: Because the joint is flexed beyond the
canonical angle and possibly slightly oblique, the standard alignment-line
heuristics (calibrated for a true 90-degree lateral) may not transfer
perfectly, risking a false-positive "malalignment" call that is really just
a projection effect. The exact center of the capitellum and axis of the
radial shaft also require careful, consistent point selection, which
introduces measurement subjectivity.

Comparison with original: Present the measurement as an overlay directly on
the original (unmodified pixel data, no contrast/intensity manipulation),
so the geometric conclusion is not confounded by the enhancement artifacts
from Proposals 1-2; explicitly note the estimated flexion angle and any
apparent obliquity alongside the measurement so the result is interpreted
in context rather than as an absolute finding.
