STEP C — INDEPENDENT PROCESSING PROPOSALS
(Research/educational exercise only — NOT a clinical diagnosis.)

PROPOSAL 1: Localized/adaptive contrast enhancement (e.g., CLAHE — contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization) applied to the hindfoot/midfoot region
- Question it tries to answer: Is the coarser, busier-looking trabecular texture I observed in the calcaneus/talus region normal cancellous bone and projectional overlap, or does it hide a subtle cortical/trabecular discontinuity?
- Why it may help: Global contrast in the original image looks compressed, especially where multiple overlapping tarsal bones create a dense, low-contrast cluster. Adaptive (locally-computed) histogram equalization redistributes contrast within small neighborhoods, which can reveal fine structure in busy regions without blowing out the already-bright tibial shaft.
- What it expects to reveal: Clearer separation of individual bone contours in the hindfoot, better visibility of any subtle lucent fracture lines or sclerotic bands, and a clearer view of the subtalar and calcaneocuboid joint spaces.
- Possible artifacts/misleading effects: CLAHE can amplify noise and create false "texture" or pseudo-trabecular patterns that look like pathology but are processing artifacts; it can also introduce blocky/tiled boundary effects at tile edges if tile size is too small; over-aggressive clip limits can exaggerate normal variation into something that looks abnormal.
- How to compare with original: View side-by-side at matched crop/zoom; treat any "finding" in the enhanced image as a hypothesis only, then verify it is also plausible (even if subtler) in the original unprocessed image before giving it any weight. Any feature present only in the processed image and absent in the original should be treated as a probable artifact.

PROPOSAL 2: Edge enhancement / unsharp masking focused on cortical margins (distal fibula, talar dome, calcaneus)
- Question it tries to answer: Are the cortical outlines of the distal fibula, talar dome, and calcaneus fully continuous, or is there a step-off/lucent line suggestive of a hairline fracture that isn't obvious at native resolution?
- Why it may help: Unsharp masking accentuates high-frequency edge transitions (bone cortex vs. surrounding tissue/marrow), which can make a thin fracture line or subtle cortical irregularity more visually detectable than in the softly-rendered original.
- What it expects to reveal: A crisper cortical boundary line around the malleoli, talar dome, and calcaneal margins, making any break in that continuous line easier to trace by eye.
- Possible artifacts/misleading effects: Unsharp masking can create ringing/halo artifacts along real edges that mimic a second cortical line or a lucent gap where none exists (a classic false-positive fracture appearance); it also amplifies grain/noise, which could be mistaken for a fine fracture line, especially near the image's dark border where signal is weaker.
- How to compare with original: Overlay or flip rapidly between original and sharpened versions at the same crop; confirm any suspected discontinuity is visible as at least a faint feature in the unprocessed image, and check whether the "gap" follows a plausible anatomic path (e.g., along the expected fibular cortex) rather than a straight artifact-like line that could come from sharpening halos.

PROPOSAL 3: Targeted magnified crops (region-of-interest zoom, with simple upscaling/interpolation) of the ankle mortise and anterior/posterior soft tissue
- Question it tries to answer: Is there any soft-tissue swelling or fat-pad displacement anteriorly (suggesting a joint effusion) or posteriorly (pre-Achilles fat pad), and is the ankle mortise/joint space width uniform, given these details are hard to judge at full-image scale?
- Why it may help: The full image compresses a lot of anatomy into a small viewing area; simply cropping and enlarging the ankle joint and adjacent soft tissue lets the eye (and any automated edge/contrast analysis) assess subtle contour and spacing changes that are easy to overlook at whole-image scale.
- What it expects to reveal: Whether the anterior soft-tissue stripe is bulged/thickened relative to the posterior contour, whether the joint space is symmetric, and whether small bony fragments or avulsions near the malleoli are present.
- Possible artifacts/misleading effects: Simple pixel interpolation used for zooming does not add real information and can create smooth "fake detail" (interpolation artifacts) that might be over-interpreted as a defined edge or margin; magnification alone does not improve underlying resolution, so any conclusion should be tempered by the true resolution limit of the source image.
- How to compare with original: Note the zoom factor and interpolation method used; cross-check any feature seen in the zoomed crop against its (smaller but still present) appearance in the original at 1:1 scale to make sure it is a real signal and not an interpolation artifact.

Note: All three proposals are exploratory visualization aids intended to help a human/automated reviewer look more carefully at regions already flagged in the first-pass review (hindfoot trabecular texture, malleolar/talar cortex, ankle soft tissue). None of these processing steps should be interpreted as confirming or ruling out pathology on their own — they only change how existing pixel data is displayed, and every processed-image observation should be validated against the original before being treated as meaningful.
