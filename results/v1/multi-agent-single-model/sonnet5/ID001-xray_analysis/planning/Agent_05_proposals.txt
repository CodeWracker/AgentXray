STEP C — INDEPENDENT PROCESSING PROPOSALS
===========================================

PROPOSAL 1: Histogram / intensity-range analysis and linear contrast stretch
- Question it is trying to answer: Is the image using its full available dynamic range, and is the black "background" truly uniform, or does it contain hidden structure (e.g., the lighter rectangular region I noticed) that could be masking or mimicking anatomic contrast?
- Why this method may help: A histogram immediately shows whether pixel values are compressed into a narrow band (low contrast) or spread out. A linear stretch (rescaling the darkest/lightest pixels to the full 0–255 range) is a simple, non-distorting way to recover any contrast that exists but is currently compressed, which is cheap to compute and easy to sanity-check against the original.
- What it expects to reveal: Whether faint cortical lines, subtle lucencies, or soft-tissue swelling are present but currently too low-contrast to see; and whether the lighter rectangular background region is a smooth gradient or a hard-edged geometric shape (clarifying whether it's vignetting, collimation, or a second exposure).
- Artifacts / misleading effects: Stretching contrast can also amplify noise and digitization artifacts (film grain, JPEG-type blocking, scan banding), potentially creating the appearance of texture or lines that are not real anatomy. It will also make the background artifact more visually prominent, which must not be misread as a pathologic finding.
- How to compare with the original: View the stretched image side-by-side with the original at the same crop/zoom; anything newly visible in bone should be cross-checked against the original at the same location before being considered a real finding. The background region should be interpreted only as an acquisition artifact, not annotated as anatomy.

PROPOSAL 2: Local/adaptive contrast enhancement (CLAHE) restricted to the tarsal/ankle region
- Question it is trying to answer: Are there subtle cortical discontinuities or joint-space irregularities in the densely overlapping talus/calcaneus/midfoot region that are hidden by superimposition and limited local contrast?
- Why this method may help: Unlike a global stretch, adaptive/local contrast enhancement boosts contrast differently in different sub-regions, which is well suited to a lateral foot view where the hindfoot is much denser (more overlapping bone) than the tibial shaft. This can help visually separate adjacent bone margins in the crowded region without over-brightening the already well-exposed tibia/fibula shafts.
- What it expects to reveal: Better delineation of the talar dome, subtalar joint line, and calcaneal cortex; possible improved visibility of a subtle non-displaced fracture line or joint space narrowing/widening in the midfoot.
- Artifacts / misleading effects: Local contrast methods are well known to create halo artifacts at high-contrast boundaries (e.g., bone-to-background edges) and can exaggerate texture/noise into false "lines" that look like fracture lines but are processing artifacts. Over-aggressive tiling can also create blocky, unnatural transitions.
- How to compare with the original: Overlay or flip between the enhanced crop and the original crop at identical zoom/position; treat any newly visible line as only a hypothesis to re-examine on the original (and ideally would need a true second view or higher-quality acquisition to confirm), not a confirmed finding.

PROPOSAL 3: Edge-detection / high-pass filtering overlay on the tibial, fibular, talar, and calcaneal cortices
- Question it is trying to answer: Are the cortical outlines of the major long bones and hindfoot bones smooth and continuous, or is there a subtle step-off, angulation, or break consistent with a non-displaced fracture that is difficult to appreciate by eye against the surrounding soft-tissue/bone gradient?
- Why this method may help: Edge/gradient filters (e.g., Sobel-type or unsharp-mask-derived edge maps) make the boundary between cortical bone and surrounding tissue more explicit, turning a subtle intensity change into a clearer line, which can help the eye track continuity along a cortex over its full length.
- What it expects to reveal: Confirmation of continuous, unbroken cortical margins (reassuring) versus a discontinuity, doubling, or offset in the edge map that warrants closer visual scrutiny at that specific location.
- Artifacts / misleading effects: Edge filters respond to any strong gradient, including film-grain noise, scan artifacts, overlapping bone margins (talus overlying calcaneus, tibia overlying fibula) that are anatomically normal, and the edge of the odd lighter background rectangle — all of which can produce lines that look like "breaks" but are not fractures. Every apparent discontinuity must be individually checked against the plain image.
- How to compare with the original: Examine the edge map strictly as an overlay/guide next to the unprocessed image at the same scale; any candidate discontinuity is only worth noting if it is also plausible (i.e., visible as an actual intensity change, not just a filter artifact) on the original grayscale image.

NOTE ON SCOPE
All three proposals are aimed at clarifying uncertainty introduced by (a) the image's apparent low contrast/soft reproduction quality and (b) the unexplained background artifact, and at helping separate genuine bone-margin detail from overlap and noise in the crowded hindfoot region. None of these methods can substitute for additional clinical views (e.g., an orthogonal AP/mortise view) or expert clinical correlation, and none should be used to assert a diagnosis — only to guide where a human/clinical reader might want to look more closely.
