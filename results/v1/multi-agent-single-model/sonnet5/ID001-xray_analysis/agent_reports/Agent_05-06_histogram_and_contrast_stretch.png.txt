PURPOSE: To determine whether the original radiograph is using its full available dynamic range or whether contrast is compressed in a way that could be hiding subtle bone/soft-tissue detail, and to check whether the unusual lighter background region noted in first-pass review is related to a range-compression issue.

OBSERVATIONS: The figure shows three panels: the original image, a linear min-max contrast stretch (rescaled to 0-255), and an overlaid histogram of both. Visually, the original and stretched panels look nearly identical — same apparent brightness, contrast, and visibility of the tibia/fibula, ankle, and tarsal bones. The histogram confirms this: the blue (original) and orange (stretched) distributions overlap almost exactly across the full range, both showing a tall sharp peak at low pixel values (~15-30, count near 17,500-18,000) corresponding to the large background area, plus a broad low, fairly flat tail extending out to roughly 150-200 corresponding to soft tissue and bone.

RELEVANT FINDINGS: Because the stretched histogram essentially reproduces the original, the original image must already span close to the full 0-255 range (i.e., it already contains near-black and near-white pixels), so a simple linear stretch adds no new visible contrast. The "flat/soft" look I noted in my first-pass review is therefore not caused by unused/compressed dynamic range — it reflects genuinely modest relative contrast between background, soft tissue, and bone at the mid-tone level, not a simple technical fix. The dominant low-value peak is consistent with the large uniform-looking dark background (including the lighter rectangular artifact region) rather than anatomy.

The near-total overlap of the two histograms should not be over-interpreted as "no contrast issue exists"; it only means a naive linear stretch specifically does not help here.
