PURPOSE: Calibrate how much of the fine texture seen throughout the image (especially in the hindfoot/midfoot region and along the shaft) is high-frequency noise versus real anatomical structure, by applying non-local-means denoising and visualizing exactly what content was removed.
OBSERVATIONS: Three panels. Left: the original lateral ankle/foot radiograph, matching the previously reviewed original. Middle: the same image after non-local-means denoising — visibly smoother, with softened edges throughout; the tibia/fibula shafts look more uniformly gray, and the hindfoot/midfoot bone cluster has lost most of its fine internal texture, now appearing as smoother, blob-like light regions with less distinguishable internal trabecular detail; overall bone-to-background edges remain but are softer. Right: an amplified difference ("removed content") map on a black background, showing bright white/gray fibrous and linear patterns concentrated almost entirely within the limb silhouette — a dense meshwork of fine lines and speckle in the hindfoot/calcaneus/talus region, a network of longitudinal linear striations running along the tibial/fibular shaft, and a fainter but present linear texture continuing into the forefoot/metatarsal area. Some faint horizontal/vertical streak patterns are also visible in the nominally "empty" background area outside the limb, echoing the banding noted in the background-artifact panel.
RELEVANT FINDINGS: The bulk of the fine texture removed by denoising is anatomically located — concentrated within the bone/soft-tissue silhouette rather than randomly scattered across the whole frame — which suggests much of it reflects real (if fine) trabecular/structural detail and edge information rather than pure random sensor noise, since true random noise would be expected more uniformly across the whole image including the background. At the same time, the denoised (middle) image losing so much internal structure in the hindfoot cluster shows that a meaningful fraction of what looked like "coarse, busy texture" in that region in the original does correspond to fine, removable high-frequency content — consistent with either real fine trabecular architecture, edge/overlap effects between overlapping tarsal bones, or acquisition noise superimposed on the bone signal. The faint streaking visible in the background portion of the difference map corroborates that at least some non-anatomical, structured noise (matching the pattern flagged in the background-artifact map) does exist and was successfully separated out by the denoising process.
RELATION TO ORIGINAL IMAGE: Directly derived from and matched to the original image; the "removed content" pattern aligns spatially with the hindfoot/midfoot region flagged as texturally coarser in the original first-pass review, and the faint background streaking in the diff map corresponds to the vertical banding/non-uniform margin already observed directly in the original and quantified in the background-artifact map (image 07).
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: The difference panel is explicitly "amplified," so its visual intensity should not be read as proportional to the true magnitude of noise in the original — small, clinically/structurally insignificant variations can appear as strong bright lines once amplified. Non-local-means denoising can also smooth over or blur genuinely thin real structures (e.g., a fine cortical line or subtle fracture), meaning structure present in the "removed" map is not necessarily noise — some of it may be real fine anatomy that the algorithm treated as removable high-frequency content; this is a known risk/limitation of the technique itself, not evidence that those specific structures are unimportant. The presence of background streaking in the diff map, rather than confirming a pure sensor artifact, could also partly reflect the denoising algorithm's own edge-handling near the image borders.
