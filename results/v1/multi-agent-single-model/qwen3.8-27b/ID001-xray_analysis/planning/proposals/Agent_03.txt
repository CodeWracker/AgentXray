PROPOSAL 1: Local illumination normalization (de-vignetting) of the whole image, prioritizing the right half
QUESTION: Is there forefoot pathology (metatarsal cortical break, TMT joint abnormality, soft-tissue swelling) in the darker right third of the image that the vignetting is masking?
METHOD: Estimate the smooth background illumination (e.g., large-kernel Gaussian/morphological opening or low-pass surface fit of the background) and divide the image by it (or fit a polynomial dome and normalize), preserving relative contrast while evening out the left-to-right brightness gradient.
WHY IT MAY HELP: The vignetting is a smooth, low-frequency intensity gradient; forefoot anatomy is high-frequency structure. Dividing out the smooth background selectively lifts the dark right side without globally reducing contrast, recovering metatarsal cortex and joint detail that is below the visible threshold on the raw scan.
EXPECTED REVELATION: Recovered visibility of distal metatarsals, metatarsal heads, and the most visible tarsometatarsal joints; possible detection of a faint cortical break, erosive change, or soft-tissue fullness in the forefoot; confirmation that the right side is simply underexposed rather than pathologically dense/absent.
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: Over-correction can create false bright "hot spots" that mimic lesions; edge halos along the limb border; residual gradient if the dome fit is imperfect; noise in the dark background can be amplified into false speckles.
COMPARISON TO ORIGINAL: View the corrected right half side by side with the raw image; any "new" finding must also be at least faintly perceptible in the original (or explainable by the known gradient) — features that appear only in the corrected image and have no counterpart in the raw film are treated as correction artifacts, not anatomy.

PROPOSAL 2: Adaptive local contrast enhancement (CLAHE) of the heel region of interest
QUESTION: What is the precise morphology of the calcaneal spur and its relationship to the Achilles insertion and plantar fascia, and is there retrocalcaneal/plantar soft-tissue fullness or fascial calcification?
METHOD: Crop the posteroinferior calcaneus region (roughly the lower-left quadrant), then apply contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) with a moderate clip limit (e.g., 2.0-3.0) and an 8-16 block grid so local contrast is raised without globally amplifying film grain.
WHY IT MAY HELP: The spur and the adjacent soft tissues sit in a region where bone and soft tissue overlap the mid-gray range of a degraded film scan. CLAHE redistributes local intensity histograms, separating the bony spur edge from the heel-pad soft tissue and revealing low-contrast fascial/bursal interfaces that global stretching would smear or saturate.
EXPECTED REVELATION: Sharp demarcation of the spur base vs tip, identification of a second (plantar) spur component or a single posterior beak, thickening of the Achilles or plantar fascia, and possible bursal fluid/calcification in the retrocalcaneal space; better definition of the cortical margin of the calcaneal tuberosity.
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: Film grain and dust specks are amplified into false "calcifications"; CLAHE tiling can produce blocky patches at tile boundaries; over-stretched soft tissue can make normal heel pad look edematous; small bright scan specks near the heel (already noted as dust) may look like enthesophyte calcification.
COMPARISON TO ORIGINAL: Overlay/flip-flop between CLAHE and raw images continuously; every structure claimed in the enhanced image (spur tip, bursal line, speck) must be verifiable at its corresponding location in the original, accepting that it may be fainter there. Anything visible only in the enhanced image is an enhancement artifact.

PROPOSAL 3: Edge-enhancement overlay for occult fracture screening (calcaneus, talus, midfoot)
QUESTION: Is there a subtle non-displaced fracture line or cortical break in the calcaneus, talus, or midfoot that is below the visual threshold on the raw scan?
METHOD: Compute an edge map (e.g., Canny or Sobel gradient magnitude) with thresholds set to the local noise floor of the film grain, optionally after light median (3x3) denoising to suppress isolated grain specks; display the edge map as a colored overlay (e.g., red edges) on top of the original image so edges can be checked against real anatomy.
WHY IT MAY HELP: A non-displaced fracture is a thin linear low-contrast discontinuity running through cortex/trabeculae. Gradient-based edge detection is more sensitive to such linear structures than the eye on a grainy scan, and the overlay ties each detected edge back to a real location so it can be judged anatomically (continuous cortex = normal; interrupted cortex = possible break).
EXPECTED REVELATION: A short oblique linear edge traversing the calcaneal body or sustentaculum (classic occult calcaneal fracture), a cortical step-off at the talus or talar dome, or confirmation of smooth continuous cortices throughout (supporting the "no fracture" impression).
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: Trabecular bone pattern and film grain produce abundant false linear edges; dust specks create short edge pairs; enhancement of normal overlapping cortical margins (fibula over tibia) can mimic a double line; median denoising may erase a genuinely faint fracture line.
COMPARISON TO ORIGINAL: Each candidate linear edge must be examined in the raw image at the same location: a true fracture line, even if faint, should be traceable in the original across its full length; edges that exist only in the map, that follow grain specks, or that stop abruptly where anatomy is smooth are dismissed as noise/trabecular pattern.

PROPOSAL 4: Landmark-based geometric measurement of calcaneal parameters (Böhler's angle, posterior calcaneal height, calcaneal pitch)
QUESTION: Is the calcaneal geometry normal, i.e., can an old or missed (healed, minimally displaced) calcaneal fracture be excluded, and is the longitudinal arch configuration (pitch) documented for correlation with the spur?
METHOD: Manually place landmarks on the raw (or lightly CLAHE-enhanced for confidence, but measured on raw) image: (a) highest point on the posterior calcaneal tuberosity, (b) highest point on the posterosuperior facet, (c) highest point on the anterosuperior facet; compute Böhler's angle. Add the most inferior point of the tuberosity for posterior height, and the inferior borders of the calcaneus and first metatarsal (to the extent visible) for calcaneal pitch. Report in degrees with a short note on landmark confidence.
WHY IT MAY HELP: Böhler's angle (normally about 20-30 degrees) is the standard lateral radiographic index for calcaneal fractures; a flattened or negative angle strongly suggests old fracture even without a visible fracture line. Pitch documents forefoot-drop/rocker geometry, which is clinically relevant to the heel spur (plantar overload) and to whether the film was taken in plantarflexion (which falsely reduces pitch).
EXPECTED REVELATION: A quantitative Böhler's angle confirming normal calcaneal architecture (supporting the first-pass "no fracture" impression) or revealing a flattened angle pointing to an old, healed calcaneal fracture; a quantified calcaneal pitch noting the plantarflexed positioning.
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: Landmark placement error, especially at the facet "high points" where the contours are rounded and partially obscured by the talus; non-true-lateral rotation and plantarflexion bias the pitch; a spur can be mistaken for the tuberosity extreme; small pixel-level landmark shifts change the angle by several degrees, so measurement must be reported with a confidence caveat.
COMPARISON TO ORIGINAL: Landmarks must be placed on the original image (or its unaltered crop); the measured angles are then sanity-checked by eye against the raw contours (does the drawn Böhler line look consistent with the visible facets?). If the image is too degraded to place facet landmarks reliably, say so explicitly rather than reporting a false-precision number.

PROPOSAL 5: Magnified joint-space inspection of the ankle and subtalar joints after local contrast enhancement
QUESTION: Are the tibiotalar and subtalar joint spaces preserved, or is there early degenerative narrowing/osteophyte change obscured by superimposition and low contrast?
METHOD: Crop the central region containing the tibiotalar joint, talus, subtalar joint, and calcaneal body; apply a mild CLAHE (lower clip limit, e.g., 1.5-2.0) to preserve dynamic range; display the crop at 2-3x magnification alongside the corresponding region of the original.
WHY IT MAY HELP: Joint-space width on a lateral film is only a few pixels wide and is degraded by oblique projection and superimposition (talar dome over posterior tibia, talus over calcaneus). Local enhancement plus magnification increases the effective sampling of the space and makes marginal osteophytes or early narrowing more perceptible.
EXPECTED REVELATION: A preserved, uniform tibiotalar and subtalar joint space (supporting "no gross degenerative change") or a focal narrowing/marginal osteophyte suggesting early osteoarthritis, which would reframe the heel spur as part of a broader degenerative picture.
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: Magnification does not add real resolution — it only interpolates, so fine detail may look artificially smooth or blocky; mild CLAHE can create banding within the joint space that mimics irregularity; overlapping soft tissue shadows can fake a "narrowed" space; interpolation artifacts can create false step-offs at the joint margin.
COMPARISON TO ORIGINAL: Judge the joint space first on the raw image; use the enhanced/magnified crop only as a second look. A narrowing or osteophyte must be consistent with the raw image's anatomy at that location; if the space is uninterpretable in the original, state that it remains unassessable rather than calling the enhanced appearance diagnostic.
