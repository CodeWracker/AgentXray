PROPOSAL 1: Anatomical ROI Crop and Reframe
============================================================

Question being answered:
Does isolating the actual anatomical region eliminate wasted dynamic range and
reveal detail currently compressed by the large black margin?

Method:
Detect the bounding box of pixels > 50 (the threshold where soft tissue signal
begins above background), crop to that region, and upscale the result to 1600x1280
to maintain resolution for pixel-level analysis.

Why it helps:
The image currently wastes ~50% of horizontal space on black background.
The top-right quadrant (mean 82.2) contains most anatomical content, while
the left half (mean 5.1) contributes nothing. Reframing concentrates all
pixel budget on the anatomy.

Expected revelation:

- Better soft tissue visibility from the humeral soft tissue shadow region
- Easier visual assessment of all structures

Possible artifacts:
- Upscaling interpolation may introduce artificial smoothing. Use nearest-neighbor

- Cropping may exclude peripheral soft tissue findings if the bounding box is
  too tight. Include a 50-pixel margin around the bounding box.

Comparison with original:
Overlay the cropped result on the original at the same display size to verify
no anatomical structures were truncated. The reframed image should show the
same structures at higher effective resolution.

Actionable steps:

PROPOSAL 2: CLAHE (Contrast-Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization)
============================================================

Question being answered:
Are there details in mid-tones and soft-tissue regions that are invisible in the
linear presentation because local contrast is too low?

Method:
Apply CLAHE with clip limit 2.0, tile size 64x64 (in pixels), on the grayscale
version of the image. CLAHE equalizes histogram locally within each tile while
clamping extreme contrast amplification to avoid noise explosion.

Why it helps:
The image has regions of very different brightness (bone mean 219.5, forearm
region mean 76.6, soft tissue mean ~1.4 in lower areas). Global contrast
stretching would over-amplify the bright bone or lose the dark soft tissue.
CLAHE adapts to each local region, revealing subtle gradients.

Expected revelation:
- Enhanced visibility of the anterior and posterior fat pads in the elbow joint
  region (currently anterior fat pad region mean 170.3, posterior 42.6 — a
  significant difference that may contain diagnostic information about the
  sail sign or fat pad displacement)
- Better differentiation of overlapping soft tissue layers
- Improved trabecular pattern visibility in the metaphyseal regions

Possible artifacts:
- CLAHE can amplify background noise, especially in the low-signal areas.
  The current background noise std is ~1.05, so with clip limit 2.0, noise
  amplification should be moderate.
- Tile boundaries may show visible seams if the tile size is too small.
  64x64 should be large enough to avoid this for this image size.
- Over-enhancement in homogeneous bone regions may create artificial texture.

Comparison with original:
View side-by-side with the original at identical display settings. CLAHE
should make more structures visible without distorting proportions. Any
structure visible in the CLAHE version should be verifiable as a real signal
change in the original (not pure noise amplification).

Actionable steps:
1. Convert to grayscale (luminance channel)
2. Apply CLAHE (clip_limit=2.0, tile_grid_size=64x64)
3. Save as ID002-clahe.png

PROPOSAL 3: Unsharp Mask for Cortical Edge Enhancement
============================================================

Question being answered:
Are there subtle cortical disruptions, hairline fracture lines, or irregularities
along bone margins that are present but visually suppressed by the natural
frequency response of the X-ray detector?

Method:
Apply unsharp masking: subtract a Gaussian-blurred version (sigma=2.0 pixels)
from the original, then add a scaled version of the difference (amount=1.5)
back to the original. Formula: result = original + amount * (original - blurred).

Why it helps:
The maximum gradient magnitude in this image is 77.47, indicating sharp edges
exist but many cortical boundaries may have gradient magnitudes in the 5-15
range, which the visual system can miss in the presence of surrounding bone
texture. Unsharp masking amplifies these mid-frequency edge components.

Expected revelation:
- Sharper definition of the anterior cortex of the distal humerus (the
  supracondylar region, a common fracture site in pediatric patients)
- Enhanced visibility of the olecranon cortex and the radiocapitellar joint margins
- Potential identification of subtle cortical step-offs or buckle fractures

Possible artifacts:
- Halo effect: bright edges may show dark halos on one side and bright halos
  on the other, creating artificial step-off appearances.
- Noise amplification along high-frequency noise patterns.
- The sigma parameter is critical: too small amplifies noise, too large misses
  the fine fractures we are looking for.

Comparison with original:
Any cortical irregularity visible in the sharpened image MUST be present in the
original as a measurable intensity change (even if subtle). To validate, draw a
line profile across the suspicious area in both images — the sharpened version
will show a steeper gradient, but the underlying intensity change must exist in
the original.

Actionable steps:
1. Apply Gaussian blur (sigma=2.0) to grayscale
2. Compute difference: original - blurred
3. Add 1.5 * difference back to original
4. Clip to [0, 255], save as ID002-sharp.png

PROPOSAL 4: Soft Tissue Window (Focused Contrast for Fat Pads and
Swelling Assessment)
============================================================

Question being answered:
Is there evidence of elbow joint effusion, soft tissue swelling, or displaced
fat pads (anterior "sail sign" or posterior fat pad sign), which are key indirect
signs of occult fracture in pediatric elbow injuries?

Method:
Apply a narrow window centered on the soft tissue intensity range. Based on
analysis, soft tissue in the anterior fat pad region has mean 170.3 (std 46.9)
and the posterior region mean 42.6 (std 59.5). Map pixel values in the range
[30, 200] linearly to [0, 255], clipping values outside this range.

Why it helps:
In a standard X-ray presentation, bone (219.5 mean) dominates the display
dynamic range, leaving soft tissue details (which vary in a much narrower
intensity band) under-exposed. By remapping only the soft tissue range to
full display contrast, subtle density differences become visible.

Expected revelation:
- Anterior fat pad visibility: normally the anterior (coronoid) fat pad may be
  barely visible; elevation or widening suggests joint effusion
- Posterior fat pad visibility: a visible posterior fat pad is always abnormal
  and strongly correlates with fracture
- Overall soft tissue swelling pattern around the elbow
- Soft tissue foreign bodies or calcifications

Possible artifacts:
- Bone cortex will be clipped to white and lose all internal detail
- The narrow window may amplify subtle noise in soft tissue, making noise
  appear as soft tissue texture
- Compression artifacts or processing gradients may be exaggerated

Comparison with original:
This window should ONLY be used to identify structures of interest, which are
then verified on the original image. A fat pad that is "visible" in this window
must correspond to a real density difference in the original (check with a line
profile). The window does not create new information; it redistributes existing
dynamic range.

Actionable steps:
1. Compute window: values [30, 200] map to [0, 255]
2. Clip below 30 to black, above 200 to white
3. Save as ID002-soft-tissue-window.png

PROPOSAL 5: Line Intensity Profiles Along Key Anatomical Axes
============================================================

Question being answered:
Do quantitative intensity profiles reveal measurable irregularities along bone
cortex, through the joint space, or across the physes that support or contradict
visual impressions?

Method:
Extract pixel intensity profiles along four specific transects:
  a) Distal humerus: horizontal line through the supracondylar region (~row 380)
     from column 800 to 1000, to assess anterior and posterior cortical integrity
  b) Elbow joint space: horizontal line through the trochlear notch (~row 450)
     from column 800 to 1000, to measure the anterior humeroulnar interval and
     detect joint space narrowing or widening
  c) Proximal radius: horizontal line through the radial neck (~row 470)
     from column 850 to 1050, to assess radial head/neck cortex
  d) Distal humerus physis: horizontal line through the growth plate (~row 400)
     from column 820 to 980, to check for physeal asymmetry or widening

Why it helps:
Visual inspection is subjective and influenced by display conditions. A numeric
profile provides a reproducible, quantifiable trace of bone cortex position,
width, and density. A cortical fracture would appear as a localized dip or
irregularity in the profile that is measurable rather than just "visible."

Expected revelation:
- Quantitative measurement of the anterior humeroulnar interval (normal ~2-4mm
  on lateral view); widening >4mm suggests effusion
- Identification of subtle cortical step-offs not obvious by eye
- Symmetry assessment of the physis: asymmetric widening may suggest Salter-Harris
  injury
- Baseline data for comparing with contralateral side if available

Possible artifacts:
- Profile line placement is critical: a 1-pixel offset can cross from cortex
  into medullary bone and give misleading values
- Partial volume effect: if the patient's arm was not perfectly in the lateral
  plane, some cortex will appear thicker or thinner than actual
- The profiles represent a single pixel-thin transect; a wider transect (mean
  over 5 pixels) is more robust

Comparison with original:
Plot the profiles and overlay their positions on the original image for
verification. Any feature in the profile should be visually identifiable on
the original at the corresponding location.

Actionable steps:
1. Define the four transect lines with their pixel coordinates
2. Extract intensity values along each line
3. Also compute 5-pixel-wide running mean for robustness
4. Plot profiles (intensity vs. pixel position) with annotations

PROPOSAL 6: High-Pass Filter Decomposition (Laplacian Detail Extraction)
============================================================

Question being answered:
Does high-frequency analysis reveal detail structures (fine trabecular pattern,
micro-fracture lines, subtle periosteal reactions) that are present in the image
data but below the visual detection threshold?

Method:
Apply Laplacian filtering (kernel: center 4, neighbors -1) to the grayscale
image. The Laplacian responds to second derivatives — regions where intensity
curvature is high (fine edges, transitions). Display the absolute value of the
result, inverted (so high-frequency detail is bright on dark). Also compute
a Gaussian-Laplacian (LoG) at two scales (sigma=1.0 and sigma=3.0) to
separate fine detail from coarser structural features.

Why it helps:
The overall gradient magnitude in this image is low (mean 0.84), indicating
most of the image is smooth. The Laplacian will be near-zero in smooth regions
and spike at edges and fine features. This is a form of band-pass filtering
that suppresses both the low-frequency bone/soft-tissue bulk and the
high-frequency noise, isolating the diagnostic mid-high frequency range.

Expected revelation:
- Fine trabecular pattern in the metaphyseal bone
- Subtle periosteal reaction (a thin line of new bone formation)
- Micro-fracture lines that may not reach the visual threshold in standard
  viewing
- The relationship between bone trabecular architecture and the physis

Possible artifacts:
- The Laplacian responds equally to noise and real detail; both will appear
  as bright spots
- Double-edge effect: each edge produces two responses (one positive, one
  negative), creating pairs of lines
- The filter is highly sensitive to the sigma parameter; wrong values will
  either miss features or be overwhelmed by noise

Comparison with original:
Treat this as a hypothesis generator only. Any feature flagged by the
Laplacian must be investigated on the original image. The Laplacian does
not provide diagnostic confirmation; it provides targets for focused
inspection. A practical approach: add a transparent Laplacian overlay
(blended at 30% opacity) on the original to highlight areas for review.

Actionable steps:
1. Apply Laplacian filter to grayscale
2. Apply LoG at sigma=1.0 (fine detail) and sigma=3.0 (coarser structure)
3. Take absolute values, scale to [0, 255]
4. Save each as separate images and as a blended overlay
5. Save as ID002-laplacian.png, ID002-log1.png, ID002-log3.png

PROPOSAL 7: Physis (Growth Plate) Quantitative Assessment
============================================================

Question being answered:
Given that this is a pediatric elbow, is the distal humeral physis normal in
width, symmetry, and density, or does it show signs of Salter-Harris injury,
physiolysis, or asymmetric growth disturbance?

Method:
1. Manually or semi-automatically segment the distal humeral physis region
   (the radiolucent gap between the humeral metaphysis and epiphysis, visible
   in the upper portion of the joint)
2. Measure the physis width at 5 equidistant points across its extent
   (medial to lateral in this lateral projection, i.e., anterior to posterior
   aspect)
3. Compute the intensity gradient across the physis at each point to assess
   how sharp the metaphyseal and epiphyseal boundaries are
4. Compare the anterior vs. posterior physis width ratio (normal ratio ~1.0,
   ratio >1.5 may suggest injury)

Why it helps:
Salter-Harris type I fractures (through the physis alone) can be extremely
difficult to see on standard viewing. The injury manifests as physis widening
that may be only 1-2mm and easily missed. Quantitative measurement is more
sensitive than visual estimation.

Expected revelation:
- Quantitative physis width (normal distal humeral physis ~2-4mm depending on
  age)
- Asymmetry between anterior and posterior aspects
- Whether the physis boundary is sharp (normal) or irregular (suggestive of
  disruption)
- Baseline for comparison with the contralateral elbow

Possible artifacts:
- Measurement accuracy depends on correct identification of the physis
  boundaries; partial volume effect may blur the transition
- On a lateral view, the physis appears foreshortened compared to AP view
- The measurement is in pixel units and requires knowledge of the pixel-to-mm
  ratio for clinical interpretation

Comparison with original:
The measured physis boundaries should be marked directly on the original image
so the reader can verify each measurement point. The numeric measurements
should be displayed as annotations alongside the image.

Actionable steps:
1. Identify physis region in the distal humerus
2. Place 5 measurement transects perpendicular to the physis
3. At each transect, find the two intensity peaks (metaphysis and epiphysis)
   and measure the gap between them
4. Compute mean width, anterior/posterior ratio
5. Annotate findings on the original image
6. Save as ID002-physis-assessment.png with measurement report

PROPOSAL 8: Global Histogram Analysis and Display Optimization
============================================================

Question being answered:
Is the image being displayed at optimal contrast and brightness, or would
adjusting the window/level reveal hidden detail?

Method:
1. Compute the full 256-bin histogram of the grayscale image
2. Identify the intensity percentiles at 1%, 5%, 95%, and 99% to determine
   the effective content range
3. Based on the histogram, compute an optimal linear window/level
4. Generate three display variants:
   a) Window set to [p1, p99] (content-adaptive, excludes outliers)
   b) Window set to [p5, p95] (inner content, higher contrast)
   c) Window optimized for bone detail: [180, 240] (narrow window on bright
      bone range)

Why it helps:
The current image uses the full 0-254 range, but the anatomically relevant
content occupies a much narrower band. Background is near 0, and the
distribution shows that most non-black pixels are in the 50-200 range
(12.1% in 50-150, 2.6% in 150-200). A content-adaptive window would shift
the display to maximize contrast within the actual anatomical signal range.

Expected revelation:
- Whether the current presentation is optimal or suboptimal
- Potential loss of detail in shadows (low values) or highlights (bone)
- An evidence-based recommendation for the best display settings for this
  specific image

Possible artifacts:
- Overly narrow windows will clip legitimate signal and lose context
- Window/level changes do not add information; they redistribute existing
  contrast. A narrow bone window will lose all soft tissue detail
- Display optimization is subjective: the "optimal" window depends on what
  the reader is looking for

Comparison with original:
Present all three optimized variants alongside the original, using identical
display size. The reader can verify whether structures of interest are better
or worse represented in each variant.

Actionable steps:
1. Compute histogram and percentiles
2. Generate three windowed variants
3. Display in a 2x2 grid with the original
4. Save as ID002-window-comparison.png with histogram plot
