PROPOSAL 1 — Tight Crop to Anatomical Content
==============================================================================

What to do:
  Crop the image to the bounding box of non-background content. Identify
  background as columns where the row-wise mean intensity stays below a
  threshold (e.g., pixel value 10) for the full column height. The left
  boundary is where columns first consistently exceed this threshold
  (approximately column 720-750). Preserve the full vertical extent but
  trim the black left margin. Save the cropped result.

(Q) Where is the actual radiographic data?
  The image wastes ~45% of its horizontal real estate on black background.
  Cropping concentrates pixel resolution on the anatomy.

(W) Why cropping helps:
  Removing the dead zone gives a more manageable aspect ratio and allows
  subsequent analyses to focus exclusively on signal-bearing pixels. It
  also simplifies histogram analysis by removing the overwhelming mass
  of zero-value background pixels.

(E) Expected result:
  A cropped image of approximately 850-900 x 1200 pixels showing only the
  arm, elbow, and forearm. The histogram of the cropped image will be
  significantly less skewed, with the background peak greatly reduced.

(A) Caveats:
  Do not crop too aggressively on the right edge where the humeral shaft
  exits the frame — any partial anatomy is still relevant. Preserve the
  full vertical extent even if the bottom includes partial wrist structures.
  Cropping alone does not improve any per-pixel information; it only
  redistributes existing pixels.

(C) Comparison:
  Overlay the cropped result on the original at full size to verify no
  anatomical content was lost. The cropped image should contain all
  visible bone and soft-tissue structures.

PROPOSAL 2 — Soft-Tissue Window and Bone Window Dual Contrast Stretching
==============================================================================

What to do:
  Produce two independently windowed versions of the cropped image:

  (a) SOFT-TISSUE WINDOW:
      Map the intensity range [20, 130] linearly to [0, 255]. Clip
      values below 20 to black and above 130 to white.

  (b) BONE WINDOW:
      Map the intensity range [150, 254] linearly to [0, 255]. Clip
      values below 150 to black and above 254 to white.

  Save both versions as separate images.

(Q) Can we simultaneously assess soft tissue detail and cortical bone?
  The original image maps the full 0-254 range to display, which means
  soft tissue (intensity ~30-127) occupies only a narrow band of perceived
  contrast, while bone cortex (180-254) is compressed at the bright end.
  Neither region is optimally visualized.

(W) Why dual windowing helps:
  A soft-tissue window expands the narrow 30-130 range across the full
  display range, making fat pads, muscle boundaries, and potential swelling
  visible. A bone window compresses the dark soft tissue to black and
  spreads the cortical/trabecular range, revealing subtle density changes
  in bone structure. Radiologists routinely switch between windows; here
  we produce both for static review.

(E) Expected results:
  - Soft-tissue window: The anterior and posterior fat pads around the
    elbow joint become clearly visible. Muscle bulk around the upper arm
    and anterior forearm becomes discernible. Any joint effusion would
    manifest as soft tissue elevation in the posterior or anterior aspects.
  - Bone window: Cortical thickness variations, trabecular pattern detail,
    and subtle cortical irregularities (hairline fracture lines) become
    visible. The distinction between dense cortex and cancellous bone
    improves.

(A) Caveats:
  - Linear mapping amplifies noise within the selected range. Small
    intensity fluctuations that were invisible become prominent. A mild
    Gaussian smoothing before windowing may be needed if noise is visible.
  - The window boundaries (20-130 for soft tissue, 150-254 for bone)
    are starting estimates. The actual optimal boundaries depend on the
    histogram of the anatomical content only (after cropping).
  - Windowing is a DISPLAY transformation, not a data transformation.
    No new information is created; existing contrast is redistributed.
    Over-interpretation of amplified noise is the main risk.

(C) Comparison:
  Always interpret the windowed images alongside the original. Any feature
  highlighted in a window should be cross-referenced with the original
  to confirm it is a real structure and not an amplification of random
  noise. Use the original as the ground truth and the windowed versions
  as hypothesis generators.

PROPOSAL 3 — Anterior and Posterior Fat Pad Visualization
==============================================================================

What to do:
  On the soft-tissue windowed image from Proposal 2(a), specifically
  examine the regions anterior and posterior to the elbow joint:

  (a) Define an anterior region of interest (ROI) in the coronoid fossa
      area: approximately rows 200-350, columns at the anterior aspect
      of the distal humerus (relative to the cropped image).

  (b) Define a posterior ROI in the olecranon fossa area: approximately
      rows 200-400, columns at the posterior aspect of the distal humerus.

  (c) For each ROI, generate a horizontal intensity profile across the
      region, and compute the mean and maximum soft-tissue intensity.

  (d) Mark the anterior fat pad as a triangular radiolucent region in the
      coronoid fossa if visible. Assess whether it is elevated (sail sign).

  (e) Assess whether a posterior fat pad is visible at all.

(Q) Is there an occult intra-articular fracture suggested by fat pad signs?
  In elbow imaging, the anterior fat pad may be visible normally (as a
  subtle triangular lucency in the coronoid fossa). An elevated anterior
  fat pad ("sail sign") is always abnormal and indicates joint effusion,
  most commonly from an occult supracondylar or radial neck fracture.
  A visible posterior fat pad is ALWAYS abnormal and strongly indicates
  intra-articular pathology.

(W) Why this specific analysis:
  The fat pad sign is one of the most sensitive indicators of occult
  fracture in the elbow. Up to 90% of patients with a visible posterior
  fat pad have an underlying fracture. Even a positive anterior sail
  sign carries a high positive predictive value. This analysis is
  purpose-built for elbow imaging and is not a generic enhancement.

(E) Expected results:
  - Normal: anterior fat pad may be barely visible as a thin triangular
    lucency; posterior fat pad is NOT visible (hidden by the olecranon
    fossa).
  - Abnormal (sail sign): anterior fat pad is clearly elevated into a
    triangular shape protruding from the coronoid fossa.
  - Abnormal (posterior fat pad sign): any visible radiolucent triangle
    posterior to the distal humerus. This is always pathological.

(A) Caveats:
  - The posterior fat pad sign is definitive when present, but absence
    does NOT rule out fracture (a negative posterior fat pad sign can
    coexist with fracture if the effusion is small).
  - The lateral projection is actually the BEST view for fat pad
    assessment, which is fortunate for this image.
  - The soft-tissue window from Proposal 2 is essential; the original
    image may not show enough soft-tissue contrast.
  - A small normal fat pad vs. a pathologically elevated one can be
    subtle. Quantitative measurement (angle of the fat pad triangle,
    or distance from the anterior cortex) adds objectivity.

(C) Comparison:
  Compare the fat pad visualization against the original image's soft
  tissue region. The soft-tissue window should reveal structures that
  are barely perceptible or invisible in the original. Confirm that any
  identified fat pad corresponds to a real anatomical structure and not
  a noise artifact by checking its shape and location.

PROPOSAL 4 — Cortical Edge Gradient Analysis for Fracture Detection
==============================================================================

What to do:
  (a) Compute the Sobel gradient magnitude and direction maps for the
      bone window image.

  (b) Trace the expected cortical margins of the key bones:
      - Anterior and posterior cortex of the distal humerus (supracondylar
        region)
      - Anterior cortex of the ulnar shaft and olecranon
      - Posterior cortex of the ulnar shaft
      - Lateral cortex of the radial shaft and radial head

  (c) For each traced cortical line, extract the intensity profile
      perpendicular to the cortex (3-5 pixels on either side).

  (d) Identify locations where the expected sharp cortical edge (high
      gradient magnitude, typically >80 in pixel intensity over 2-3
      pixels) is interrupted, shows a step-off, or has an unexpected
      dip (radiolucent line within the bright cortex).

  (e) Flag any discontinuity for visual review.

(Q) Are there subtle cortical disruptions or step-offs that indicate
    nondisplaced or hairline fractures?
  Fracture lines appear as radiolucent (dark) lines interrupting the
  continuous bright cortical margin. In a lateral elbow view, key
  fracture sites include the radial head, olecranon, coronoid process,
  and supracondylar humerus.

(W) Why gradient analysis helps:
  Cortical bone produces the highest intensity gradients in an X-ray.
  A smooth, continuous cortex shows a consistent high-gradient band.
  A fracture line — even a nondisplaced hairline fracture — introduces
  a localized disruption: a brief drop in intensity (the radiolucent
  fracture gap) within the cortex, or an unexpected change in the
  gradient direction (a cortical step-off). Quantitative gradient
  analysis is more sensitive to these subtle disruptions than the
  naked eye, especially in an 8-bit image with limited contrast.

(E) Expected results:
  - A gradient magnitude map showing bright lines at all cortical
    boundaries and dark interiors of medullary canals.
  - Along each cortical trace, a smooth gradient profile with occasional
    minor fluctuations (normal anatomical variation, noise).
  - If a fracture is present, a sharp, localized dip in the cortical
    intensity profile or an unexpected gap in the high-gradient band.
  - A list of flagged locations with their coordinates, gradient values,
    and the nature of the disruption.

(A) Caveats:
  - Sobel gradients are sensitive to noise. In the soft-tissue/bone
    interface, gradients from soft-tissue structures can be confused
    with cortical edges. The bone window helps by suppressing soft
    tissue to black.
  - The radial head on a lateral view is often superimposed on the
    capitellum and trochlea, making isolated cortical tracing difficult.
    Expect some ambiguity in the radial head region.
  - A cortical step-off could be a normal anatomical landmark (e.g., the
    trochlear notch contour) rather than a fracture. Each flag must be
    anatomically contextualized.
  - The 8-bit quantization can introduce small step-artifacts in the
    gradient that mimic a cortical disruption. A minimum displacement
    threshold (e.g., >15 intensity units over 2+ pixels) is needed.

(C) Comparison:
  Map each flagged location back to the original image (not the windowed
  version) and visually inspect at maximum zoom. A real fracture line
  will be visible in the original image, albeit faintly. If a flagged
  location shows nothing abnormal in the original, it is likely noise
  or an anatomical variant.

PROPOSAL 5 — Anterior Humeral Line and Capitellar Line Analysis
==============================================================================

What to do:
  (a) Trace the anterior cortex of the distal humeral shaft as a line
      from approximately 3 cm proximal to the joint extending distally.

  (b) Project this line forward (extend it past the joint) and determine
      which third of the capitellum it intersects: proximal, middle,
      or distal third.

  (c) Trace a line through the center of the capitellum (the capitellar
      line) and determine its angle relative to the distal humeral
      articular surface.

  (d) Measure the carrying angle in the lateral projection (this is the
      lateral equivalent — the angle between the humeral shaft axis and
      the forearm axis).

(Q) Is the elbow properly aligned, or is there a subtle subluxation or
    posterior angulation suggestive of a supracondylar fracture-dislocation?
  The anterior humeral line is the single most important alignment
  criterion in lateral elbow imaging. In a normal elbow, it should
  pass through the middle third of the capitellum. If it passes through
  the anterior (proximal) third or anterior to the capitellum entirely,
  it indicates posterior angulation, most commonly from a displaced
  supracondylar fracture.

(W) Why this geometric analysis:
  Supracondylar fractures are among the most common pediatric elbow
  fractures. Even a small posterior angulation (>10-20 degrees) is
  clinically significant and may require intervention. The anterior
  humeral line provides a simple, objective measurement that is
  reproducible and does not depend on image windowing.

(E) Expected results:
  - A traced line along the anterior humeral cortex.
  - A projected line extending through the capitellum.
  - A categorical assessment: the line intersects the proximal, middle,
    or distal third of the capitellum.
  - Quantitative: the angle of the anterior humeral line relative to the
    humeral shaft axis, and the perpendicular distance from the line to
    the center of the capitellum.

(A) Caveats:
  - This analysis assumes the image is a TRUE lateral view. If the
    elbow is rotated (even slightly), the anterior humeral line will
    not be in the correct plane, and the analysis is invalid. The
    superimposition of the humeral condyles on a true lateral view
    should confirm adequate rotation.
  - The anterior cortex may be curved in the supracondylar region. The
    line should be fitted to the mid-shaft portion, not the very distal
    metaphysis where natural curvature exists.
  - In a pediatric patient with an open physis, the line should be
    extended to the ossification center of the capitellum, not the
    entire capitellum.

(C) Comparison:
  Overlay the traced line on the original image to verify that the
  anterior cortex was correctly identified. Compare the capitellar
  intersection point visually. A normal intersection (middle third) is
  reassuring; any deviation should be cross-checked against the original
  for potential subtle angulation.

PROPOSAL 6 — Frequency-Domain Noise Characterization and Artifact Check
==============================================================================

What to do:
  (a) Apply a 2D Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to the cropped anatomical
      region (or to a representative sub-region of the bone area).

  (b) Compute the radial power spectrum: average the FFT magnitude at
      each radial frequency bin.

  (c) Look for periodic peaks in the power spectrum that would indicate
      grid lines, moiré patterns, or compression artifacts.

  (d) Assess the high-frequency falloff to characterize the spatial
      resolution limit of the image.

  (e) Check for anisotropic frequency content (directional bias), which
      would suggest motion blur or scanning artifacts.

(Q) What is the frequency content of this image, and are there any
    periodic artifacts that could interfere with diagnostic interpretation?
  If the image was processed through a pipeline that introduced grid
  lines, compression artifacts, or scanning patterns, these would appear
  as periodic signals in the frequency domain. They could be mistaken
  for fracture lines or cortical irregularities.

(W) Why frequency analysis:
  Periodic artifacts are invisible or subtle in the spatial domain but
  appear as sharp peaks in the frequency domain. Characterizing the
  noise spectrum also tells us about the effective spatial resolution
  of the image — the point at which signal falls below noise. This
  determines the smallest detectable feature (e.g., the minimum
  fracture line width that can be reliably detected).

(E) Expected results:
  - A power spectrum plot showing the expected ~1/f^2 falloff typical
    of natural images, with no sharp periodic peaks.
  - Identification of the cutoff frequency where signal drops below
    the noise floor, giving an estimate of the smallest resolvable
    feature size.
  - Confirmation of isotropic frequency content (no directional bias),
    ruling out motion blur.
  - If artifacts are present, their spatial frequency and orientation
    will be identified.

(A) Caveats:
  - Edge effects from cropping can introduce low-frequency artifacts in
    the FFT. Apply a Hann window to the image before the FFT to mitigate
    this.
  - The analysis is informative but not diagnostic. It characterizes
    image quality but does not identify pathology.
  - A smooth power spectrum confirms the ABSENCE of artifacts, which is
    reassuring but not particularly actionable.

(C) Comparison:
  If periodic artifacts are found, attempt to filter them out and
  compare the filtered image with the original to see if any features
  were previously obscured by artifact patterns. Otherwise, this is a
  quality assessment that informs confidence in all other findings.

PROPOSAL 7 — Regional Bone Density Heatmap and Comparison Profile
==============================================================================

What to do:
  (a) Create a binary bone mask by thresholding the bone window image
      at a level that separates cortical/trabecular bone from soft
      tissue and background (e.g., pixel value > 160 in the original
      scale, adjusted for the windowed range).

  (b) Define five regions of interest along the bones:
      (i)   Distal humeral metaphysis (proximal to the joint)
      (ii)  Distal humeral epiphysis (condylar region)
      (iii) Proximal ulna (olecranon + coronoid)
      (iv)  Radial head and neck
      (v)   Mid-shaft radius and ulna

  (c) For each ROI, compute the mean, median, and standard deviation
      of pixel intensities. Also compute the bone area fraction (ratio
      of pixels above threshold to total ROI area).

  (d) Create a color-coded heatmap overlay on the image where bone
      regions are colored by their relative density: warm colors for
      higher density, cool colors for lower density.

  (e) Generate a summary table of density values for all ROIs.

(Q) Is bone density uniform across the visible skeleton, or are there
    focal areas of osteopenia, sclerosis, or lytic lesions?
  Focal changes in bone density can indicate pathology: osteolytic
  lesions (tumor, infection), focal sclerosis (healing fracture,
  osteoid osteoma), or regional osteopenia (disuse, reflex sympathetic
  dystrophy).

(W) Why density mapping helps:
  Visual assessment of bone density is subjective and influenced by
  viewing conditions and fatigue. A quantitative, region-by-region
  comparison provides objective data. An unexpected density difference
  between symmetrically corresponding regions or between adjacent bone
  regions flags areas for closer inspection.

(E) Expected results:
  - A table of quantitative density values for each ROI.
  - A heatmap visualization showing density variation across the
    skeleton.
  - Identification of any ROI whose density falls outside 2 standard
    deviations of the group mean, flagging it for review.
  - Expected normal pattern: cortical bone (shaft) > trabecular bone
    (metaphysis/epiphysis) > soft tissue > background.

(A) Caveats:
  - Partial volume effects: thin cortical bone may not reach the full
    pixel intensity of thick cortex, even if mineral density is the
    same. A thin cortical shell (e.g., radial head) will have a lower
    mean intensity than a thick shaft, purely due to geometry.
  - Beam hardening and X-ray attenuation through varying amounts of
    overlying tissue can introduce apparent density differences that
    are acquisition artifacts, not anatomical.
  - The 8-bit quantization limits the precision of density measurements.
    A 16-bit DICOM source would be much more reliable for this analysis.
  - Be cautious about flagging the distal humeral epiphysis as "low
    density" — it is predominantly cancellous bone and should be lower
    than the diaphysis.

(C) Comparison:
  Any flagged density abnormality should be examined in the original
  image and the bone window. A true focal lesion will have visible
  anatomical correlates. A density difference that has no visible
  correlate is likely a measurement artifact (partial volume, beam
  hardening, or quantization).

PROPOSAL 8 — Radial Head Fracture-Specific Analysis
==============================================================================

What to do:
  (a) On the lateral view, the radial head is seen articulating with the
      capitellum of the humerus. Define an ROI centered on the radial
      head (approximately rows 180-320, columns corresponding to the
      lateral aspect of the proximal forearm in the cropped image).

  (b) Apply high-contrast edge enhancement (unsharp masking with a small
      kernel, sigma ~1 pixel) to this ROI.

  (c) Trace the expected circular/oval outline of the radial head cortex
      using an edge-detection algorithm (Canny or active contour/snake).

  (d) Compute the circularity and smoothness of the traced outline. Flag
      any locations where the outline deviates from a smooth curve — these
      could represent a cortical step-off from a fracture.

  (e) Examine the "radial head line" — a line tangent to the anterior
      cortex of the radial head should pass through the capitellum.
      Deviation suggests radial head subluxation or fracture-dislocation.

  (f) Check for a joint effusion that could suggest an occult radial head
      fracture (Meyer's triad: radial head fracture, coronoid fracture,
      and lateral collateral ligament injury).

(Q) Is there a radial head fracture?
  Radial head fractures are the most common elbow fracture in adults
  (5-15% of all adult fractures). On a lateral view alone, nondisplaced
  fractures are notoriously difficult to see because the fracture line
  is often perpendicular to the X-ray beam. However, indirect signs
  (cortical step-off, subtle irregularity, joint effusion) can be
  detected.

(W) Why this focused analysis:
  The radial head is a small, complex structure that is often obscured
  by superimposed anatomy on the lateral view. A dedicated analysis
  targeting this specific region with edge enhancement and geometric
  assessment has a better chance of detecting subtle pathology than a
  general cortical review.

(E) Expected results:
  - A traced outline of the radial head with quantified smoothness.
  - Identification of any cortical irregularities > 2 pixels in width.
  - Assessment of the radial head to capitellum alignment.
  - If a fracture is suspected, the location and orientation of the
    fracture line will be indicated.

(A) Caveats:
  - This is the WEAK point of a lateral-only view. A nondisplaced
    radial head fracture with the fracture line perpendicular to the
    beam will be invisible on X-ray. The absence of findings does NOT
    rule out a radial head fracture.
  - The radial head cortex is thin, and the edge detection may pick up
    the overlying soft tissue edge rather than the bone cortex.
  - Normal anatomical variants exist in radial head shape; not every
    irregularity is a fracture.
  - Edge enhancement amplifies noise, which can create false step-offs.
    Use a minimum size threshold for flagged irregularities.

(C) Comparison:
  Map any flagged irregularity back to the original image and the bone
  window. A real fracture will be consistent across both views. Compare
  with the clinical context — a positive fat pad sign (Proposal 3) plus
  a subtle radial head irregularity is highly suggestive of a radial
  head fracture even if the fracture line itself is not visible.

PROPOSAL 9 — Interosseous Space Width Profile Along the Forearm
==============================================================================

What to do:
  (a) For each row in the forearm region (from the elbow joint to the
      distal visible extent), find the rightmost column of the ulnar
      cortex and the leftmost column of the radial cortex.

  (b) Compute the interosseous distance (in pixels) at each row.

  (c) Plot the interosseous distance as a function of distance from the
      elbow joint.

  (d) Compare the profile to the expected normal pattern: the interosseous
      space should be narrowest at the proximal radioulnar joint and
      gradually widen distally, maintaining a consistent, smooth curve.

  (e) Flag any abrupt narrowing or widening of the interosseous space,
      which could indicate a forearm fracture with displacement or a
      proximal radioulnar joint dislocation.

(Q) Is there an abnormal narrowing or widening of the interosseous
    space that would suggest a forearm fracture or dislocation?
  The interosseous space between the radius and ulna should maintain a
  consistent width. A sudden change in width at a particular level
  strongly suggests a fracture with cortical displacement. This is
  a sensitive indicator even when the fracture line itself is subtle.

(W) Why this quantitative profile:
  Visual assessment of the interosseous space is influenced by the
  perspective and projection. A row-by-row quantitative measurement
  provides an objective, reproducible profile. The expected smooth
  widening curve is a strong prior — any deviation is immediately
  suspicious.

(E) Expected results:
  - A plot of interosseous distance vs. distance from the elbow.
  - A smooth, monotonically increasing curve in the normal case.
  - If a fracture is present, a step change in the curve at the level
    of the fracture.
  - Quantitative measurement of the maximum deviation from the expected
    curve.

(A) Caveats:
  - On a lateral view, the radius and ulna are partially superimposed,
    making cortical edge detection ambiguous in some regions. The
    interosseous space may not be clearly visible at all levels.
  - The forearm bones are not perfectly parallel — normal anatomy
    includes a gentle convergence. The expected widening pattern must
    account for this.
  - The analysis is most reliable in the mid-forearm where the bones
    are best separated. Near the elbow and wrist, superimposition
    increases.

(C) Comparison:
  Overlay the measured interosseous distances on the original image as
  horizontal markers. Verify that the edge detection correctly identified
  the cortical margins. A flagged narrowing should correspond to a
  visible change in bone alignment in the original image.

PROPOSAL 10 — Multi-Scale Laplacian Pyramid for Feature Scale Analysis
==============================================================================

What to do:
  (a) Build a 4-level Laplacian pyramid from the cropped image:
      - Level 0: Gaussian-blurred version (sigma ~8 pixels) — captures
        large-scale anatomy (bone outlines, soft tissue bulk)
      - Level 1: Difference between levels 0 and 1 — captures
        medium-scale features (joint spaces, cortical thickness)
      - Level 2: Difference between levels 1 and 2 — captures
        fine features (trabecular pattern, small cortical irregularities)
      - Level 3: Difference between levels 2 and 3 — captures
        finest details (potential fracture lines, noise)

  (b) Display each level as a separate image, with appropriate contrast
      stretching for each.

  (c) At each level, flag regions where the absolute intensity exceeds
      2 standard deviations of the level's global distribution.

  (d) Correlate flags across levels: a feature that appears at multiple
      scales is likely anatomical; a feature that appears only at the
      finest scale is likely noise.

(Q) What features exist at different spatial scales, and which are
    anatomically significant vs. noise?
  Pathological features like fracture lines, subtle cortical irregularities,
  and early lytic lesions span specific spatial scales. Noise and artifacts
    tend to be either very high frequency (fine-grained noise) or very
    low frequency (illumination variation). A multi-scale decomposition
    separates features by their spatial frequency, making it easier to
    identify meaningful structures.

(W) Why a Laplacian pyramid helps:
  The human visual system naturally processes images at multiple scales.
  A Laplacian pyramid provides a computational analog: each level isolates
  a specific range of spatial frequencies. This is more informative than
  a single edge-detection pass, which conflates all scales. A real
  fracture line will typically span both medium and fine scales (it has
  a characteristic width and length), while noise is mostly fine-scale
  only.

(E) Expected results:
  - Four pyramid level images, each revealing different features.
  - Level 0: Overall bone anatomy, major alignment.
  - Level 1: Joint spaces, cortical margins, soft-tissue boundaries.
  - Level 2: Trabecular pattern, small cortical irregularities,
    potential nondisplaced fracture lines.
  - Level 3: Fine detail and noise.
  - A set of flagged features at each level, with cross-level
    correlation to distinguish real features from noise.

(A) Caveats:
  - The pyramid decomposition is a linear operation. It redistributes
    information across levels but does not create new information.
  - Features that span multiple scales may appear fragmented across
    levels, making them harder to recognize than in the original image.
  - The choice of the number of levels and the Gaussian kernel size
    affects the scale separation. A 4-level pyramid is a reasonable
    default for the resolution of this image.
  - Interpretation requires visual inspection at each level. Automated
    thresholding alone is unreliable.

(C) Comparison:
  The key insight from the pyramid is cross-scale correlation. A feature
  that appears in levels 1 AND 2 is a real structure at a meaningful scale.
  A feature that appears only in level 3 is likely noise. Map the
  cross-scale confirmed features back to the original image for final
  interpretation. The original image remains the ground truth; the pyramid
  is a tool for systematic feature discovery.
