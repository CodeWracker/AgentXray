PURPOSE: Assess whether periosteal reaction or callus formation is present, to differentiate acute from subacute or chronic fracture.
OBSERVATIONS: The magenta-outlined "Periosteal Gradient Map" applies edge-enhancement to the mid-to-distal forearm, exaggerating linear densities along the cortical surfaces of the radius and ulna. The side-by-side image (08_periosteal_sidebyside.png) confirms that most of these linear patterns correspond to normal cortical margins made more prominent by the gradient filter, rather than distinct layers of periosteal new bone. In the processed image, the cortex of both bones appears as multiple parallel bright lines, but these lack the concentric, lamellated appearance of true periosteal reaction. The fracture zone itself shows no bridging density or callus mass.
RELEVANT FINDINGS: Absence of callus formation at the fracture site. No layered (onion-skin, sunburst, or solid) periosteal reaction on either the radius or ulna. Fracture margins remain sharp and angular without the rounding or sclerosis that accompanies older injuries. The interosseous space is preserved without reactive ossification.
RELATION TO ORIGINAL IMAGE: The original radiograph (ID003-xray.png) independently shows no callus, no periosteal reaction, and sharp fracture lines. The absence of chronicity markers is not an artifact of processing; it is the actual radiographic appearance of the specimen.
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: The gradient processing amplifies normal cortical edges, which could be misinterpreted as periosteal new bone by a reader unfamiliar with the technique. The multiple parallel lines along the diaphyseal cortex in the processed image are an edge-detection artifact, not multiple layers of periosteal bone. No other processing artifacts materially alter the fracture appearance.
CONFIDENCE: high
NEXT STEP: No further imaging processing is needed to address chronicity. The absence of periosteal reaction and callus on both the processed and original images is internally consistent. If clinical timing is in question, comparison with prior imaging or correlation with mechanism-of-injury history would be more informative than additional image processing.

Does the processed image change or confirm the acute assessment? It confirms it. The gradient-enhanced periosteal map fails to reveal any periosteal reaction or callus formation. The lack of these healing markers supports the prior assessment of an acute fracture.
