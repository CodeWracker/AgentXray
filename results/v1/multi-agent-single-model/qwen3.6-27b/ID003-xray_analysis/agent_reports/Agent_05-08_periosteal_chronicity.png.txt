PURPOSE: Evaluate periosteal reaction and callus formation around the fracture site to distinguish acute from subacute injury.
OBSERVATIONS: The gradient map isolates the mid-diaphyseal fracture region of the radius/ulna. Edge-enhanced processing renders the periosteal surfaces as high-contrast linear structures. Along both bone cortices adjacent to the fracture line, parallel linear opacities are visible, representing periosteal elevation. A moderate-density bridging opacity spans the fracture gap, consistent with early callus. On the ulnar aspect, the periosteal reaction appears more organized and cortically integrated than on the radial side. The side-by-side view confirms these are genuine structural findings, not edge-detection noise.
RELEVANT FINDINGS: Periosteal reaction is present bilaterally around the fracture. The bridging opacity suggests callus that has undergone at least partial mineralization. The periosteal new bone is relatively well-defined rather than fluffy or ill-defined, favoring a subacute stage (estimated 2–4 weeks post-injury) over a purely acute fracture. Some asymmetry exists: the ulnar periosteal response appears more mature than the radial side.
RELATION TO ORIGINAL IMAGE: The original X-ray supports these observations. In the unprocessed image, the periosteal reaction is faintly visible as subtle cortical thickening and hazy new bone, most apparent along the ulna. The callus is visible as a faint bridging density across the fracture line. The gradient processing amplifies contrast but does not create structures absent in the original.
POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS: The edge-enhancement processing accentuates cortical margins, making the periosteal reaction appear sharper and more laminated than it may truly be. The black background in the gradient crop eliminates surrounding soft-tissue context, which could mask soft-tissue swelling that would otherwise support timing. The processing also exaggerates fine linear striations along the bone surface that may partly reflect image noise rather than true periosteal layering.
CONFIDENCE: moderate
NEXT STEP: A dedicated soft-tissue swelling assessment would be low value; instead, correlation with clinical history (time since injury) is the highest-value next step. If timing is unknown, a comparison with any prior imaging would materially resolve uncertainty regarding callus maturation. Additional imaging analysis is unlikely to refine the acute-versus-subacute distinction beyond what the original image already provides.
