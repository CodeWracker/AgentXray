PROPOSAL 1: CORTICAL EDGE DELINEATION VIA GRADIENT-BASED EDGE DETECTION
=======================================================================

QUESTION:
  Can we precisely trace the cortical margins of both the radius and ulna across
  the fracture zone to quantify the exact displacement, angulation, and cortical
  step-off at each fracture site? This is essential to distinguish a truly
  non-displaced or minimally displaced fracture from one that has subtle angular
  or transverse displacement missed by naked-eye review.

METHOD:
  Apply a Canny edge detector with dual thresholding optimized for the radiodensity
  range of cortical bone (high-contrast bright structures against dark background).
  Specifically:
  (a) Crop to a region of interest (ROI) encompassing the mid-forearm — from
      10 cm proximal to the fracture zone to 10 cm distal, covering both bones.
  (b) Convert to grayscale and apply Gaussian smoothing (sigma ~1.5) to reduce
      high-frequency noise while preserving edges.
  (c) Apply Canny edge detection with low and high thresholds calibrated to the
      histogram peaks corresponding to cortical bone density (approximately
      upper 10-15% of pixel values in the ROI).
  (d) Use Hough line transform or probabilistic Hough transform to detect linear
      edges along the anterior and posterior cortex of both the radius and ulna.
  (e) Fit piecewise linear models to each cortical margin, with a breakpoint
      forced near the expected fracture zone, and compute angular deviation
      across the breakpoint.

WHY:
  Cortical edges in X-rays are high-contrast transitions, which is exactly what
  Canny edge detection is optimized for. By fitting piecewise linear models, we
  can numerically quantify angulation (in degrees) and transverse displacement
  (in pixels, then calibrated to mm if pixel resolution is known). The naked eye
  may under-appreciate subtle cortical steps of 1-2 mm, especially when both
  bones are fractured at the same level and visual attention is divided.

EXPECTED RESULT:
  Quantitative measurements of:
  - Angular deformity at the ulnar fracture site (expected: slight medial
    angulation of 5-10 degrees, if present).
  - Angular deformity at the radial fracture site.
  - Transverse cortical step-off in mm for each bone.
  - Confirmation or exclusion of cortical continuity on at least one cortices
    (which would suggest an incomplete/greenstick fracture rather than complete).

ARTIFACT WARNING:
  - Canny detection may produce "double edges" at the anterior and posterior
    cortex, requiring post-processing to pair them correctly.
  - Soft tissue edges adjacent to the bones may be picked up as spurious edges;
    masking the ROI to a tight perimarginal zone around each bone is essential.
  - The oblique projection angle means the anterior and posterior cortices may
    not be equally visible, producing asymmetric edge detection.
  - Gaussian smoothing may blur very fine cortical disruptions if sigma is too
    high.

COMPARISON:
  Overlay the detected edges (colored red for ulna, blue for radius) on the
  original ROI crop as a semi-transparent overlay. Any detected cortical
  discontinuities that are not visible on the original should be flagged for
  manual review. Conversely, any visible cortical disruptions missed by the
  algorithm indicate a need for threshold recalibration.

PROPOSAL 2: FRACTURE LINE SEGMENTATION VIA LOCAL ADAPTIVE THRESHOLDING
======================================================================

QUESTION:
  What is the precise orientation and extent of the fracture line through each
  bone (transverse, oblique, spiral, comminuted)? This determines whether the
  fracture pattern is stable or unstable and guides the choice of fixation.

METHOD:
  Apply adaptive local thresholding specifically within the medullary cavity of
  each bone to isolate the fracture line as a hypodense (darker) linear feature
  crossing the normally trabeculated bone interior. Specifically:
  (a) Segment each bone separately using morphological operations: create a
      mask by thresholding at a high density level (cortical + medullary bone)
      and keeping the two largest connected components in the forearm ROI.
  (b) Within each bone mask, apply a local adaptive threshold using a sliding
      window (kernel size ~50x50 pixels) to identify regions that are locally
      darker than their surroundings — these correspond to the fracture cleft
      within the medullary cavity.
  (c) Apply a Sobel gradient operator oriented in both horizontal and vertical
      directions within the medullary cavity to detect the linear fracture
      line orientation. The dominant gradient direction perpendicular to the
      fracture line reveals its orientation.
  (d) Skeletonize the detected hypodense region to reduce it to a one-pixel-wide
      line representing the fracture trajectory.

WHY:
  The fracture line appears as a subtle hypodense linear band within the brighter
  medullary cavity. Global thresholding fails here because the fracture is narrow
  and its density is similar to the background. Local adaptive thresholding
  exploits the fact that the fracture is locally darker than its immediate
  medullary surroundings, even if it is globally similar to background density.

EXPECTED RESULT:
  - A skeletonized fracture line trace for each bone, showing the trajectory
    from entry to exit through the bone.
  - Fracture line angle relative to the bone long axis (transverse if ~90°,
    oblique if 45-90°, spiral if <45° with a curved path).
  - Evidence of comminution: multiple hypodense regions or branching fracture
    lines rather than a single clean trajectory.
  - Butterfly fragment detection: isolated regions of intermediate density
    surrounded by hypodense fracture clefts.

ARTIFACT WARNING:
  - Trabecular bone pattern can produce false hypodense regions that mimic
    fracture lines, especially in the proximal metaphyseal region where
    trabeculae are coarser.
  - The sliding window size must be carefully chosen: too small and noise
    dominates; too large and the local contrast of the fracture is lost.
  - Bone overlap at the fracture level (due to rotation/angulation) can create
    artifactual density changes.
  - The hypodense cleft of a clean transverse fracture is narrow and may be
    below the resolution of adaptive thresholding if it is only 1-2 pixels wide.

COMPARISON:
  Display the segmented fracture lines overlaid on the original ROI. The traced
  lines should align with the visible fracture clefts. Any segments of the traced
  line that extend into intact bone indicate false positives. Conversely, visible
  fracture extensions not captured by the algorithm suggest the threshold is too
  conservative. A side-by-side comparison (original | adaptive threshold result
  | combined overlay) is recommended.

PROPOSAL 3: REGIONAL CONTRAST OPTIMIZATION FOR WRIST AND HAND
=============================================================

QUESTION:
  Are there any occult fractures in the carpal bones, metacarpals, or phalanges
  that are currently obscured by overpenetration? Also, is the distal radial
  physis open or closed (skeletal maturity assessment)?

METHOD:
  Apply piecewise histogram equalization (also known as CLAHE — Contrast Limited
  Adaptive Histogram Equalization) to the distal forearm/wrist/hand region to
  recover detail lost to overpenetration. Specifically:
  (a) Define a rectangular ROI covering the distal 40% of the image, from the
      distal radius/ulna through the entire hand.
  (b) Extract this ROI and compute its histogram. The overpenetrated region will
      have a histogram skewed toward lower pixel values (darker) because the X-ray
      beam was more attenuated by the forearm shaft, leaving excess exposure for
      the thinner hand region.
  (c) Apply CLAHE with a clip limit of 2.0-3.0 and a tile grid size of 8x8 to
      enhance local contrast without amplifying noise in uniform regions.
  (d) Additionally, apply a gamma correction (gamma ~0.7) to brighten the overall
      ROI, compensating for the relative underexposure of the hand region.
  (e) Crop sub-regions for the carpal bones, metacarpal bases, and distal
      radial/ulnar physes with 2x and 4x digital magnification.

WHY:
  The hand and wrist region is thinner and less radiodense than the forearm shaft,
  so with a single exposure optimized for the forearm, the hand receives relatively
  more X-ray photons, pushing its density values to a range where subtle
  differentiations are compressed. CLAHE redistributes the local pixel value
  range to exploit the full display dynamic range, recovering detail in the
  carpal bones and distal physes that is present in the raw data but compressed
  into a narrow value range.

EXPECTED RESULT:
  - Improved visibility of individual carpal bones (scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum,
    pisiform, trapezium, trapezoid, capitate, hamate) and their cortical margins.
  - Detection or exclusion of occult carpal fractures (especially scaphoid waist,
    which is a common associated injury).
  - Clear visualization of the distal radial and ulnar physes, allowing
    determination of whether growth plates are open (skeletally immature) or
    closed (skeletal maturity).
  - Better assessment of the DRUJ — the relationship between the distal ulnar head
    and the ulnar notch of the radius.

ARTIFACT WARNING:
  - CLAHE amplifies noise in uniform regions, which may create artificial
    "fracture-like" dark lines in soft tissue or homogeneous bone.
  - Over-enhancement may produce edge halos around cortical margins, mimicking
    periosteal reaction or subtle fracture lines.
  - Gamma correction shifts the entire histogram and may over-brighten areas
    that were adequately exposed.
  - Digital magnification does not add resolution; it only interpolates existing
    pixels. Fine cortical detail below the native pixel resolution cannot be
    recovered.

COMPARISON:
  Display a three-panel comparison: (left) original wrist/hand region, (center)
  CLAHE-enhanced version, (right) side-by-side toggle with the two versions.
  Any finding (fracture line, joint abnormality) that appears only in the
  enhanced version must be cross-validated against the original to rule out
  enhancement artifacts. The physis assessment should be independently confirmed
  on the original — CLAHE may sharpen the physis margin artificially.

PROPOSAL 4: SOFT TISSUE SWELLING QUANTIFICATION VIA PROFILE ANALYSIS
====================================================================

QUESTION:
  What is the extent and severity of soft tissue swelling around the fracture
  site? Is there evidence of compartment syndrome risk (massive swelling) or soft
  tissue interposition at the fracture gap?

METHOD:
  Generate a perpendicular soft tissue thickness profile across the forearm at
  multiple longitudinal positions. Specifically:
  (a) Define the long axis of the forearm as the line connecting the midpoint of
      the elbow joint to the midpoint of the wrist joint.
  (b) At 1 cm intervals along this axis (or at 50-pixel intervals, whichever
      provides finer resolution), draw a perpendicular transect line extending
      from the medial soft tissue boundary to the lateral soft tissue boundary.
  (c) For each transect, compute the soft tissue width as the distance between
      the two soft tissue–background transition points. These transitions are
      detected by finding where the pixel gradient drops below a threshold
      (indicating the edge of the arm silhouette against the black background).
  (d) Plot soft tissue width vs. longitudinal position along the forearm.
  (e) Identify the region of maximal swelling by finding the local maximum of the
      profile and computing the percentage increase relative to the baseline
      width measured 5 cm proximal and distal to the fracture zone.
  (f) Within the fracture zone itself, examine the soft tissue density profile
      across the fracture gap. Look for a localized hypodense band corresponding
      to the fracture cleft that extends into the soft tissue — this would
      represent hematoma or soft tissue interposition.

WHY:
  Soft tissue swelling is visible but not precisely quantified on the original
  image. A numerical profile provides an objective measure of swelling extent
  (in cm of width increase), which can be correlated with clinical compartment
  syndrome risk. Additionally, the density profile across the fracture gap may
  reveal a soft tissue density occupying the fracture cleft — an important
  finding because soft tissue interposition is a known cause of failure in
  closed reduction.

EXPECTED RESULT:
  - A quantitative swelling profile showing:
    * Normal forearm soft tissue width (baseline): approximately 6-8 cm.
    * Maximal width at the fracture zone.
    * Percentage increase over baseline (e.g., 30-50% increase would indicate
      significant swelling).
  - A density profile across the fracture gap showing:
    * Whether the fracture cleft contains bone density (impaction/fragment),
      soft tissue density (interposition), or air density (open fracture —
      unlikely but worth ruling out).

ARTIFACT WARNING:
  - The arm contour against the background is not perfectly sharp; gradient-based
    edge detection may overestimate or underestimate soft tissue width by 2-4
    pixels at each boundary.
  - Patient positioning (arm pressed against imaging plate vs. suspended) affects
    soft tissue contour. If the arm is compressed against the detector, the soft
    tissue will spread laterally, artificially increasing measured width.
  - Overlying clothing, tape, or padding will be detected as soft tissue.
  - The profile is 2D and cannot distinguish anterior-posterior swelling from
    medial-lateral swelling.

COMPARISON:
  Overlay the transect lines on the original image at key positions (proximal
  baseline, fracture zone, distal baseline) to visually confirm that the
  detected soft tissue boundaries match the visible arm contour. The computed
  widths should be compared with clinical measurements (circumference) if
  available. The density profile across the fracture gap should be compared with
  a direct visual inspection of the cleft region on a contrast-enhanced crop.

PROPOSAL 5: FRACTURE GAP MORPHOMETRY VIA STRUCTURED ANALYSIS
============================================================

QUESTION:
  What is the morphology of the fracture gap — specifically, is there cortical
  impaction, comminution with butterfly fragments, or a clean gap? Is there
  cortical overlap or shortening of either bone? This affects the choice between
  conservative management and surgical fixation.

METHOD:
  Perform a detailed morphometric analysis of each fracture zone. Specifically:
  (a) For each bone (radius, ulna) at the fracture site, define a narrow ROI
      (approximately 3 cm wide, centered on the fracture).
  (b) Rotate the ROI so that the bone long axis is horizontal.
  (c) Subdivide the ROI into the proximal fragment and distal fragment by finding
      the midpoint of the hypodense fracture gap.
  (d) For each fragment, measure:
    - The cortical thickness at three points: 5 mm proximal/distal to the gap,
      and at the gap margin itself.
    - The medullary canal diameter at the same three points.
    - The bone width (anterior-posterior dimension on this view).
  (e) Compare the cortical thickness at the gap margin with the baseline thickness
      5 mm away. An increase at the gap margin suggests cortical impaction
      (bone ends driven together, compressing the cortex).
  (f) Compare the medullary canal diameter: if it narrows at the gap relative to
      baseline, this also suggests impaction. If it widens, it suggests the gap
      is open.
  (g) Measure the total bone length from a fixed proximal landmark (olecranon
      tip for ulna, radial head inferior margin for radius) to a fixed distal
      landmark (distal articular surface). Compare with expected anatomical
      length ratios (ulna is typically ~0.7-0.75 of humeral length; radius is
      typically slightly shorter than ulna). Significant shortening indicates
      telescoping/overlap of fracture fragments.

WHY:
  Impacted fractures are more stable and may be managed conservatively.
  Non-impacted displaced fractures with comminution are unstable and typically
  require surgical fixation. The degree of shortening also matters: more than
  3 mm of shortening in a both-bone forearm fracture is generally an indication
  for surgery. These measurements cannot be reliably made by eye but can be
  quantified with pixel-level morphometry.

EXPECTED RESULT:
  - For each bone: cortical thickness profile across the fracture zone, showing
    whether impaction is present (thickened cortex at gap) or the cortex is
    disrupted (normal or thinned cortex at gap).
  - Bone length measurements: comparison with expected ratios to detect
    shortening/overlap.
  - Fracture gap width at the level of the cortex and at the medullary level.
  - Evidence of butterfly fragments: isolated bone-density regions between the
    two main fragments.

ARTIFACT WARNING:
  - Impaction assessment is confounded by projection: if the fracture fragments
    are angled toward or away from the detector (in the anterior-posterior plane),
    the apparent cortical thickness will be distorted.
  - Length measurement requires accurate identification of anatomical landmarks,
    which may be obscured by the fracture displacement or superimposed anatomy
    (the flexed elbow changes the apparent length of the forearm segment).
  - The oblique projection may cause apparent overlap of the two bones at the
    fracture zone, confusing the segmentation of individual bone fragments.
  - Computed tomography would be far superior for impaction assessment, as it
    eliminates projection ambiguity.

COMPARISON:
  Display the measured cortical thickness profile as a line graph with annotations
  for each measurement point, overlaid on the rotated ROI. Mark the gap midpoint
  and the three measurement positions on the bone for visual verification.
  Reported lengths should be expressed as a ratio (e.g., ulna length / humeral
  length) to be independent of image magnification.

PROPOSAL 6: DISTAL RADIAL PHYSIS ASSESSMENT VIA ENHANCED LOCAL CONTRAST
=======================================================================

QUESTION:
  Is the distal radial physis open or closed? This determines skeletal maturity
  and dramatically changes the treatment approach. In skeletally immature patients,
  both-bone forearm fractures may be managed with closed reduction and casting.
  In mature patients, surgical fixation is more commonly indicated.

METHOD:
  Focus on the distal radius and ulna growth plates. Specifically:
  (a) Extract a high-magnification ROI (4x digital zoom) of the distal 3 cm of
      the radius and ulna.
  (b) Apply unsharp masking: subtract a Gaussian-blurred version of the ROI
      (sigma ~3.0) from the original, then add the result back with a weight
      of 1.5-2.0. This enhances edges (like the physis line) while preserving
      overall contrast.
  (c) Apply directionally tuned edge detection: the physis is a horizontal
      transverse line. Apply a Sobel filter with kernel oriented vertically
      (detecting horizontal edges) to selectively enhance the physis line.
  (d) Measure the physis width in pixels, if visible: an open physis appears as
      a radiolucent (dark) band of 1-3 mm width. A closing physis appears as
      a narrow irregular line. A closed physis has no visible radiolucent gap.
  (e) Assess the distal ulnar physis as well, though it typically closes earlier
      than the radial physis and is less reliable as a maturity indicator.

WHY:
  The growth plate is a thin radiolucent line that is subtle on standard
  exposure, especially in this image where the wrist region is overpenetrated.
  Unsharp masking combined with directional edge enhancement specifically targets
  the horizontal orientation of the distal physes, enhancing their visibility
  while suppressing other structures. The radial physis is the most clinically
  relevant maturity indicator in the forearm, closing around age 18-20 in
  females and 20-22 in males.

EXPECTED RESULT:
  - Clear visualization of the distal radial physis as either:
    * A distinct radiolucent band (open, skeletally immature).
    * A thin irregular line (closing, transitional maturity).
    * Absent, with smooth cortical transition (closed, mature).
  - Estimated skeletal maturity status.
  - Incidental assessment of the styloid processes for associated avulsion
    fractures (especially the ulnar styloid, which can be fractured in DRUJ
    injury patterns).

ARTIFACT WARNING:
  - Unsharp masking at high weights (2.0+) creates dark halos on one side of
    bright edges and bright halos on the dark side, which can make a closed
    physis appear as a thin line.
  - Directional filtering may enhance other horizontal structures (e.g., the
    distal articular surface) and confuse them with the physis.
  - Digital magnification does not create new information; if the physis is
    truly sub-resolution, no amount of enhancement will make it visible.
  - The oblique projection may show the physis at an angle, narrowing its
    apparent width.

COMPARISON:
  Three-panel display: (left) original distal ROI, (center) unsharp masked
  version, (right) directionally filtered version. The physis identification
  must be consistent across all three panels. If it appears only in the
  enhanced versions, it must be treated as a possible artifact and flagged for
  clinical correlation.

PROPOSAL 7: BONE DENSITY AND TRABECULAR PATTERN ANALYSIS
=========================================================

QUESTION:
  Is there any evidence of underlying bone pathology (osteoporosis, osteopenia,
  metabolic bone disease, or bone tumor) that would affect fracture healing or
  fixation strategy? Also, does the trabecular pattern show any stress lines
  or microfracture patterns around the fracture zone?

METHOD:
  Quantitative trabecular bone pattern analysis. Specifically:
  (a) Define three ROIs within the medullary cavity of each bone:
    - Proximal ROI: 5 cm proximal to the fracture.
    - Mid ROI: 1 cm proximal and 1 cm distal to the fracture (avoiding the
      fracture gap itself).
    - Distal ROI: 5 cm distal to the fracture.
  (b) For each ROI, compute:
    * Mean pixel intensity (proxy for bone density within the medullary cavity).
    * Standard deviation of pixel intensity (proxy for trabecular pattern
      variability — higher variability suggests a more distinct trabecular
      pattern, lower variability suggests osteopenia).
    * Gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) features: contrast, correlation,
      and homogeneity, which characterize the spatial organization of
      trabecular structures.
  (c) Compare the proximal and distal ROIs for each bone. A significant density
    mismatch could suggest underlying pathology.
  (d) Look for localized regions of abnormal density (either increased or
    decreased) within the medullary cavity that could suggest a bone tumor
    or infection.

WHY:
  Osteoporotic bone affects surgical planning (screw purchase, need for
  augmentation) and fracture healing prognosis. A both-bone forearm fracture
  in osteoporotic bone is unusual for a young patient and could suggest
  underlying pathology or extremely high-energy trauma. The trabecular pattern
  near the fracture zone may also reveal stress lines radiating from the
  fracture, which could suggest an underlying stress component to the
  injury.

EXPECTED RESULT:
  - Density values and trabecular texture metrics for each ROI.
  - Assessment of whether the bone density is consistent with the patient's
    apparent age (based on skeletal maturity findings from Proposal 6).
  - Detection or exclusion of focal density abnormalities (tumor, infection,
    stress reaction).

ARTIFACT WARNING:
  - X-ray pixel values are not linearly proportional to bone density without
    calibration phantoms; this is a relative, not absolute, density measure.
  - Overlap of the two bones in the ROI will confound medullary cavity
    measurements unless each bone is carefully segmented.
  - Beam hardening and scatter artifacts affect the relationship between
    pixel values and tissue density.
  - GLCM features are sensitive to ROI size and orientation; results must be
    compared using identical ROI dimensions.
  - This analysis is exploratory, not diagnostic — it flags areas for
    further investigation, not definitive bone density diagnosis.

COMPARISON:
  Present results as a table comparing the three ROIs for each bone. Highlight
  any ROI where metrics deviate by more than 20% from the average of the
  other two ROIs. Any focal abnormality should be marked on the original image
  with its coordinates for radiological review.

Priorities (in order of clinical importance):
  1. Proposal 5 (fracture gap morphometry) — directly affects treatment decision.
  2. Proposal 1 (cortical edge delineation) — quantifies displacement/angulation.
  3. Proposal 2 (fracture line segmentation) — characterizes fracture pattern.
  4. Proposal 6 (physis assessment) — determines skeletal maturity.
  5. Proposal 3 (wrist/hand contrast optimization) — rules out concomitant injury.
  6. Proposal 4 (soft tissue analysis) — assesses swelling and interposition risk.
  7. Proposal 7 (trabecular pattern) — exploratory, lower priority.
