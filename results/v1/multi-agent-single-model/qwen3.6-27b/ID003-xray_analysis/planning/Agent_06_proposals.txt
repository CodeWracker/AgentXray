PROPOSAL 1: Local CLAHE (Contrast-Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization)
          on the Fracture Zone to Resolve Corticomedullary Junction Detail
-----------------------------------------------------------------------

QUESTION:
  What is the exact course of the fracture line through the corticomedullary
  junction of both the radius and ulna? Is the fracture purely transverse, or
  does it have subtle oblique or spiral components at the inner cortex that are
  not visible in the current global contrast window? Is there evidence of a
  butterfly fragment or posterior cortex comminution hidden by the low local
  contrast at the fracture site?

METHOD:
  Apply CLAHE (Contrast-Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization) with the
  following parameters:
  - Clip limit: 3.0 (to limit noise amplification)
  - Tile grid size: 8x8 (to capture local contrast variations at the
    corticomedullary transition)
  - Apply only to a cropped ROI centered on the mid-diaphyseal fracture zone,
    extending approximately 3cm proximal and distal to the visible fracture
    lines on both bones
  - Preserve the original Hounsfield-scale relationship; this is a
    visualization enhancement only, not a quantitative transformation

  Additionally, apply the same CLAHE to a second ROI that isolates each bone
  individually (radius-only crop and ulna-only crop) to eliminate superposition
  artifacts between the two forearms bones.

WHY:
  The original image uses a global contrast range optimized for the entire
  field-of-view (humerus through fingers). At the fracture zone specifically,
  the cortical bone margins — particularly the inner cortex facing the
  medullary canal — have low local contrast against the trabecular pattern.
  CLAHE enhances local contrast without the washout effect of global histogram
  equalization. The clip limit prevents noise amplification that could be
  mistaken for micro-fragments. Separating the radius and ulna eliminates the
  confounding effect of bone superposition on this lateral projection.

EXPECTED RESULT:
  - Sharper delineation of whether the fracture line is purely transverse or
    has a subtle oblique extension along the endosteal surface
  - Potential visualization of a small butterfly fragment on the cortex that
    is currently obscured
  - Clearer view of the cortical step-off at the fracture margins, which aids
    in displacement measurement
  - Better definition of the periosteal reaction (or lack thereof) at the
    fracture margins, supporting acute vs. subacute timing

ARTIFACT WARNING:
  - CLAHE can amplify quantum mottle and digital noise, potentially creating
    false "fragment" appearances along the cortex. The clip limit of 3.0
    mitigates this but does not eliminate it
  - Edge halos may appear around the high-contrast cortical margins, creating
    the illusion of a cortical step where none exists. These halos are typically
    narrow (1-2 pixels) and uniform, distinguishing them from true cortical
    irregularity
  - Adaptive contrast can create false "lines" at the boundary between tiles
    if the tile grid does not align with anatomical features. Using 8x8 tiles
    on a region of this size minimizes blockiness
  - Individual bone isolation requires careful cropping; any misalignment
    will distort anatomical relationships

COMPARISON:
  Side-by-side with the original image at 1:1 pixel scale is essential. The
  CLAHE output must NEVER be evaluated in isolation. Key comparison points:
  - Any new fracture line or fragment visible in CLAHE must correspond to at
    least a subtle density change in the original
  - Cortical margins in the CLAHE image should appear sharper but not thinner
    or thicker than in the original
  - Measure the apparent fracture gap width in both images; CLAHE may make the
    gap appear wider due to edge enhancement, which would overestimate
    displacement

PROPOSAL 2: Hessian-based Fracture Line Enhancement for Displacement Angle
          Measurement and Fracture Orientation Quantification
-----------------------------------------------------------------------

QUESTION:
  What is the precise fracture line orientation (angle relative to the long
  axis of each bone) and what is the exact angular displacement at the fracture
  site? Is the angular deformity consistent between the radius and ulna, or is
  there differential angulation suggesting rotational displacement?

METHOD:
  Implement a multi-scale Hessian-based line enhancement filter:
  1. Pre-process: Gaussian smoothing (sigma = 1.0) to reduce high-frequency
     noise
  2. Compute the Hessian matrix at multiple scales (sigma = 0.8, 1.2, 1.6,
     2.0) across the fracture zone ROI
  3. Extract the eigenvector corresponding to the smallest eigenvalue at each
     scale — this eigenvector points along the direction of maximum intensity
     change (the fracture line)
  4. Create a vesselness/lineness response map using the second and third
     eigenvalues (Frangi filter approach), selecting the scale with maximum
     response at each pixel
  5. Apply non-maximum suppression along the principal eigenvector direction
  6. Threshold the response map to isolate linear structures (the fracture
     lines)
  7. Fit a least-squares line to the enhanced fracture line for each bone
  8. Fit a least-squares line to the shaft axis of the proximal and distal
     fragments for each bone (using the corticomedullary boundaries 2cm away
     from the fracture zone)
  9. Compute the angle between proximal and distal shaft lines to quantify
     angular displacement

WHY:
  The Hessian-based enhancement is specifically designed to amplify linear,
  tubular structures while suppressing blob-like and planar features. Fracture
  lines are essentially linear radiolucencies, making them ideal targets for
  this filter. The multi-scale approach captures both the narrow fracture line
  (small scale) and the broader displacement zone (larger scale). Quantitative
  angle measurement removes the subjectivity of visual estimation, which is
  particularly important in pediatric fractures where displacement angles
  determine conservative vs. operative management thresholds.

EXPECTED RESULT:
  - A quantitative measurement of the fracture line angle relative to the
    shaft axis (transverse = 90°, oblique = < 90°)
  - A quantitative angular displacement measurement for each bone
  - Comparison of displacement angles between radius and ulna to assess
    rotational injury component
  - A visual enhancement map where the fracture lines are prominently
    highlighted, aiding in identification of any secondary fracture lines
    or comminution not visible on the original

ARTIFACT WARNING:
  - The Hessian filter enhances ANY linear structure, including trabecular
    patterns, vessel shadows, and image grid lines. The scale selection and
    thresholding must be carefully chosen to suppress these false positives
  - At the fracture site, the cortical step-off itself produces a strong
    linear signal that may be confused with the fracture line
  - The line-fitting step assumes a single, dominant linear fracture line.
    Comminuted fractures or oblique components will produce inaccurate angle
    measurements
  - Gaussian pre-smoothing may blur the fracture line at very small scales,
    shifting the measured position slightly (sub-pixel error, typically < 0.5
    pixel)

COMPARISON:
  - Overlay the enhanced fracture lines and fitted lines directly on the
    original image in a different color (e.g., red overlay on grayscale
    original)
  - Verify that the fitted shaft axis lines align with the visible cortical
    margins in the original image
  - The angular displacement measured computationally should be within ± 5
    degrees of visual estimation; large discrepancies indicate that the
    line-fitting captured an incorrect anatomical structure

PROPOSAL 3: Physis (Growth Plate) Isolation and Corticomaturation Index
          Analysis for Pediatric Bone Age Estimation and Fracture Proximity
-----------------------------------------------------------------------

QUESTION:
  How close is the fracture zone to the proximal and distal physes (growth
  plates) of the radius and ulna? Is there any radiographic evidence of
  physeal involvement or metaphyseal extension of the fracture line? What is
  the estimated skeletal age based on the morphological characteristics of
  the growth plates?

METHOD:
  1. Crop and independently magnify (4x) two ROIs:
     a) Distal radius and distal ulna physes
     b) Proximal radius and proximal ulna physes
  2. Apply a direction-independent adaptive edge detection algorithm (Canny
     edge detector with dual thresholds, sigma = 1.5) to each ROI to clearly
     delineate the physes from the metaphysis and epiphysis
  3. For each physis:
     - Measure the longitudinal distance from the nearest fracture endpoint
       to the physis (in both pixels and estimated mm using the known bone
       width as a scale reference)
     - Measure the transverse width of the physis (widest point)
     - Measure the metaphyseal cupping depth (concave indentation of the
       metaphysis at the physis level, a key indicator of growth plate
       activity and skeletal age)
     - Assess the epiphyseal ossification center size relative to the
       metaphyseal width
  4. Compute a corticomaturation ratio: cortical thickness at the metaphysis
     (just adjacent to the physis) divided by cortical thickness at the
     mid-diaphysis. In younger children, the metaphyseal cortex is relatively
     thinner due to endochondral growth; this ratio increases with skeletal
     maturity
  5. Compare the physis morphology against Greulich-Pyle reference criteria:
     - Narrow, well-defined physis with significant metaphyseal cupping =
       younger (approx. 8-11 years)
     - Wider, less distinct physis with minimal cupping = older (approx.
       12-15 years)

WHY:
  In pediatric fracture management, the proximity of a fracture to a growth
  plate is the single most important factor influencing treatment decisions.
  A fracture that involves or is adjacent to a physis carries the risk of
  growth disturbance, premature physeal closure, and angular deformity during
  continued growth. Quantitative distance measurement removes the subjectivity
  of "close" or "far." The corticomaturation analysis provides an independent
  estimate of skeletal maturity, which is critical for predicting remodeling
  potential (younger children remodel more aggressively).

EXPECTED RESULT:
  - Quantitative distance (in mm) from each fracture end to the nearest physis
  - Confirmation that the fracture is purely diaphyseal, with adequate margin
    from both proximal and distal physes
  - Estimated skeletal age range based on physis morphology
  - Assessment of metaphyseal modeling that may indicate pre-existing
    conditions (e.g., rickets, osteogenesis imperfecta) — ruled out if
    metaphyseal margins are smooth and regular

ARTIFACT WARNING:
  - The lateral projection causes superposition of the radial and ulnar physes
    at the distal end, making independent measurement difficult. Edge
    detection may merge the two physes into a single contour
  - Magnification artifacts in radiographic imaging mean that pixel-to-mm
    conversion depends on the object-to-detector distance, which varies along
    the curved forearm. Proximal structures are magnified more than distal
    ones
  - Canny edge detection is sensitive to noise; the trabecular pattern near
    the metaphysis may produce spurious edges
  - Skeletal age estimation from a single view is approximate; lateral
    projection is suboptimal for physis assessment compared to AP view
  - The corticomaturation ratio is affected by the fracture displacement
    itself, which may alter the apparent cortical thickness

COMPARISON:
  - The edge-detected physis contours must align precisely with the visible
    radiolucent growth plate line in the original image
  - Distance measurements should be annotated directly on the original image
    with scale bars for verification
  - Compare the distal radial physis appearance with published Greulich-Pyle
    atlas images at the estimated age range
  - Verify that the corticomaturation ratio is calculated from regions of
    the cortex that are not distorted by the fracture displacement

PROPOSAL 4: Interosseous Membrane and Radioulnar Joint Space Analysis via
          Gradient-based Segmentation
-----------------------------------------------------------------------

QUESTION:
  Is the interosseous space between the radius and ulna maintained, narrowed,
  or widened at the fracture level? Is there evidence of interosseous
  membrane disruption or distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ) instability that
  would suggest a Galeazzi-equivalent or Essex-Lopresti variant injury?

METHOD:
  1. Segment the radial and ulnar cortical margins using a gradient-based
     active contour model (snake algorithm) initialized along the visible
     cortex on both bones
  2. Define the interosseous space as the region between the medial cortex
     of the radius and the lateral cortex of the ulna at each longitudinal
     position along the bone shafts
  3. Measure the interosseous space width at 10 evenly spaced intervals
     from 2cm proximal to the fracture to 2cm distal to the fracture
  4. Additionally measure the interosseous space at the proximal radioulnar
     joint (pronator shelf) and distal radioulnar joint for reference
  5. Generate a graph of interosseous space width vs. longitudinal position
  6. At the DRUJ: assess the radioulnar overlap and ulnar variance (relative
     length of the ulna to the radius at the wrist)

WHY:
  In both-bone forearm fractures, disruption of the interosseous membrane and
  DRUJ instability significantly alter the treatment approach. On a lateral
  projection, the interosseous space is partially obscured by bone overlap,
  making visual assessment unreliable. Automated segmentation of the cortical
  margins provides a quantitative measure that is less susceptible to
  perceptual bias. The interosseous space typically narrows at the fracture
  level due to muscle spasm and displacement, but a paradoxical widening may
  indicate interosseous membrane rupture.

EXPECTED RESULT:
  - A quantitative profile of interosseous space width along the shaft,
    showing the characteristic narrowing at the fracture zone
  - Confirmation of whether the narrowing is physiological (muscle spasm
    and displacement) or pathological (membrane disruption)
  - Assessment of DRUJ alignment: normal overlap on lateral view vs.
    subluxation
  - Identification of any interosseous space widening that would suggest
    ligamentous injury

ARTIFACT WARNING:
  - On a lateral projection, the radius and ulna are partially superimposed,
    and the active contour may "jump" from one bone to the other at points of
    overlap, creating erroneous segmentations
  - The interosseous membrane itself is not radiographically visible; the
    "interosseous space" is an inference from the distance between bones, not
    a direct measurement of the membrane
  - Rotation of the forearm during image acquisition significantly affects the
    apparent interosseous space width, even without injury
  - The snake algorithm may be attracted to soft tissue edges rather than
    cortical bone edges if the initialization is not precise

COMPARISON:
  - Overlay the segmented cortical contours on the original image to verify
    alignment with the visible cortex
  - The interosseous space profile should show the characteristic narrowing
    at the fracture site; an unusual pattern (widening, or no change) warrants
    closer inspection
  - Compare the DRUJ appearance with the proximal radioulnar joint — both
    should show normal overlap on a well-positioned lateral view
  - Any apparent DRUJ abnormality must be considered in the context of the
    hand position (semi-fist), which can cause apparent ulnar deviation

PROPOSAL 5: Soft Tissue Swelling Quantification and Compartment Risk Index
          via Background Subtraction and Density Mapping
-----------------------------------------------------------------------

QUESTION:
  What is the extent and severity of soft tissue swelling around the fracture
  site? Is the swelling disproportionate to the fracture pattern, raising
  concern for compartment syndrome or significant vascular injury? Is there
  evidence of subcutaneous emphysema or fat stranding?

METHOD:
  1. Create a synthetic "expected" soft tissue contour by mirroring the
     uninjured proximal forearm soft tissue outline and extrapolating it
     through the fracture zone
  2. Subtract the expected contour from the actual soft tissue contour to
     quantify the swelling displacement at each circumferential point
  3. Generate a regional density map of the soft tissue using windowing
     parameters optimized for soft tissue (window center: 40 HU, window width:
     300 HU equivalent in the radiographic grayscale)
  4. Identify and measure the cross-sectional area of the soft tissue envelope
     at the fracture level and at two reference levels (proximal and distal)
  5. Compute the Swelling Ratio: (cross-sectional area at fracture level) /
     (average cross-sectional area at reference levels)
  6. Within the swollen soft tissue, search for:
     - Areas of abnormally low density (subcutaneous fat disruption, potential
       fat stranding indicating deep tissue injury)
     - Linear radiolucencies that could represent gas (subcutaneous emphysema)
     - Displacement of the intermuscular septa (visible as linear densities
       traversing the soft tissue)

WHY:
  Compartment syndrome is a feared complication of both-bone forearm fractures
  in children, and early radiographic signs of excessive soft tissue swelling
  can prompt earlier clinical monitoring. The lateral projection provides a
  unique view of the anterior-posterior soft tissue dimensions, which are
  critical for compartment assessment. Quantitative swelling measurement is
  superior to subjective assessment, especially when serial imaging is
  performed.

EXPECTED RESULT:
  - A quantitative swelling ratio (> 1.5 is concerning, > 2.0 is highly
    concerning for compartment syndrome)
  - Visualization of the anterior and posterior soft tissue compartments
    separately, showing whether swelling is localized or diffuse
  - Detection of any abnormal soft tissue densities (hematoma), lucencies
    (gas), or septal displacement
  - A reference against which follow-up imaging can be compared

ARTIFACT WARNING:
  - The synthetic contour method assumes that the uninjured proximal forearm
    is a valid template for the injured zone, which is reasonable but not
    exact — the forearm normally tapers distally
  - Patient positioning and arm rotation significantly affect the apparent
    soft tissue cross-section on a lateral projection
  - The hand-in-fist position at the distal end creates a bulge that may be
    confused with swelling
  - Grayscale windowing for soft tissue on a radiographic image (designed for
    bone visualization) will amplify noise in the darker regions
  - External padding, positioning aids, or clothing may be present in the
    soft tissue shadow and confound the analysis

COMPARISON:
  - The synthetic contour should be visually compared with the actual soft
    tissue outline; any systematic deviation should be noted as a limitation
  - The swelling ratio should be compared with published reference ranges for
    pediatric forearm fractures
  - Any identified abnormal soft tissue findings must be cross-checked with
    the original bone window to confirm they are not projectional artifacts
  - The soft tissue analysis should be interpreted in conjunction with the
    degree of bone displacement — minor fractures with massive swelling are
    more concerning than severe fractures with moderate swelling

PROPOSAL 6: Perosteal Reaction and Fracture Chronology Assessment via
          Multi-resolution Cortical Margin Analysis
-----------------------------------------------------------------------

QUESTION:
  Is there evidence of periosteal reaction (callus formation, periosteal
  elevation, or periosteal new bone) at the fracture site or elsewhere along
  the radius and ulna? What is the radiographic age of the fracture? Is there
  any evidence of prior injury or underlying bone pathology?

METHOD:
  1. Extract the cortical margin profiles of the radius and ulna at the
     fracture site using sub-pixel edge detection (Zernike moment-based edge
     localization for accuracy in the presence of noise)
  2. Analyze the cortical margin at three zones:
     a) Fracture zone: ± 1.5cm from the fracture line
     b) Transition zone: 1.5-3.0cm from the fracture line
     c) Reference zone: > 5cm from the fracture line
  3. In each zone, compute:
     - Cortical margin regularity index: standard deviation of the distance
       from the ideal cortical line to the actual edge (higher values indicate
       irregularity consistent with callus or periosteal reaction)
     - Extra-cortical density: mean pixel intensity in a 3mm band immediately
       outside the cortex (elevated density suggests periosteal new bone)
     - Cortical thickness uniformity: coefficient of variation of cortical
       thickness along a 2cm segment
  4. Compare the fracture zone metrics with the reference zone metrics
  5. Specifically examine the fracture gap for:
     - Sclerotic margin (increased density at the fracture end) — indicates
       a subacute or non-healing fracture (typically > 2-3 weeks)
     - Lytic margin (decreased density at the fracture end) — may indicate
       osteomyelitis or a pathological fracture
     - Clean, sharp fracture margins — consistent with acute injury

WHY:
  Determining the age of a fracture is clinically important for treatment
  planning and medico-legal assessment. In a pediatric patient, callus
  formation can begin as early as 7-10 days post-injury and is typically more
  robust than in adults. The presence or absence of periosteal reaction,
  combined with the quality of the fracture margins, provides radiographic
  evidence for the timing of the injury. Additionally, any periosteal reaction
  at sites remote from the fracture would suggest a systemic bone disorder or
  prior trauma.

EXPECTED RESULT:
  - Confirmation of clean, sharp fracture margins consistent with acute
    injury (< 7 days)
  - Quantitative evidence that the extra-cortical density at the fracture site
    is not elevated compared to the reference zone (no callus)
  - Cortical margin regularity at the fracture zone is elevated due to the
    fracture displacement itself, but no additional periosteal layer is
    present
  - Absence of periosteal reaction at sites remote from the fracture,
    supporting a simple traumatic etiology
  - Exclusion of sclerotic or lytic fracture margins, which would suggest
    subacute injury or underlying pathology

ARTIFACT WARNING:
  - The fracture displacement itself creates cortical margin irregularity that
    must be distinguished from periosteal reaction. The analysis must focus on
    the cortex beyond the immediate fracture gap
  - The pediatric periosteum is thick and loosely attached, which can create
    a normal periosteal shadow that may be mistaken for periosteal reaction
  - Sub-pixel edge detection is sensitive to image noise; the trabecular
    pattern near the fracture may produce false "extra-cortical" density
  - Sclerotic margin development begins at 2-3 weeks; a fracture that is
    1-2 weeks old may not yet show any radiographic signs of healing, leading
    to false classification as "acute"
  - Extra-cortical density measurement is confounded by soft tissue overlap
    and projectional angulation

COMPARISON:
  - The cortical margin profiles should be visually inspected for alignment
    with the visible cortex in the original image
  - The extra-cortical density at the fracture site should be compared pixel
    by pixel with the reference zone; any difference should be within the
    expected noise range for acute fractures
  - The cortical thickness measurements should be consistent with the
    expected pediatric cortical thickness (relatively thinner cortex compared
    to adults)
  - Any identified periosteal reaction should be independently confirmed by
    visual inspection of the original image before clinical significance is
    assigned
