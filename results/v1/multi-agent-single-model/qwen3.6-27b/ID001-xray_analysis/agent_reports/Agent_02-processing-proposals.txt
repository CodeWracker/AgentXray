## PHILOSOPHY
I am not proposing a checklist of standard techniques. Each proposal below
responds to a concrete observation about THIS specific image. I want to
resolve specific uncertainties, not exercise a toolkit.


## PROPOSAL 1 — Intensity Windowing & Cropping for Dynamic Range Recovery

### (a) QUESTION
A large fraction of this image (roughly 50%+) is near-black background.
This means the full 8- or 16-bit dynamic range is being "wasted" on empty
space, compressing the useful signal into a narrow band of intensity
values. Is there subtle contrast information in the soft-tissue envelope
and marrow spaces that is currently invisible because the histogram is
stretched across the full range including dead background?

### (b) WHY THIS METHOD
By cropping to the anatomical bounding box and re-mapping the resulting
intensity histogram to span the full display range, we can effectively
"zoom in" on the pixel values that carry diagnostic information. This
is not about making things "sharper" — it is about recovering contrast
that exists in the data but is being compressed below the visibility
threshold.

### (c) EXPECTED FINDINGS
- Soft tissue margins (plantar aspect, dorsum) should become more
  distinct, allowing measurement of soft-tissue thickness for signs
  of swelling or effusion.
- Trabecular patterns in the calcaneus and talus should show more
  texture, potentially revealing areas of altered bone density.
- The sinus tarsi region (between talus and calcaneus) may show
  internal structure currently lost in mid-gray noise.

### (d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS
- Noise amplification: background sensor noise that was invisible at
  low display gain may become prominent. Must compare directly with
  the original to distinguish real structure from amplified noise.
- If the image is 8-bit (not 16-bit DICOM), the gain is limited —
  there may simply not be enough bit-depth to recover meaningful
  contrast. This itself is a finding.

### (e) COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL
Side-by-side view, original left, processed right. I must be able to
point to a region and say: "this was invisible before, and it is
visible now, and it corresponds to real anatomy, not noise pattern."
Any structure I claim to see must be consistent across at least two
different re-mapping strategies (e.g., linear stretch vs. percentile
clipping).


## PROPOSAL 2 — CLAHE (Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization)

### (a) QUESTION
The image has strong global intensity gradients: the heel region is
overall denser (brighter) than the forefoot. A global histogram
adjustment cannot simultaneously optimize contrast for both regions.
Are there local contrast features — particularly at bone interfaces
and joint margins — that are being masked by these regional intensity
differences?

### (b) WHY THIS METHOD
CLAHE operates on small tiles of the image independently, equalizing
contrast locally while clamping the amplification factor to prevent
noise explosion. Unlike global equalization, it can reveal detail in
both the dense heel region and the less dense forefoot in a single
pass. The adaptive nature is critical here because the foot has
inherently non-uniform radiographic density along its length.

### (c) EXPECTED FINDINGS
- Joint margins (tibiotalar, talocalcaneal, subtalar facets) should
  show sharper definition, allowing better assessment of joint space
  width and cortical integrity.
- The Lisfranc junction (metatarsal-cuneiform-cuboid complex) is a
  region of overlapping bones on lateral view — CLAHE may separate
  superimposed structures enough to assess for subtle diastasis.
- Cortical bone continuity along metatarsal shafts may reveal hairline
  disruptions not visible in the original's mid-contrast range.

### (d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS
- Clahe boundary artifacts: tile borders can create artificial
  intensity discontinuities. I need to use sufficiently large tile
  sizes (at least 64x64 pixels) and check for blockiness.
- Over-amplification in homogeneous regions: areas of uniform soft
  tissue may develop false texture that mimics pathology. The
  contrast-limiting parameter (clip limit) must be tuned conservatively
  — start at 2.0 and go up only if needed.

### (e) COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL
Three-panel comparison: original | CLAHE-tile-64 | CLAHE-tile-128.
If a structure appears in both CLAHE versions consistently, it is
more likely real. If it appears only at the smaller tile size, it may
be a tiling artifact. Any claimed finding must survive the test:
"would I still see this if I used a different tile size?"


## PROPOSAL 3 — Unsharp Masking Targeted at Cortical Edges

### (a) QUESTION
The cortical margins of the metatarsal shafts, navicular, and cuboid
appear slightly soft in the original. This could be genuine lack of
sharpness (magnification blur from object-detector distance), or it
could be that fine detail is present but falls below the edge
detection threshold of the display. Are there micro-fractures or
cortical step-offs that become visible only when high-frequency
content is selectively boosted?

### (b) WHY THIS METHOD
Unsharp masking (USM) adds a scaled copy of the high-frequency
content (original minus blurred version) back to the original.
Unlike CLAHE, which operates on intensity distribution, USM operates
on spatial frequency — it enhances edges without changing overall
brightness. This is the right tool when the question is specifically
about edge definition, not about overall contrast.

### (c) EXPECTED FINDINGS
- Hairline fractures: a cortical step-off of 1-2 pixels would be
  invisible at native contrast but amplified by USM.
- Periosteal reaction: thin new bone along the tibial shaft or
  metatarsals would appear as a faint parallel line to the cortex —
  USM should make this pop.
- Enthesophytes (bone spurs) at the plantar calcaneal margin: the
  transition from smooth cortex to spur may be subtle; edge
  enhancement clarifies the boundary.

### (d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS
- Halo effect: USM creates dark/bright halos on either side of edges,
  which can be mistaken for cortical disruption or fracture lines.
  Critical test: a real fracture crosses the cortex; a halo runs
  parallel to it.
- Noise amplification along the same spatial frequencies as edges.
  Solution: apply a moderate Gaussian blur before USM (kernel ~5px)
  to suppress high-frequency noise before the enhancement step.

### (e) COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL
Overlay mode: 50% original + 50% USM-processed. In overlay mode,
USM halos cancel out (they appear inverted on each copy), but real
edges reinforce. This is the most reliable way to distinguish artifact
from anatomy.


## PROPOSAL 4 — Soft-Tissue Envelope Measurement

### (a) QUESTION
Is there regional soft-tissue swelling that would localize pathology
even in the absence of visible bone abnormality? The lateral view
shows the plantar and dorsal soft-tissue contours, but measuring them
by eye is unreliable — I need quantitative thickness profiles along
the foot to detect focal asymmetry.

### (b) WHY THIS METHOD
By extracting the soft-tissue boundary (the outer contour where
bone/soft-tissue transitions to air/background), I can compute a
thickness profile: at each longitudinal position along the foot,
measure the perpendicular distance from dorsal to plantar contour.
Comparing this profile against expected norms for each anatomical
region (hindfoot, midfoot, forefoot) flags focal swelling.

### (c) EXPECTED FINDINGS
- Normal: relatively uniform dorsal thickness over the midfoot,
  increasing gradually toward the heel (Achilles insertion region)
  and over the metatarsal heads.
- Abnormal: focal thickening over the midfoot would suggest deep
  space infection or hemorrhage. Plantar thickening at the calcaneal
  insertion suggests plantar fasciitis (thickened fascia). Dorsal
  thickening over specific MTP joints suggests synovitis.

### (d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS
- The soft-tissue-to-air boundary is low-contrast — simple thresholding
  will be noisy. I should use Canny edge detection with a low threshold
  followed by morphological closing to get a smooth contour.
- Patient positioning affects the apparent thickness — if the foot is
  rotated even slightly from true lateral, the profile is distorted.
  I must verify true lateral positioning (superimposed malleoli on
  ankle view) before trusting the measurements.

### (e) COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL
Annotate the original image with the detected contour lines and
thickness measurements (in mm, calibrated to the known size of the
calcaneus or talus as internal ruler). The annotations must be
visually plausible — if the contour cuts through the image in an
obvious wrong place, the segmentation failed and the measurement is
meaningless.


## PROPOSAL 5 — Frequency Domain Analysis for Periodic Artifacts

### (a) QUESTION
Does this image contain periodic or structured noise (moire patterns,
grid lines, scanner artifacts) that could be mistaken for pathology
or that could be masking real features? The first-pass assessment
noted "minimal artifacts" but visual inspection is not sensitive to
low-amplitude periodic noise.

### (b) WHY THIS METHOD
A 2D FFT (Fast Fourier Transform) converts the image from spatial to
frequency domain. Periodic structures — grid lines, moire, repetitive
noise — show up as discrete bright spots in the frequency spectrum,
even when they are imperceptible in the spatial domain. This is a
diagnostic of the image itself, not the anatomy.

### (c) EXPECTED FINDINGS
- Clean image: FFT shows a concentrated central peak (low-frequency
  content from the anatomy) with diffuse, radially symmetric
  falloff. No discrete spots outside the center.
- Grid artifact: pairs of spots symmetric about the center, at a
  frequency corresponding to the grid spacing.
- Moire pattern: clusters of spots at specific orientations,
  indicating interference between the image acquisition sampling and
  a periodic structure (digitizer screen, anti-aliasing filter, etc.)

### (d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS
- The FFT of any bounded image has sinc-function sidelobes from
  windowing — these are not real periodic content. I should apply
  a Hanning window before the FFT to suppress windowing artifacts.
- A single strong anatomical feature (like the tibia cortex running
  vertically) produces a horizontal line in the FFT — this is not
  noise, it is structure. I need to distinguish anatomy-driven
  frequency content from acquisition artifacts.

### (e) COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL
If periodic artifacts are found, apply a notch filter in the
frequency domain at the offending frequencies, then inverse FFT back
to spatial domain. Compare before/after: if a previously faint
feature becomes clearer after notch filtering, the feature was real
and the artifact was masking it. If nothing changes meaningfully,
the artifact was too weak to matter.


## PROPOSAL 6 — Radiodensity Profiling Across Key Joints

### (a) QUESTION
Can I quantify the intensity transition across joint spaces to estimate
joint space width more precisely than visual estimation? The
tibiotalar and talocalcaneal joint spaces should appear as dark bands
(low density) between two bright cortical lines. The width of this
dark band, measured in calibrated units, provides an objective
measure of joint space narrowing.

### (b) WHY THIS METHOD
Draw perpendicular intensity profiles across each joint of interest.
The profile will show a characteristic "valley" shape: high intensity
(cortical bone) → low intensity (joint space/cartilage) → high
intensity (opposing cortical bone). The distance between the two
peaks, minus the cortical thickness, gives the joint space width.
This is more precise and reproducible than visual estimation.

### (c) EXPECTED FINDINGS
- Tibiotalar joint: normal space ~3-4mm. Profile should show a clear
  valley with well-defined peaks on each side.
- Talocalcaneal joint: more complex due to three articular facets.
  Profile across the posterior facet should be clean; the middle and
  anterior facets may show superimposition.
- Subtalar joint: if narrowed (<2mm), suggests degenerative change
  or coalition.

### (d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS
- Superimposition: on a true lateral, medial and lateral structures
  overlap. The intensity profile may show multiple peaks if two
  cortical surfaces are projected on top of each other.
- Sclerosis: subchondral sclerosis increases the intensity near the
  joint, making the transition gradual rather than sharp. This can
  make peak detection unreliable.
- Profile orientation: if the profile line is not perpendicular to
  the joint surface, the measured width is artificially increased.

### (e) COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL
Superimpose the measurement lines and peak locations on the original
image. The annotated image should show: (1) where each profile was
taken, (2) the measured joint space width in mm, (3) the intensity
profile plot itself. This makes the measurement transparent and
verifiable.


## SUMMARY OF PRIORITIES

| Priority | Proposal | Reason |
|----------|----------|--------|
| 1 | Intensity Windowing + Crop | Foundational — unlocks all other analyses |
| 2 | CLAHE | Most likely to reveal hidden structural detail |
| 3 | Soft-Tissue Envelope | Unique quantitative value, orthogonal to bone analysis |
| 4 | Unsharp Masking | Targets specific question about cortical integrity |
| 5 | Frequency Domain | Quality check — should be done before trusting findings |
| 6 | Joint Space Profiling | Quantitative measure with clinical relevance |

I am most interested in seeing what happens when we combine Proposal 1
(windowing) with Proposal 2 (CLAHE). The windowing recovers global
dynamic range, and CLAHE then optimizes local contrast within that
expanded range. I suspect this combination will reveal features that
neither technique alone can show.
