================================================================
7. LIMITATIONS
================================================================
- Single-view assessment: A lateral-only view provides limited
  diagnostic information. Standard foot/ankle series includes at
  minimum AP and lateral views; oblique views are often added.
  Key pathology (e.g., lateral malleolus fracture, navicular
  fracture, Lisfranc injury) may be invisible or occult on a
  lateral-only projection.
- No clinical history available: Without knowing the mechanism of
  injury, symptoms, or location of pain, it is impossible to
  prioritize regions of interest or contextualize findings.
- No contralateral comparison: Soft tissue swelling and subtle
  asymmetries cannot be assessed.
- No weight-bearing view: If this is a non-weight-bearing image,
  stress-related findings (e.g., subtalar incongruity, metatarsal
  stress injuries) may not be apparent.
- Resolution constraints: As an image processed through a chat
  interface, the effective resolution may be lower than the
  original radiograph, limiting detection of hairline fractures
  or subtle trabecular changes.
- No ability to assess soft tissue structures in detail (ligaments,
  tendons, cartilage) — MRI or ultrasound would be required.
- Cannot evaluate vascular supply or nerve integrity from X-ray.

================================================================
8. SUGGESTED ADDITIONAL ANALYSES
================================================================
1. Obtain and review additional radiographic views:
   - AP view of foot: Assesses tarsometatarsal alignment, navicular
     and cuneiform integrity, and intermetatarsal spacing.
   - Oblique view of foot: Better visualization of cuneiforms,
     cuboid, and base of the 5th metatarsal.
   - Mortise view of ankle: Evaluates the ankle mortise, clear
     space (Dawson's line), and malleolar fractures.

2. Digital image enhancement of current lateral view:
   - Apply windowing/level adjustments to optimize trabecular
     detail in the calcaneus and distal tibia.
   - Edge-enhancement filtering to highlight subtle cortical
     step-offs.
   - Inversion/negative to make radiolucent fracture lines more
     visible.

3. Bohler angle and Gissane angle measurement:
   - Question answered: Is there a calcaneal fracture?
     Bohler angle < 5 degrees or negative suggests calcaneal
     fracture. Normal is 20-40 degrees.
   - Gissane angle normally 120-145 degrees; acute angulation
     suggests calcaneal fracture.

4. Talar tilt assessment:
   - Question answered: Is there subtalar joint incongruity?
   - Requires comparison or weight-bearing view for meaningful
     assessment.

5. Soft tissue measurement:
   - Measure Achilles tendon thickness (question: tenosynovitis or
     tendinopathy?).
   - Assess pre-patellar and dorsal foot soft tissue swelling
     (requires AP view and contralateral comparison).

6. If clinical suspicion remains high despite negative X-rays:
   - CT scan: For occult fractures (navicular, 5th metatarsal base,
     calcaneus, tibial plafond).
   - MRI: For ligamentous injury (ATFL, CFL, syndesmosis),
     tendon pathology, bone marrow edema (stress fractures),
     or occult osteochondral lesions.

================================================================
9. QUESTIONS FOR CLOSE-UP INSPECTION
================================================================
1. Distal tibial plafond (articular surface):
   - Inspect for subtle intra-articular fracture lines,
     osteochondral defects, or step-offs. Zoom to evaluate
     cortical integrity of the talar dome and tibial plafond.

2. Posterior process of the talus (posterior talar process):
   - Question: Is there a posterior ankle impingement or "os
     trigonum" variant? This area can harbor small fractures
     (Shepherd's fracture).

3. Calcaneal body and posterior facet:
   - Inspect trabecular pattern for compression or impaction
     fracture. Look for loss of Bohler's triangle height.
   - Examine the sustentaculum tali region for subtle fracture
     lines.

4. Navicular bone:
   - This is one of the most commonly missed fractures on X-ray.
   - Inspect the navicular for cortical disruption or abnormal
     lucency. Particularly relevant if there is midfoot pain.

5. Bases of the metatarsals:
   - Inspect for Lisfranc-level injury: widening of the
     tarsometatarsal joint spaces, misalignment of the
     "arc of Boot," or avulsion fragments.

6. Fifth metatarsal base (if visible in this lateral projection):
   - Question: Is there an avulsion fracture (Jones fracture or
     pseudo-Jones)? Limited on lateral view but worth inspecting
     the visible portion.

7. Soft tissue envelope around the calcaneus and heel pad:
   - Inspect for thickening or abnormal lucency that could
     suggest fat pad contusion or rupture.

8. Talar neck region:
   - Inspect for subtle fracture lines. Talar neck fractures can
     be subtle and are associated with significant morbidity.

9. Distal tibiofibular syndesmosis region:
   - Inspect for any diastasis or bony avulsion at the syndesmal
     attachment (though best seen on mortise view).

10. Phalangeal regions (distal foot):
    - Inspect visible phalanges for any cortical irregularity
      that might indicate old or acute fracture.
