AGENT_06 — IMAGE PROCESSING PROPOSALS
======================================

After independent review of the lateral foot X-ray, I am proposing the
following specific processing analyses. These are not a standard checklist;
each targets a concrete question I have about this image.

========================================================================
PROPOSAL 1: Directional Bandpass Filtering on the Distal Tibial Metaphysis
========================================================================

(a) QUESTION:
    Is the faint linear density ~5–7 cm proximal to the tibial plafond a
    real structural finding (stress fracture line) or an artifact of
    superimposed fibula cortex and beam-hardening?

(b) WHY THIS METHOD:
    A suspected stress fracture in this region would run roughly
    transverse (perpendicular to the long axis of the tibia). A
    directional bandpass filter tuned to horizontal/transverse frequencies
    at the scale of a hairline fracture (~1–3 pixels wide) will amplify
    any true transverse linear signal while suppressing the longitudinal
    trabecular pattern and fibular overlap that runs more vertically.
    Using a Gabor filter bank oriented at 90° ± 20° would isolate this
    frequency band.

(c) EXPECTED FINDING:
    - If the line persists and amplifies after directional filtering, it
      is likely a real transverse structural discontinuity (stress fracture
      or cortical irregularity).
    - If it disappears or does not amplify relative to surrounding
      trabeculae, it is likely superimposition artifact.

(d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS:
    - Over-sharpening will create false linear features at the edges of
      the tibial cortex where the X-ray beam traverses perpendicular
      boundaries. I need to mask the analysis to the bone interior, not
      the cortex.
    - Gabor filters at small scales introduce ringing at high-contrast
      boundaries.

(e) COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL:
    Overlay the filtered output (as a heat map) on the original image
    at the ROI. A real fracture line will show consistent enhancement
    across the cortical width, not just at the cortex boundary. Also
    compare with a filter bank rotated to 0° (longitudinal) — if the
    line appears stronger at 90° than at 0°, the transverse orientation
    supports a fracture interpretation.

PROPOSAL 2: Texture Quantification of Calcaneal Trabecular Pattern
========================================================================

(a) QUESTION:
    The posterior body of the calcaneus shows heterogeneous trabecular
    density. Is this pattern within the range of normal variation, or
    does it suggest focal pathology (impaction, sclerosis, cyst, or early
    osteonecrosis)?

(b) WHY THIS METHOD:
    Quantitative texture analysis — specifically Gray Level Co-occurrence
    Matrix (GLCM) features such as contrast, homogeneity, and entropy —
    will characterize the trabecular pattern in the suspicious region
    and compare it objectively to a reference region (anterior calcaneal
    body or the talar body, which appears more uniform). This moves
    beyond subjective "heterogeneous vs. normal" to measurable texture
    metrics. If available, local Fourier spectral analysis can also
    quantify the trabecular spacing and orientation.

(c) EXPECTED FINDING:
    - If the posterior calcaneal region shows significantly elevated
      contrast and reduced homogeneity compared to the reference, it
      suggests focal trabecular disruption (impaction, microfracture, or
      degenerative sclerosis with reactive changes).
    - If the metrics are comparable to the reference region, the
      perceived heterogeneity is likely normal anatomic variation.

(d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS:
    - Beam-hardening and partial-volume effects near the dense cortical
      boundary will artificially increase GLCM contrast. I need to
      exclude a 3–5 pixel margin around the cortex.
    - The posterior calcaneus receives a different effective X-ray path
      length than the anterior body, so intensity normalization is
      required before computing texture features.

(e) COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL:
    Map the GLCM contrast and entropy values as a heat map over the
    calcaneal body on the original image. Regions that light up
    consistently (not at the cortex, not at the joint interface) are
    the ones worth investigating. Compare the heat map visually with the
    unprocessed image to confirm correspondence.

PROPOSAL 3: Hough Line Transform on the Subtalar Joint Margins
========================================================================

(a) QUESTION:
    The posterior facet of the subtalar joint shows apparent marginal
    irregularity. Is this true degenerative change (osteophytes,
    subchondral sclerosis with margin remodeling) or normal anatomic
    irregularity of the posterior facet?

(b) WHY THIS METHOD:
    The posterior facet of the subtalar joint should present as a
    relatively smooth, gently curving surface. A probabilistic Hough
    transform (or RANSAC line fitting) applied to the enhanced edge map
    of the joint surface will quantify deviations from a smooth curve.
    By fitting a low-order polynomial or spline to the detected edge
    points and measuring residuals, I can distinguish small-scale
    irregularities (normal trabecular edge roughness, expected < 2 px)
    from larger deviations (> 3–4 px, suggestive of osteophyte
    formation or margin remodeling).

(c) EXPECTED FINDING:
    - Small, uniform residuals across the joint surface: normal
      anatomic irregularity.
    - Localized clusters of large residuals (3+ px deviation): focal
      osteophyte formation or margin remodeling consistent with early
      osteoarthritic change.

(d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS:
    - Edge detection alone will pick up trabecular structures projecting
      through the joint region from overlapping bones. I must restrict
      the edge detection to a narrow band along the expected joint
      surface (using anatomical priors to seed the search region).
    - Noise amplification during pre-processing will create spurious
      edge points. Median filtering before edge detection is essential.

(e) COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL:
    Superimpose the fitted curve and outlier points on the original
    image. Outliers should correspond to visible bony prominences or
    irregularities. If the outliers appear where nothing is visible
    on the original, they are artifacts and should be dismissed.

PROPOSAL 4: Longitudinal Arch Angle Quantification via Contour Analysis
========================================================================

(a) QUESTION:
    What are the precise calcaneal inclination angle and
    talocalcaneal angle? Are they consistent with a normal arch, or do
    they indicate pes planus or pes cavus? This is important because
    arch abnormality could explain the subtle entheseal and
    degenerative changes I noted.

(b) WHY THIS METHOD:
    Canny edge detection followed by contour extraction of the plantar
    calcaneal cortex and the talocalcaneal joint line, then fitting
    straight lines to each segment. The calcaneal inclination angle is
    measured between the plantar calcaneal cortex and the horizontal
    plane. The talocalcaneal angle is measured between the plantar
    calcaneal cortex and the long axis of the talus. Contour-based
    measurement is more precise than manual line drawing because it
    uses the actual detected edge rather than an operator-drawn proxy.

(c) EXPECTED FINDING:
    - Calcaneal inclination angle: normal range ~18–24°.
    - Talocalcaneal angle: normal range ~25–45° (lateral view).
    - If either angle is outside normal range, this provides mechanical
      context for any entheseal or degenerative findings (e.g., low
      inclination angle → increased load on the subtalar joint and
      plantar fascia insertion).

(d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS:
    - The plantar calcaneal cortex may be partially obscured by the
      calcaneal spur or soft tissue silhouette. If edge detection
    fails at the expected insertion point of the plantar fascia, I
      should fall back to manual landmarking.
    - The horizontal reference plane requires knowing the weight-bearing
      status of the image. If this is not a true weight-bearing
      lateral, the angles may not be clinically meaningful.

(e) COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL:
    Draw the measured angles and reference lines directly on the
    original image for visual verification. The lines should align
    with the cortical margins as I perceive them on the unprocessed
    image. If the automated lines diverge from my visual assessment,
    the edge detection likely picked up the wrong surface.

PROPOSAL 5: Soft Tissue Silhouette Extraction — Posterior Heel Pad
========================================================================

(a) QUESTION:
    Is the posterior calcaneal fat pad thickness normal? Thinning of
    the heel fat pad is associated with chronic heel pain and alters
    load distribution, which could contribute to the calcaneal findings
    (enthesopathy, possible subtalar degenerative change).

(b) WHY THIS METHOD:
    The soft tissue silhouette at the posterior heel has a smooth,
    convex contour with a characteristic thickness. By thresholding at
    a low intensity level to segment the soft tissue envelope from the
    background, then computing the perpendicular distance between the
    posterior soft tissue contour and the posterior calcaneal cortex,
    I can quantify heel pad thickness at multiple points along the
    posterior calcaneal surface. A minimum thickness of ~15 mm is
    considered normal.

(c) EXPECTED FINDING:
    - Heel pad thickness ≥ 15 mm: normal.
    - Heel pad thickness < 10 mm: significant thinning, consistent
      with chronic heel pain syndrome.
    - Localized thinning at the superior aspect of the heel pad:
      suggestive of targeted compressive stress.

(d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS:
    - Scanning artifacts (the vertical banding in the dark regions)
      may interfere with the threshold at the soft tissue periphery.
      Morphological opening or a morphological gradient with closing
      will smooth the silhouette before measurement.
    - The X-ray beam geometry (source-to-image distance) affects the
      apparent thickness. I cannot correct for this without knowing
      the acquisition parameters, so the measurement is relative, not
      absolute.

(e) COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL:
    Draw the segmented soft tissue contour on the original image. The
    contour should follow the soft tissue envelope as I see it.
    Deviations will reveal where the thresholding failed. Compare the
    measured thickness at the inferior pole of the calcaneus with
    clinical norms and note whether it appears thin or normal by eye
    on the original.

PROPOSAL 6: Crop-and-Enhance of the Plantar Calcaneal Inference Zone
========================================================================

(a) QUESTION:
    Is there a true calcaneal spur (enthesophyte) at the plantar fascia
    insertion? If so, what is its approximate size and morphology
    (sharp/spiculated vs. blunt/rounded)?

(b) WHY THIS METHOD:
    A tight crop around the plantar calcaneal tubercle with aggressive
    contrast stretching (piecewise linear histogram equalization
    targeting the bone-soft-tissue transition zone) will maximize the
    visibility of the bony prominence against the adjacent soft tissue.
    Unlike global CLAHE, which can over-amplify noise in the soft
    tissue, piecewise linear stretching targets only the intensity
    range where the spur-cortex and soft tissue boundaries fall. After
    enhancement, adaptive thresholding followed by contour tracing will
    isolate the bony prominence from the cortical surface.

(c) EXPECTED FINDING:
    - A clearly delineated bony projection from the plantar cortex:
      confirms enthesophyte. Size estimate from pixel dimensions
      (assuming approximate pixel spacing from known calcaneal
      dimensions).
    - No discrete projection, only cortical contour: no spur, the
      prominence I saw on the original is cortical anatomic variation.
    - Irregular, poorly defined density blending with soft tissue:
      ambiguous — could be early enthesopathy without frank ossification.

(d) POSSIBLE ARTIFACTS:
    - Aggressive contrast stretching will amplify scanner noise and
      digitization banding in the soft tissue region, potentially
      creating false spicules. I should apply mild Gaussian smoothing
      before thresholding.
    - The plantar fat pad has variable density on X-ray. Dense fat
      could mimic a bony spur if thresholded incorrectly.

(e) COMPARISON WITH ORIGINAL:
    Show a side-by-side: original crop vs. enhanced crop with contour
    overlay. The contour should trace what I can already vaguely
    perceive on the original. If the enhanced image reveals structures
    I cannot correlate with the original at all, they are likely
    artifacts.

NOTES ON EXECUTION ORDER
========================================================================

If computational resources are limited, I recommend the following
priority:

  1. Proposal 1 (Directional bandpass on distal tibia) — highest
     clinical stakes: distinguishing real fracture from artifact.
  2. Proposal 6 (Plantar calcaneal spur crop-and-enhance) — directly
     addresses the most accessible finding.
  3. Proposal 4 (Arch angle quantification) — provides mechanical
     context for all other findings.
  4. Proposal 2 (Calcaneal texture analysis) — most computationally
     intensive, but most rigorous for the trabecular heterogeneity
     question.
  5. Proposal 5 (Heel pad thickness) — quick to compute, moderate
     clinical value.
  6. Proposal 3 (Subtalar joint Hough analysis) — most fragile to
     artifacts due to overlapping anatomy.
